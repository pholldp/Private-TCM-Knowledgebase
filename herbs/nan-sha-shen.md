# Nan Sha Shen (南沙参)
**English Name**: Ladybell root  
**Category**: Yin-tonifying herbs (补阴药)  
**Scientific Name**: Adenophora tetraphylla (Thunb.) Fisch.  
**Pharmaceutical Name**: Radix Adenophorae  
**Toxicity**: Non-toxic  
**Temperature**: Slightly Cool | **Taste**: Sweet (甘 gān), Bitter (苦 kǔ)  
**Channels Entered**: Lungs, Stomach  
**Parts Used**: Root (根 gēn)  

---

## Summary
Nan Sha Shen is a gentle, cooling root used to moisten and soothe the lungs and stomach. It is commonly used for dry coughs, scratchy or dry throat, poor appetite, and thirst, particularly after fevers or during dry autumn weather. Compared to its close relative Bei Sha Shen (northern variety), it has the added ability to help dissolve phlegm and mildly tonify Qi.

---

## What This Herb Does
- **Therapeutic Focus**: Respiratory Health, Digestive Health, Throat and Voice Care, Post-Illness Recovery
- **TCM Actions**: Clears Lung Heat and Generates Fluids, Benefits the Stomach and Generates Fluids, Resolves Phlegm, Tonifies Qi

### How these actions work
'Nourishes Lung Yin and clears Lung Heat' means Nan Sha Shen replenishes the moisture and cooling fluids of the lungs when they have become dried out by heat or dryness. This is why it is used for dry coughs with little or sticky phlegm, sore and dry throat, and hoarseness. Its sweet flavour nourishes, while its slightly cool nature clears the mild heat that often accompanies Yin depletion in the lungs.
'Benefits the Stomach and generates fluids' means it helps restore the digestive juices and moisture of the stomach. People with Stomach Yin Deficiency may feel thirsty, have a dry mouth, lose their appetite, or experience dry retching. Nan Sha Shen gently replenishes these depleted fluids without being overly heavy or cloying.
'Resolves phlegm' refers to its ability to thin and help expel sticky, difficult-to-cough-up phlegm. This is a distinctive advantage over Bei Sha Shen (the northern variety), which lacks this phlegm-resolving action. It is particularly useful for chronic coughs where dryness and sticky phlegm coexist.
'Tonifies Qi' means it provides a mild boost to the body's vital force, particularly in the Spleen and Lung. This makes Nan Sha Shen especially suitable for people who are both Qi-deficient and Yin-deficient at the same time, such as during recovery from a prolonged fever when the body's reserves of both fluids and functional strength are depleted.

## Patterns Addressed
### Lung Yin Deficiency
- **Explanation**: Nan Sha Shen directly nourishes Lung Yin with its sweet, slightly cool nature and its affinity for the Lung channel. When Lung Yin is depleted (often by prolonged heat, dryness, or chronic illness), the lungs lose their moistening capacity, leading to dry coughs and irritation. Nan Sha Shen replenishes this moisture while gently clearing residual heat. Its additional ability to resolve phlegm makes it especially appropriate when the dryness produces sticky, difficult-to-expectorate phlegm alongside the Yin deficiency.
- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**:
  - **Hoarseness**: Hoarse voice from dryness
  - **Thirst**: Thirst with dry mouth

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
- **Explanation**: Nan Sha Shen enters the Stomach channel and uses its sweet, moistening nature to replenish depleted Stomach Yin. Stomach Yin Deficiency arises when heat or prolonged illness consumes the stomach's fluids, impairing digestion and causing dryness. Nan Sha Shen restores these fluids and gently clears the residual heat. Its mild Qi-tonifying action also supports the Spleen, making it particularly suitable when Stomach Yin depletion is accompanied by weakened digestive function.
- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**:
  - **Poor Appetite**: Reduced appetite or hunger without desire to eat

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
- **Explanation**: Nan Sha Shen is unique among Yin-nourishing herbs because it also mildly tonifies Qi, particularly Spleen and Lung Qi. This makes it ideal for the late stages of febrile illnesses or after prolonged illness when both Qi and Yin are depleted simultaneously. Its slightly cool nature means it can nourish without adding warmth, which would be inappropriate when residual heat lingers alongside the deficiency.
- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**:
  - **Thirst**: Irritability with thirst and dry mouth
  - **Poor Appetite**: Poor appetite with digestive weakness

## Commonly Used For
### Chronic Bronchitis
- **Arises from**: Lung Yin Deficiency
- **TCM Interpretation**: In TCM, chronic bronchitis that features a dry, persistent cough with scanty or sticky phlegm is most often understood as Lung Yin Deficiency with Dryness-Heat. The Lungs depend on adequate Yin (cooling, moistening fluids) to function smoothly. When this Yin is depleted by repeated infections, dry environments, or prolonged heat, the Lungs lose their ability to descend Qi smoothly and moisten the airways, leading to irritation and coughing. The phlegm becomes thick and difficult to expectorate because there is not enough fluid to thin it.
- **Why it Helps**: Nan Sha Shen's sweet, slightly cool nature directly replenishes Lung Yin while gently clearing the mild heat that accompanies Yin depletion. Unlike many Yin-nourishing herbs, it also resolves phlegm, making it especially well-suited for chronic bronchitis where sticky phlegm and dryness coexist. Its mild Qi-tonifying action supports the Lung's descending function, helping to calm the cough reflex. It is commonly combined with Mai Dong (Ophiopogon) and Xing Ren (Apricot seed) to strengthen these effects.

### Chronic Gastritis
- **Arises from**: Stomach Yin Deficiency
- **TCM Interpretation**: TCM views chronic gastritis (especially the atrophic type) through the lens of Stomach Yin Deficiency. The Stomach is described as preferring moisture and disliking dryness. When Stomach Yin is depleted by irregular eating, emotional stress, spicy food, or prolonged illness, the stomach lining loses its protective moisture. This manifests as a burning sensation in the stomach area, hunger without desire to eat, dry mouth, and a red tongue with little coating. The lack of fluids also impairs the downward movement of Stomach Qi, causing nausea or dry retching.
- **Why it Helps**: Nan Sha Shen enters the Stomach channel and nourishes its depleted Yin, restoring the protective moisture the stomach needs. Its slightly cool nature clears the mild heat that builds up when Yin is insufficient. Crucially, Nan Sha Shen also mildly tonifies Spleen Qi, which distinguishes it from Bei Sha Shen. This means it can address situations where both digestive weakness and fluid depletion exist together, as is common in chronic atrophic gastritis. It features in the classical formula Yi Wei Tang (Benefit the Stomach Decoction) alongside Mai Dong, Sheng Di, and Yu Zhu for precisely this purpose.

## Dosage & Preparation
- **Standard Dosage**: 9-15g
- **Maximum Dosage**: Up to 30g when using fresh root (鲜品). For dried herb, up to 20g may be used for severe Yin deficiency with pronounced dryness, under practitioner supervision.
- **Dosage Notes**: Use the standard dose of 9-15g for most Yin-nourishing and Lung-moistening purposes. For pronounced dry cough with sticky phlegm, doses toward the upper range (12-15g) are preferred. When using fresh root, the dose can be increased to 15-30g as the fresh herb has higher water content. Nan Sha Shen has a milder action than Bei Sha Shen for moistening dryness and clearing Lung Heat, so slightly higher doses or combination with other Yin-nourishing herbs may be appropriate for severe dryness.
- **Preparation**: No special decoction handling required. Nan Sha Shen is simply decocted with other herbs in the normal manner.

## Processing Methods
### M&#xEC; Zh&#xEC; N&#xE1;n Sh&#x101; Sh&#x113;n (&#x871C;&#x7099;&#x5357;&#x6C99;&#x53C2;)
- **Processing Method**: The dried root is stir-fried with refined honey until the surface becomes slightly yellow and is no longer sticky to the touch.
- **How it Changes Properties**: Honey-processing enhances the herb's moistening and Qi-tonifying actions, making it slightly warmer and sweeter. The Lung-nourishing and cough-relieving properties are strengthened, while the cooling and heat-clearing properties become somewhat reduced.
- **When to Use**: Preferred for chronic dry coughs with significant Qi deficiency, when the patient needs stronger tonification and moistening rather than heat-clearing. Choose this form when the raw herb's cooling nature is not needed or when the cough is predominantly from weakness rather than heat.

## Common Herb Pairs
### Mai Dong (Ratio: 1:1 (e.g. 10g each) to 1:1.5 (Nan Sha Shen 9g : Mai Dong 15g))
- **Description**: Nan Sha Shen and Mai Dong together powerfully nourish Lung and Stomach Yin while generating fluids. Nan Sha Shen contributes mild Qi-tonifying and phlegm-resolving actions, while Mai Dong adds stronger moistening depth and also enters the Heart channel to calm restlessness. Together they cover the full scope of Lung-Stomach Yin nourishment.
- **When to Use**: Dry cough with scanty phlegm, dry mouth and throat, post-febrile fluid depletion, chronic pharyngitis with hoarseness. This is the core pairing in Sha Shen Mai Dong Tang and Yi Wei Tang.

### Yu zhu (Ratio: 1:1 (e.g. 10g each))
- **Description**: Nan Sha Shen and Yu Zhu (Solomon's seal rhizome) together nourish Yin and generate fluids with a gentle, non-cloying quality. Yu Zhu adds its ability to moisten dryness throughout the body including the skin and muscles, while Nan Sha Shen focuses its action on the Lung and Stomach. Together they provide broad Yin nourishment without heaviness.
- **When to Use**: Autumn dryness affecting the lungs and stomach, dry skin and throat, thirst, reduced appetite. A staple combination in dietary therapy soups throughout southern China.

### Chuan Bei Mu (Ratio: 2:1 (e.g. Nan Sha Shen 10g : Chuan Bei Mu 5g))
- **Description**: Nan Sha Shen nourishes Lung Yin and resolves sticky phlegm from dryness, while Chuan Bei Mu (Fritillaria) clears Lung Heat and powerfully dissolves phlegm. Together they address the full spectrum of dry, heat-type cough with difficult-to-expectorate phlegm.
- **When to Use**: Chronic dry cough with sticky or blood-streaked phlegm, Lung Yin depletion with phlegm-heat. Commonly used in the recovery phase of respiratory infections.

### Tai Zi Shen (Ratio: 1:1 (e.g. 10g each))
- **Description**: Nan Sha Shen nourishes Yin and clears mild heat, while Tai Zi Shen (Pseudostellaria root) gently tonifies both Qi and Yin without being overly warm. Together they address simultaneous Qi and Yin Deficiency with a mild, balanced approach suitable for weakened constitutions.
- **When to Use**: Recovery from febrile illness with both Qi and Yin depletion, fatigue with dry mouth and poor appetite, paediatric Yin-deficient cough with underlying weakness.

## Safety & Warnings
### Toxicity Classification
Non-toxic
Nan Sha Shen is classified as non-toxic in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia and has a long history of safe use, including as a food ingredient in soups and porridges. No toxic components of clinical concern have been identified at standard dosages. The root contains saponins, polysaccharides, beta-sitosterol, and triterpenoids, none of which pose toxicity risks at therapeutic doses.

### Contraindications
- **[Caution]** Spleen deficiency with cold and dampness. Its cool, moistening nature can further impair an already weak and cold digestive system, potentially worsening loose stools, poor appetite, and abdominal bloating.
- **[Caution]** Excessive phlegm-dampness without Heat. Although Nan Sha Shen can resolve some phlegm, it is a moistening herb. In patterns of profuse watery or white phlegm from Spleen deficiency, its moistening quality may aggravate phlegm accumulation.

### Classical Incompatibilities
Nan Sha Shen is incompatible with Li Lu (藜芦, Veratrum) according to the Eighteen Incompatibilities (十八反). The Ben Cao Jing Ji Zhu also states it "clashes with Fang Ji" (恶防己).

### Special Populations
- **Pregnancy**: Generally considered safe at standard doses during pregnancy. Nan Sha Shen is a mild, nourishing Yin-tonifying herb with no known uterine-stimulating or teratogenic properties. It has a long history of use in food-based preparations. However, its cool nature means it should be used cautiously in pregnant women with Spleen deficiency and cold digestion. As always, use during pregnancy should be under practitioner guidance.
- **Breastfeeding**: Nan Sha Shen is considered safe during breastfeeding and has traditionally been used in postpartum dietary therapy. A classical folk remedy from the Hunan Yao Wu Zhi involves cooking the root with pork to promote lactation. No adverse effects on breast milk or nursing infants have been reported. Its gentle, nourishing nature makes it suitable for replenishing fluids in breastfeeding mothers, particularly those experiencing dryness or Yin deficiency.
- **Children**: Nan Sha Shen is traditionally considered suitable for children and has been used in pediatric herbal cough remedies. Dosage should be reduced proportionally based on the child's age and body weight, typically one-third to one-half the adult dose for children over 3 years. Its mild, sweet taste and gentle action make it well-tolerated. Particularly appropriate for children with dry coughs from Lung Yin deficiency following febrile illness. Not recommended for infants under 1 year without practitioner guidance.

### Drug Interactions
No well-documented interactions with specific pharmaceutical drugs have been established in clinical studies. Based on pharmacological properties:
- Antidiabetic medications: Preliminary in vitro research suggests Nan Sha Shen polysaccharides may affect glucose metabolism. People taking insulin or oral hypoglycaemics should monitor blood sugar if using this herb regularly.
- Immunosuppressants: Animal studies suggest Nan Sha Shen may modulate immune function (enhancing cellular immunity). Theoretically, this could interact with immunosuppressive therapy, though clinical significance is not established.
Overall, Nan Sha Shen has a strong safety profile and no high-risk drug interactions have been documented. Patients on multiple medications should consult their healthcare provider.

### Dietary Advice
When taking Nan Sha Shen for Yin deficiency and dryness conditions, avoid spicy, deep-fried, and heavily roasting/grilling foods that can further damage Yin and body fluids. Complementary foods include pears, lily bulb (bai he), lotus seeds, and mildly sweet moistening soups. Nan Sha Shen is commonly used as a food ingredient itself in Chinese dietary therapy, often simmered with lean pork, chicken, or pork lung in soups and congee for gentle nourishment. Avoid excessively cold or raw foods if there is underlying Spleen weakness.

## Botanical & Sourcing
### Botanical Description
Nan Sha Shen comes from two main species in the Campanulaceae (bellflower) family. The primary source, Adenophora tetraphylla (Thunb.) Fisch. (轮叶沙参, Four-leaf Ladybell), is a perennial herb growing 30 to 150 cm tall with a single unbranched stem. The leaves are arranged in distinctive whorls of 3 to 6, oval to lance-shaped with serrated edges and sparse soft hairs. It produces narrow panicle-like inflorescences with blue to blue-purple bell-shaped flowers (7 to 11 mm long) that bloom from July to September. The root is stout, carrot-shaped, yellowish-brown, rough-textured with horizontal wrinkles and a crown at the top. Both the root and stem contain white milky sap.
The second official species, Adenophora stricta Miq. (沙参), is somewhat shorter at 40 to 80 cm, with alternate (not whorled) leaves and broader, purple or blue bell-shaped flowers. It flowers from August to October. Both species grow on mountain slopes, grasslands, forest edges, and among shrubs, and can be found at elevations up to 2,000 metres in southern regions.

### Sourcing & Harvesting
- **Harvesting Season**: Autumn (typically November to December). The roots are dug up, stripped of fibrous rootlets, scraped of rough outer bark while still fresh, then dried in sunlight or by gentle heat.
- **Primary Growing Regions**: The best quality Nan Sha Shen traditionally comes from Anhui, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces (considered the daodi or 'terroir' regions). Guizhou province produces the largest quantity. The herb is widely distributed across China: Adenophora tetraphylla grows in the northeast, Inner Mongolia, Hebei, Shanxi, Shandong, eastern China, Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan, and Guizhou. Adenophora stricta is found mainly in Jiangsu, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, and Hunan. Both species also occur in Korea, Japan, and parts of the Russian Far East.
- **Quality Indicators**: Good quality Nan Sha Shen roots are thick, plump, and evenly shaped, with a pale yellowish-white colour and no remaining rough outer bark. The texture should be light and spongy (described classically as 'hollow and puffy like a lung'), easy to break, with a yellowish-white cross-section showing many fissures but no woody core. The taste should be mildly sweet with a slight bitterness. The herb should have minimal odour. Avoid roots that are dark, shrivelled, overly thin, insect-damaged, or excessively fibrous. Roots harvested in autumn are preferred as they are whiter and more substantial than spring-harvested roots.

### Identity & Adulterants
Nan Sha Shen has a complex adulteration problem due to the large number of Adenophora species (over 40 in China). The most common adulterants are Adenophora hunanensis (杏叶沙参) and Glehnia littoralis (Bei Sha Shen/北沙参). While A. hunanensis is sometimes used interchangeably, it is not an officially recognized source in the Pharmacopoeia. Bei Sha Shen (from the completely different Umbelliferae family) is easily distinguished: it is thin, firm, dense, and heavy, while Nan Sha Shen is thick, spongy, light, and easily broken. The authentic identification of dried Adenophora roots by appearance alone is difficult, which has prompted the development of DNA-based authentication methods. Historically, dishonest merchants would steam and compress Sha Shen roots to mimic the appearance of the more expensive Ren Shen (ginseng), though this could be detected by the lighter weight and blander taste.

### Historical Context
Sha Shen (沙参) was first recorded in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing, where it was listed as a superior-grade (上品) herb. Notably, in ancient texts there was no distinction between "Southern" and "Northern" Sha Shen. All historical references before the Qing dynasty referred to the Campanulaceae (bellflower family) root that we now call Nan Sha Shen. The name "Sha Shen" (沙参, literally "sand root") derives from the plant's preference for growing in sandy soil, as Li Shizhen explained in the Ben Cao Gang Mu.
The formal separation into Nan (Southern) and Bei (Northern) Sha Shen was first made by the Qing dynasty physician Zhang Lu (张璐) in his Ben Jing Feng Yuan (《本经逢原》), where he observed that the northern variety was firm and strongly cold while the southern variety was lighter and milder in action. The Qing physician Zhang Bingcheng later summarized their clinical distinction concisely: "In nourishing and clearing, Bei Sha Shen is inferior to Nan; in moistening and descending, Nan is not as good as Bei." Despite centuries of use, the two herbs were only formally separated in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia as distinct entries in 1963, when Bei Sha Shen was officially classified under the Umbelliferae family plant Glehnia littoralis.

### Modern Scientific Research
1. **[Optimization of Extraction Conditions for Water-Soluble Polysaccharides from Adenophora tetraphylla and Effects on Glucose Consumption (In vitro study, 2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29133049)**
   *Journal: Wu J, Zhou X, Sun H, Yu D. Molecules. 2024;29(13):3049.*
   This study optimized the extraction of polysaccharides from Nan Sha Shen roots and tested their effect on glucose metabolism in liver cells. The extracted polysaccharides significantly promoted glucose consumption in HepG2 cells, suggesting potential antidiabetic activity worth further investigation.

2. **[Exploring the Structural Characteristics and Antioxidant Capacity of Pectins from Adenophora tetraphylla (In vitro study, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules30061301)**
   *Journal: Yan S, Zhang S, Liu Y, Zang H, Zhang L, Liu D. Molecules. 2025;30(6):1301.*
   Researchers isolated and characterized pectic polysaccharides from Nan Sha Shen roots, identifying two fractions with distinct structural domains. Both fractions demonstrated antioxidant activity, supporting the traditional use of this herb for conditions involving tissue damage from oxidative stress.

3. **[Seven New Triterpenoids from the Roots of Adenophora tetraphylla with Cytotoxic Activity (In vitro study, 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2024.105931)**
   *Journal: Yang BY et al. Fitoterapia. 2024;175:105931.*
   Seven previously unknown triterpenoid compounds were isolated from Nan Sha Shen root extract. Several of these compounds showed significant inhibitory effects against a human glioma cell line (LN229), highlighting the structural diversity and potential anticancer properties of the herb's chemical constituents.

4. **[Genetic Distinction of Radix Adenophorae from Its Adulterants by DNA Sequence of 5S-rRNA Spacer Domains (Identification study, 2003)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14992544/)**
   *Journal: Cai ZH et al. American Journal of Chinese Medicine. 2003;31(6):919-926.*
   Researchers at Hong Kong University of Science and Technology developed a molecular genetic method using 5S-rRNA spacer domain sequences to reliably distinguish authentic Nan Sha Shen (A. stricta and A. tetraphylla) from its twelve known adulterant species, addressing a significant quality control challenge.
