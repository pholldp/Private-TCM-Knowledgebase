## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
Gastroparesis is a condition where the stomach takes too long to empty its contents into the small intestine, without any physical blockage. Common symptoms include nausea, vomiting, early satiety (feeling full after a few bites), bloating, and upper abdominal discomfort. It is most often diagnosed through a gastric emptying study, which tracks how quickly food leaves the stomach.
Diabetes is a leading cause, because high blood sugar can damage the vagus nerve that controls stomach contractions. It can also occur after stomach surgery, as a side effect of certain medications, or without a known cause (idiopathic). The condition can significantly impact nutrition and quality of life.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Conventional management starts with dietary changes - eating small, low-fat, low-fiber meals and sometimes liquid nutrition to ease the stomach’s workload. Prokinetic medications like metoclopramide and domperidone are used to stimulate stomach contractions, though metoclopramide carries a risk of serious neurological side effects with long-term use. Antiemetics help control nausea, and in severe cases, gastric electrical stimulation or feeding tubes may be considered.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
In TCM, the Stomach is meant to send food downward - this is its natural direction. When that downward movement fails, food lingers, causing the bloating, nausea, and early fullness that define gastroparesis. The Spleen is the other half of the digestive partnership: it transforms food into Qi and sends the clear, useful part upward. If the Spleen is weak, the whole digestive engine sputters, and the Stomach loses the strength to push food along.
The Liver plays a crucial but often overlooked role. Its job is to keep Qi flowing smoothly throughout the body. When stress, frustration, or anger cause the Liver Qi to stagnate, that stuck energy often attacks the Stomach horizontally, jamming its downward motion. This is why emotional upset so often triggers or worsens gastroparesis symptoms.
Over time, if the Spleen stays weak, it can’t manage fluids properly. Dampness accumulates and eventually thickens into phlegm, which settles in the stomach like sludge and further obstructs movement. In long-standing cases, Qi deficiency can even lead to blood stasis - a lack of vital push that slows circulation in the stomach wall. This is why one Western diagnosis can unfold into several TCM patterns, each demanding a different treatment strategy.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner begins by listening carefully to what you feel - the type of bloating, the timing of nausea, and what makes it better or worse. The quality of these sensations is the first clue that points toward one pattern rather than another. A look at the tongue and a reading of the pulse then confirm which organ systems are out of balance.
In Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency, the dominant feeling is a dull, dragging bloating after eating, along with early satiety and deep fatigue. There is little acid reflux or vomiting; instead the body simply lacks the energy to digest. The tongue is pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels weak or thready.
When Rebellious Stomach Qi is the main pattern, the symptoms move upward forcefully. Frequent belching, acid regurgitation, and vomiting soon after meals are the hallmarks. The tongue may appear red with a thin yellow coating, and the pulse is typically wiry, reflecting the stomach’s failure to guide food downward.
Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach is strongly tied to emotions. Stress, frustration, or anxiety triggers epigastric distension, pain that moves around, and reflux. The tongue edges may be redder than the center, with a thin white or yellow coating, and the pulse is wiry. The practitioner will ask about mood and tension.
Spleen Deficiency with Dampness adds a heavy, waterlogged sensation to the usual bloating. Loose stools, a foggy head, and a thick, greasy tongue coating are common. The pulse is often soft or slippery, and the person feels sluggish rather than simply tired. This pattern signals that fluids are not being processed properly.
Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner is a more stubborn accumulation. Persistent nausea, a sensation of a lump in the stomach, and a very greasy tongue coating dominate. The pulse is slippery. This pattern often develops after long-standing dampness and does not improve with simple rest or light eating.
Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation appears when the condition has lasted a long time, especially in diabetic gastroparesis. The key sign is a fixed, stabbing pain in the epigastrium that may worsen at night. The tongue looks dark or purplish, sometimes with stasis spots, and the pulse is choppy or thready.

## กลุ่มอาการการแพทย์แผนจีนและการรักษา (TCM Patterns and Treatment)

### Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-stomach-qi-deficiency)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Poor appetite and early satiety, Upper abdominal bloating after eating, Loose stools or diarrhea, Fatigue and physical weakness, Sallow or pale complexion
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overeating or large, heavy meals, Cold and raw foods, Overwork or physical overexertion, Emotional stress or worry, Skipping meals
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm, cooked meals, Small, frequent meals, Rest after eating, Gentle walking after meals, Warmth on the abdomen
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Spleen and Stomach are the engines of digestion, responsible for transforming food into Qi and moving it downward. When their Qi is deficient, this engine sputters - food sits in the stomach instead of being processed, causing bloating, early satiety, and a heavy sensation after meals. Because the body cannot extract enough energy from food, deep fatigue and physical weakness set in, and the Spleen's weakened grip on fluids leads to loose stools.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, and slightly enlarged with tooth marks on the edges; thin white coating. Pulse: Weak, soft, and forceless, especially at the right middle (Guan) position.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: The Spleen's digestive fire is easily smothered by cold, raw, and heavy foods, which demand more Qi than a weakened system can supply. Overwork and worry further deplete Spleen Qi, while large meals overwhelm its already sluggish processing. Warm, easily digested foods and rest conserve Qi, allowing the Spleen to slowly rebuild its transformative capacity. Gentle movement encourages Qi to flow without exhausting it, and warmth on the abdomen directly supports the Spleen's preference for a warm, dry environment.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat small, frequent, warm meals - think congees, soups, and well-cooked grains - and avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Chew thoroughly and rest briefly after eating. A gentle walk after meals helps Qi move without exhausting you. Prioritize regular sleep and avoid overwork, as pushing through fatigue further drains the Qi you are trying to rebuild.

### Rebellious Stomach Qi
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/rebellious-stomach-qi)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Nausea that may lead to vomiting, Frequent belching and hiccups, Acid reflux or sour regurgitation, Sensation of Qi rising from stomach to throat, Feeling of food not going down properly, Upper abdominal bloating after eating
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overeating or large, heavy meals, Cold and raw foods, Lying down after eating, Emotional stress or worry, Greasy, fried, or dairy foods
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Small, frequent meals, Sitting upright after eating, Warm, cooked meals, Ginger tea
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Stomach is meant to send food downward for digestion. When its Qi rebels and flows upward instead, food lingers, causing nausea, belching, and a sensation of fullness. This upward counterflow is the direct mechanism behind the delayed emptying seen in gastroparesis.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal body colour with a white coating (may be yellow if Heat, greasy if Phlegm). Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), possibly slippery (滑 huá) if Phlegm or Food Stagnation.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: The Stomach Qi must descend for proper digestion. Large, fatty meals overload the Stomach and cause stagnation, forcing Qi to rebel upward. Cold and raw foods constrict Qi flow, making it harder to descend. Lying down after eating removes the help of gravity and allows Qi to rise. Stress disrupts the smooth flow of Qi, often triggering rebellion. Small, warm, frequent meals are easier for the Stomach to move downward. Sitting upright and gentle movement after eating encourage the natural descending direction. Ginger warms the Stomach and specifically directs Qi downward to relieve nausea.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and avoid large portions. Choose warm, cooked foods over cold or raw ones. Sit upright for at least an hour after eating to help gravity assist the Stomach’s downward movement. Gentle walking after meals can also encourage Qi to descend.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Distending pain in the upper abdomen that radiates to the ribs, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Irritability, moodiness, or frequent sighing
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Emotional stress or worry, Overeating or large, heavy meals, Cold and raw foods, Irregular eating habits
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Relaxation and stress reduction, Gentle walking after meals, Small, frequent meals, Ginger tea
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger build up, that flow gets stuck - a state called Liver Qi Stagnation. The stuck energy then attacks the Stomach horizontally, disrupting its normal downward movement. This causes Qi to rebel upward and pool in the upper abdomen, producing the bloating, belching, reflux, and distending pain that radiates toward the ribs.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, may have redder sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, most noticeable at the left middle position.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Emotional stress is the core driver of Liver Qi Stagnation; when the Liver seizes up, it immediately attacks the Stomach, so calming the mind directly eases the digestive symptoms. Gentle movement helps keep Qi flowing smoothly, preventing the stagnation that leads to distension and reflux. Overeating or heavy, greasy foods overwhelm the already compromised Stomach, while cold foods constrict Qi flow, making the blockage worse. Warm, easy-to-digest meals and aromatic herbs support the Stomach's natural downward movement and help unstick stuck Liver Qi.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat small, regular meals and avoid eating when you are upset or rushed. Gentle, rhythmic movement like walking, tai chi, or yoga helps circulate Liver Qi and prevent stagnation. Incorporate aromatics like fresh ginger, fennel, and peppermint tea to gently move Qi. Prioritize stress management through deep breathing or journaling, as emotional tension directly fuels this pattern.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Heavy sensation in the upper abdomen, Upper abdominal bloating after eating, Loose stools or diarrhea, Heaviness in the body and limbs, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, Drowsiness, especially after meals, Thirst but no real desire to drink, Head feels heavy or muzzy, as if wrapped
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Cold and raw foods, Greasy, fried, or dairy foods, Damp, humid weather, Overeating or large, heavy meals, Sedentary lifestyle
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm, cooked meals, Dry, warm weather, Gentle walking after meals
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it cannot transform food and fluids efficiently. Unprocessed fluids collect as dampness, which is heavy and sticky, and this dampness further bogs down the digestive machinery. The result is a sluggish stomach that empties slowly, leaving you feeling heavy, bloated, and full even after a small meal.

Favor warm, cooked meals like soups and congees, and include small amounts of ginger or cardamom to help the Stomach process food. Gentle daily movement, especially after meals, helps prevent stagnation and heaviness.

Stress knots the Liver Qi, which further blocks the already sluggish circulation, so emotional calm is essential.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat small, warm, easily digestible meals like congee with a little ginger. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that further weaken Qi and congeal blood. Gentle daily walking helps Qi move without exhausting you. Prioritize rest and avoid overworking.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### How does TCM explain gastroparesis differently from Western medicine?
Western medicine focuses on the stomach as a mechanical pump - if it empties too slowly, the treatment aims to speed it up with drugs or electrical stimulation. TCM looks at the whole digestive ecosystem: the Spleen’s energy to transform food, the Liver’s role in keeping Qi flowing, and whether dampness or phlegm is gumming up the works. The goal isn’t just to force the stomach to move, but to restore the balance that lets it move on its own.

### Can acupuncture really help a stomach that empties too slowly?
Yes. Acupuncture points like Zusanli (ST-36) and Zhongwan (REN-12) have been shown to influence gastric motility through nerve pathways. In TCM terms, these points strengthen the Spleen and Stomach, descend rebellious Qi, and regulate the middle burner. Many patients notice less bloating and nausea within the first few sessions, though lasting change requires consistent treatment over weeks.

### Will I need to take Chinese herbs forever?
No. Herbal formulas are used to correct the underlying imbalance. Once the pattern shifts - for example, Spleen Qi strengthens or Liver Qi flows smoothly - the formula is adjusted or discontinued. Some patients with chronic, deep-seated deficiency may benefit from a maintenance formula or periodic acupuncture, but the aim is always to reduce dependency over time.

### Can TCM help if I have diabetic gastroparesis?
Absolutely. Diabetic gastroparesis often presents as Spleen and Stomach Qi deficiency with dampness or blood stasis. TCM herbs and acupuncture can improve gastric emptying and reduce nausea without the side effects of prokinetic drugs. It’s important to coordinate with your endocrinologist, as improved digestion may affect blood sugar levels and require medication adjustment.

### Is it safe to combine TCM with my prokinetic medication?
In most cases, yes. Common TCM herbs like Bai Zhu and Fu Ling are mild and don’t interact dangerously with metoclopramide or domperidone. However, if your formula includes blood-moving herbs (Dang Gui, Chuan Xiong, Tao Ren) and you’re on anticoagulants, your practitioner needs to know. Always bring a full medication list to your TCM consultation and keep your doctor informed.

### What diet changes should I make while getting TCM treatment?
Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods - congee, soups, steamed vegetables. Small, frequent meals are gentler on a weak Spleen. Avoid cold drinks, raw salads, greasy foods, and dairy, which create dampness and bog down digestion. Ginger tea before meals can help settle the stomach and encourage downward movement.

### How soon will I feel better?
Most people notice some relief - less bloating, reduced nausea - within 2-4 weeks. If your pattern is primarily excess (Liver Qi stagnation, Rebellious Stomach Qi), improvement can be quicker. Deficiency patterns take longer because the body needs time to rebuild Qi reserves. Your practitioner will track your progress through symptom changes and tongue/pulse shifts.

### What if I have severe vomiting or weight loss?
Severe, persistent vomiting that prevents you from keeping down food or liquids, or unintentional weight loss of more than 5% of your body weight in a month, requires urgent medical evaluation. See the Safety section below for other red flags - TCM works best alongside proper medical care, not as a replacement when symptoms are dangerous.
