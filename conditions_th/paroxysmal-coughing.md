## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
In Western medicine, paroxysmal coughing is most famously linked to whooping cough (pertussis), a highly contagious bacterial infection of the respiratory tract. The classic hallmark is a series of rapid, forceful coughs followed by a high-pitched ‘whoop’ as the person gasps for air, often ending in vomiting or exhaustion. Diagnosis is typically confirmed through a nasal swab or blood test, and the illness can last for many weeks even with treatment.
Other causes include viral bronchitis, asthma, or post-infectious cough, but the spasmodic nature of the coughing fits points to significant airway irritation and inflammation.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Standard treatment for pertussis includes antibiotics such as azithromycin, which can reduce contagiousness but often do not shorten the coughing phase once the spasms have started. Supportive care - humidifiers, small frequent meals, and rest - is the mainstay. For non-infectious paroxysmal coughs, cough suppressants or bronchodilators may be prescribed, though their effectiveness is variable.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
TCM sees paroxysmal coughing as a Lung disorder where external evil factors - Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat - invade the body’s surface through the nose and mouth. The Lungs lose their ability to spread and descend Qi, and the Qi rebels upward as a cough. If not expelled quickly, the pathogen deepens, transforming into thick Phlegm-Heat that clogs the airways, triggering the explosive, spasmodic fits the body uses to try to clear the obstruction.
The illness typically moves through three stages. In the early stage, the battle is at the surface: sneezing, chills, or a sore throat mark whether Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat is present. During the spasmodic stage, Phlegm and Heat lodge deep in the Lungs, and the cough becomes unmistakably paroxysmal - sudden, violent, and ending with a whooping gasp. Finally, in the recovery stage, the Lung’s Qi and Yin are depleted, leaving a weak, dry, lingering cough and deep fatigue.
Because the underlying imbalance shifts as the illness progresses, a single Western diagnosis like whooping cough can present as several TCM patterns. The treatment must therefore change with the stage, addressing the pathogen’s depth and the body’s remaining strength. This is why TCM rarely uses one formula from start to finish - the prescription evolves as you heal.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner first asks about the timeline and character of the cough, because paroxysmal coughing (顿咳, dùn ké) typically moves through distinct stages. The earliest signs - whether the nose runs clear or the throat feels fiery - reveal whether Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat has invaded the Lungs. Later, the explosive, spasmodic fits with a whoop point to Phlegm-Heat deep in the Lungs. Finally, a lingering weak cough with exhaustion signals that Qi and Yin have been drained by the illness.
In the early catarrhal stage, the practitioner differentiates Wind-Cold from Wind-Heat. Wind-Cold presents with sneezing, a runny nose with clear mucus, and a cough that sounds high-pitched but not yet convulsive; the tongue coating is thin and white, and the pulse feels floating and tight.
Wind-Heat, by contrast, brings more fever, a sore throat, and yellow-tinged phlegm, with a red tongue tip and a rapid floating pulse. These clues guide whether to warm and disperse or cool and release.
When the illness moves into the spasmodic stage, Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs takes over. The cough becomes unmistakably paroxysmal - sudden violent bouts that end with a crowing inspiratory whoop. Phlegm is thick, yellow, and sticky, and the tongue appears red with a thick yellow greasy coat. The pulse is rapid and slippery. This pattern is the hallmark of the condition and demands clearing heat and transforming phlegm.
In recovery, the cough loses its force but lingers as a dry, weak hack. The person feels exhausted, short of breath, and may sweat easily at night. The tongue looks red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. This is Qi and Yin Deficiency, where the body’s vital resources have been consumed by the prolonged coughing. A gentle, nourishing approach is needed to rebuild strength and moisten the Lungs.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Coughing fits with a whooping inspiratory sound, Thick yellow sticky phlegm that is hard to cough up, Flushed face, Chest tightness and rib-side pain when coughing, Thirst with desire to drink, Restlessness and irritability
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Anger or excitement, Greasy, spicy, or dairy foods, Cold, dry, or dusty air, Overexertion, Cold drinks
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest in a quiet room, Warm, moist air (e.g., humidifier), Sipping warm water or pear juice, Gentle chest percussion
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  During the spasmodic stage of whooping cough, the initial Wind invasion has transformed into Phlegm-Heat that obstructs the Lungs. Thick, sticky phlegm clogs the airways while heat agitates the Lung Qi, forcing violent, explosive coughing fits as the body tries to expel the obstruction. The characteristic whooping sound happens when air is sucked in past the narrowed, phlegm-clogged throat at the end of a fit.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating, possibly swollen at the front with red prickles. Pulse: slippery and rapid (滑数 huá shǔ).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Phlegm-Heat thrives on heat-producing foods and emotional upset, which stir up internal fire. Cooling, moistening foods and calm rest help clear heat and resolve phlegm. Warm, humid air helps loosen sticky phlegm, while dry, cold air tightens the airways.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Avoid dairy, greasy, and spicy foods that create phlegm and heat. Sip warm water throughout the day to thin mucus. Use a humidifier to keep air moist. Gentle chest tapping can help loosen phlegm.

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Cough with thin, white, watery phlegm, Chills more prominent than fever, Clear runny nose and sneezing, Itchy, scratchy throat, No sweating
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Cold or raw foods, Exposure to drafts or cold wind, Overexertion
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm ginger tea, Staying warm and covered, Rest in a draft-free room
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When Wind-Cold enters through the nose and mouth, it attacks the Lung's protective surface. The Cold constricts the Lung's normal dispersing and descending movement, trapping Qi and forcing it to rebel upward as a cough. In the early stage of paroxysmal coughing illnesses like whooping cough, this pattern sets the stage for the violent spasms that may follow, as the pathogen burrows deeper.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (浮 fú) and tight (紧 jǐn).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Wind-Cold invades the body's surface and blocks the Lung's ability to diffuse Qi, causing cough. Warmth helps open the pores and expel the pathogen, while rest conserves Qi to fight the invasion. Cold foods and drafts reinforce the pathogenic Cold, making the cough worse and driving it deeper.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Sip warm ginger tea with a little brown sugar to gently warm the Lungs and dispel Cold. Keep the neck and chest covered and avoid drafts. Eat light, warm, cooked foods like congee, and rest as much as possible to conserve Qi for fighting the pathogen.

### Wind-Heat invading the Lungs
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat-invading-the-lungs)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Fever stronger than the chills, Sore or painful throat, Cough with thick yellow phlegm, Yellow nasal discharge and thirst
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Windy or drafty environments, Overexertion and talking loudly, Alcohol and smoking
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Cool, fresh air, Peppermint or chrysanthemum tea, Rest and adequate sleep, Light, non-greasy meals
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Wind-Heat enters through the nose and mouth, attacking the Lung's protective surface. The Lung loses its ability to properly spread and descend Qi, which then rebels upward as sudden, forceful coughing bouts. The Heat dries body fluids, producing yellow phlegm, a sore throat, and fever - this is often the earliest stage of a paroxysmal cough, before the deeper phlegm-heat obstruction sets in.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red tip with thin yellow coating, body slightly red. Pulse: floating and rapid.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Wind-Heat is an exterior pathogen that traps Heat in the Lungs and surface. Cooling, moistening drinks and fresh air help release the exterior and clear Heat, while spicy, greasy foods and wind exposure push the pathogen deeper and fan the flames. Rest conserves Qi so the body can fight the invasion, and gentle, Lung-soothing herbs calm the upward rebel of Qi that triggers coughing bouts.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Drink plenty of warm fluids like peppermint or chrysanthemum tea to gently release the exterior. Avoid spicy, fried, or greasy foods that generate heat. Rest in a well-ventilated but draft-free room. A light diet with pears or loquats can help moisten the Lungs and ease throat discomfort.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Weak, lingering cough (not violent spasms), Fatigue and shortness of breath, Dry mouth and throat with little desire to drink, Spontaneous sweating during the day or night sweats, Weak voice, reluctance to speak, Poor appetite
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overexertion or prolonged talking, Cold, dry, or dusty air, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Emotional stress or worry, Late nights and insufficient sleep
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, moist air (e.g., humidifier), Gentle, slow breathing exercises, Nourishing, easy-to-digest foods like congee
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  After days or weeks of violent, spasmodic coughing, the Lung's vital energy (Qi) and nourishing fluids (Yin) become deeply depleted. Without enough Qi to propel a strong cough or enough Yin to moisten the airways, the cough turns weak and lingering, often with a dry, scratchy throat. This is a recovery-stage pattern where the body is trying to rebuild after the storm of the acute illness.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red or pale-red body with little or no coating, possibly dry or cracked. Pulse: fine (thready) and weak, possibly rapid.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: After the exhausting coughing phase, the body's Qi and fluids are deeply depleted. Overexertion and talking further drain Qi, while dry air and spicy foods consume Yin. Rest, moist air, and nourishing foods help rebuild Qi and Yin, allowing the Lungs to recover and the cough to gradually resolve.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Focus on gentle, nourishing foods like congee, steamed pears, and bone broth. Avoid spicy, drying, or cold raw foods. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion; gentle breathing exercises and short walks can help rebuild strength without straining the Lungs.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### Can TCM treat whooping cough?
Yes. TCM does not treat the bacterium directly but addresses the body’s response to it - the pattern of coughing, phlegm, and inflammation. By clearing Wind, Heat, and Phlegm according to the stage of the illness, TCM can reduce the severity and duration of the coughing fits and support recovery. It is used alongside antibiotics when needed.

### How soon can I expect the coughing fits to stop?
Most patients notice a decrease in the severity and frequency of coughing fits within the first week of herbal treatment and acupuncture. In the early stage, progression to severe spasms can often be halted in a few days. Once the full spasmodic phase is underway, it may take two to four weeks to calm the cough significantly. Full recovery of energy and resolution of the lingering cough can take several more weeks.

### Is acupuncture safe for children with paroxysmal coughing?
Yes, acupuncture is safe for children when performed by a licensed practitioner. For very young children, non-needle techniques like acupressure, pediatric tui na massage, or gentle cupping are often used instead. Points on the back and chest help open the Lungs and calm the cough, and children typically tolerate these sessions well.

### Can I use TCM alongside antibiotics?
Yes. TCM herbal formulas and acupuncture can be safely combined with antibiotics for pertussis. There are no known serious interactions between common cough-relieving herbs and macrolide antibiotics. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor of all medications and supplements you are taking.

### What foods should I avoid during treatment?
Generally, you should avoid cold, raw, dairy, greasy, and overly spicy foods, as these can generate or worsen Phlegm and irritate the Lungs. Instead, favor warm, moistening foods like pear, congee, and easy-to-digest soups. Specific dietary guidance will vary with your pattern, and your practitioner will give you detailed advice.

### Will the cough come back after TCM treatment?
TCM aims to restore the body’s underlying balance, not just suppress the cough. By fully clearing the pathogen and then strengthening the Lung’s Qi and Yin, the treatment reduces the likelihood of a lingering or recurrent cough. However, a new exposure to infection could still trigger another episode, though a stronger constitution may handle it more effectively.

## การวิจัยทางวิทยาศาสตร์สมัยใหม่ (Modern Scientific Research)
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for pertussis** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004091.pub2))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Wu T, Yang X, Zeng X, Poole P. Chinese herbal medicine for pertussis. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2008, Issue 1. Art. No.: CD004091.*
   A Cochrane systematic review of 15 RCTs involving 1,678 participants. Some Chinese herbal preparations appeared to reduce cough duration and improve recovery compared to antibiotics, but the evidence was limited by poor study quality and small sample sizes.

2. **Clinical observation on modified Sang Bai Pi Tang in treating pertussis in children**
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Li X, Zhang Y, Wang H. Clinical observation on modified Sang Bai Pi Tang in treating pertussis in children. Journal of Pediatrics of Traditional Chinese Medicine. 2015;11(3):28-30.*
   A trial of 60 children with pertussis in the spasmodic stage (Phlegm-Heat pattern) found that modified Sang Bai Pi Tang significantly reduced daily coughing fits and shortened the overall disease course compared to conventional symptomatic treatment alone.

3. **Acupuncture for chronic cough: a systematic review**
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Lee MS, Choi TY, Kim JI, et al. Acupuncture for chronic cough: a systematic review. European Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2013;5(2):114-120.*
   This systematic review of 12 RCTs concluded that acupuncture may provide some benefit for chronic cough, including cough of respiratory infection origin, but the evidence for spasmodic cough specifically is still preliminary and requires further rigorous study.
