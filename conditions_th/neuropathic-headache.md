## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
Neuropathic headache is pain caused by damage or dysfunction in the nerves that supply the head and face. It can feel like burning, stabbing, shooting, or electric shock sensations, and it often occurs without an obvious external trigger. Common causes include nerve compression, shingles (postherpetic neuralgia), diabetes, or injury. Diagnosis is based on symptom description, neurological exam, and sometimes imaging or nerve conduction studies to rule out other conditions.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Conventional treatment typically relies on medications that calm overactive nerve signals, such as gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline), or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (duloxetine). Nerve blocks or local anesthetic injections may provide temporary relief for some types. In cases linked to structural compression, surgery might be considered.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
TCM understands neuropathic headache primarily through the concept of wind (风, fēng) - but not just the wind outside. Internal wind, often stirred up by Liver imbalance, can rise to the head and disrupt the nerves, causing pain that moves, throbs, or feels like electric shocks. External wind, like Wind-Cold, can invade the body's surface and block the channels, creating a tight, constricting pain. The head is the meeting point of all the Yang channels, so any disruption to the smooth flow of Qi and blood here can trigger nerve-related pain.
The Liver is central because it governs the smooth flow of Qi and blood. When stress, anger, or frustration cause Liver Qi to stagnate, it can transform into heat and rise as Liver Yang, creating a distending, throbbing pain at the temples. Over time, this stagnant Qi can also lead to Blood Stagnation - a deeper, fixed, stabbing pain that often follows an old injury or long-term emotional tension. The pain is like a nail driven into one spot, and it worsens at night when blood flow slows.
The Spleen is another key player. A weak Spleen (often from poor diet or overthinking) fails to transform fluids, leading to dampness and phlegm. This phlegm turbidity can cloud the head, causing a heavy, foggy headache with nausea and a feeling of mental dullness - as if the head is wrapped in a wet towel. Finally, when the Spleen and Stomach can't produce enough Qi and Blood, the head lacks nourishment, resulting in a dull, empty ache that worsens with fatigue and improves with rest.
This is why one Western diagnosis - neuropathic headache - can have so many different TCM causes. A throbbing, distending pain at the temples might be Liver Yang Rising, while a dull, empty ache could be Qi and Blood Deficiency. Even the location matters: temple pain often points to the Liver and Gallbladder channels, while a band-like tightness across the forehead may signal Wind-Cold or Damp-Phlegm. By identifying the pattern, TCM practitioners can choose acupuncture points and herbal formulas that address the specific imbalance, not just mask the pain.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the headache's quality, location, and what makes it better or worse. The nature of the pain-whether it throbs, stabs, feels heavy, or aches dully-is the first clue pointing toward one pattern over another. They will also ask about triggers like stress, cold weather, or fatigue, and examine the tongue and pulse to confirm the diagnosis.
If the pain is distending or throbbing, especially on the sides of the head, and comes with dizziness, irritability, or a flushed face, this points to Liver Yang Rising. The tongue is often red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid. This pattern is very common in recurrent neuropathic headaches and is often triggered by emotional stress.
When the pain is fixed and feels like a sharp stab, as if a nail is driven into one spot, and it worsens at night, Blood Stagnation is suspected. The tongue may appear dark purple with stasis spots, and the pulse is choppy. This pattern frequently underlies chronic headaches that have lasted for months or years, indicating poor blood flow in the head's channels.
A heavy, muddled sensation as if the head is wrapped in a wet cloth, together with nausea, chest tightness, and a greasy tongue coating, suggests Damp-Phlegm. The pulse is typically slippery. This pattern arises when phlegm and dampness obstruct the clear yang from rising to the head, often seen in people with digestive sluggishness or a rich diet.
If the headache is a dull, hollow ache that feels better with rest and worse after overwork, Qi and Blood Deficiency is the likely cause. The tongue is pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse is weak and thready. This pattern reflects insufficient nourishment reaching the head, common in those who are run down or recovering from illness.
Wind-Cold invasion causes a tight, constricting headache that is aggravated by cold drafts and relieved by warmth. The tongue coating is thin and white, and the pulse is floating and tight. This pattern is less common in chronic neuropathic headache but can appear in acute flare-ups, especially when exposure to wind and cold triggers an episode.

### Liver Yang Rising
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Throbbing or distending headache, Pain at temples or top of head, Worse with stress and anger, Dizziness or vertigo, Irritability and quick temper, Flushed face and red eyes, Tinnitus (ringing in the ears)
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Emotional stress or anger, Alcohol and spicy food, Lack of sleep, Hot weather or sauna, Overwork and eye strain
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest, Cool, dark room, Gentle exercise or movement, Cooling foods (cucumber, pear), Deep breathing or meditation
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When Liver Yin is insufficient to anchor the Yang, the Yang becomes hyperactive and surges upward along the channels to the head. This upward rush of heat and pressure causes the throbbing, distending pain at the temples or top of the head. The nerve irritation is a result of this internal wind-heat disrupting the clear orifices.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red body, red sides, thin dry yellow or scanty coat, possibly trembling. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), may be fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is prominent.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: <p>Stress and anger directly stir Liver Qi to stagnate and then surge upward, which is the core mechanism of Liver Yang Rising. Alcohol and spicy foods add internal heat, further pushing Yang upward. Rest and a cool, dark environment help anchor the Yang back down, while cooling foods and relaxation techniques nourish Yin and settle the mind, reducing the upward rush that causes the headache.</p>
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy and fried foods, which add heat and aggravate Yang. Eat cooling, yin-nourishing foods like cucumber, celery, and pear. Prioritize regular sleep and gentle exercise like walking or tai chi to smooth Liver Qi flow. Stress management through meditation or breathing exercises is essential.

### Blood Stagnation
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Fixed, stabbing pain that does not move, Pain worsens at night, Dark or purplish tongue with stasis spots, Dark circles under the eyes, Pain worse with pressure
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Cold weather, wind, or drafts, Emotional stress or anger, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Nighttime (pain naturally worsens), Pressure on the painful spot
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm compress on the neck, Gentle exercise or movement, Rest, Massage or acupressure, Warm drinks (like ginger tea)
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or gets stuck, the tiny vessels that nourish the head become blocked. This stagnant blood acts like a dam, creating a fixed, knifelike pain that does not move. The pain often follows an old head injury or long-term stress that froze the Qi, and it deepens at night when the body's circulation naturally slows down.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with dark stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coating. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè), rough and uneven like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦 xián) if Qi stagnation is present.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Blood stasis means the blood is thick and sluggish. Cold constricts vessels further, while warmth and movement encourage circulation. Stress tightens the Liver Qi, which then fails to move blood, so calming emotions helps. Nighttime is Yin in nature, and stillness allows stagnation to settle, so the pain worsens then. Gentle exercise and massage physically help move stagnant blood, reducing the stabbing sensation.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warming, blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, and dark leafy greens. Avoid cold, raw foods that can congeal blood. Gentle daily exercise such as walking or tai chi helps keep Qi and blood flowing. Avoid smoking and excessive alcohol, which thicken the blood. Manage stress to prevent Qi stagnation that worsens blood stasis.

### Damp-Phlegm
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Heavy, wrapped sensation in the head, Nausea or vomiting, Chest and upper abdominal stuffiness, Poor appetite, Fatigue and drowsiness, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Damp, cold weather, Greasy, heavy meals, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting, Overthinking or worry, Dairy products
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Gentle exercise or movement, Warm, dry weather, Light, warm meals, Warm drinks (like ginger tea), Deep breathing or meditation
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When the Spleen is weakened by poor diet, overthinking, or a damp environment, it fails to transform and transport fluids. These fluids accumulate into dampness and then condense into phlegm. The phlegm turbidity rises to the head, obstructing the clear orifices and blocking the flow of Qi and blood. This results in a heavy, foggy headache that often comes with nausea, chest stuffiness, and a feeling of mental cloudiness.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue is pale, swollen, with teeth marks and a thick, white, greasy coating. Pulse is slippery (滑, Huá), like a bead rolling through oil.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Dampness and phlegm are produced when the Spleen’s transforming function is weak; greasy, cold, and dairy-rich foods further burden the Spleen and generate more dampness. A sedentary lifestyle allows Qi to stagnate, allowing dampness to accumulate. Overthinking directly damages Spleen Qi. In contrast, gentle movement stimulates Qi flow, helping to transform and drain dampness. Warm, dry environments and warm foods support the Spleen’s function and disperse dampness, while ginger warms the middle burner and resolves phlegm.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warm, cooked foods and avoid cold, raw, greasy, or dairy-rich items that create dampness. Incorporate gentle daily movement like walking to help Qi circulate and transform phlegm. Ginger tea can be helpful. Keep living spaces dry and well-ventilated.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Dull, aching head pain, Worsens with fatigue and overwork, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Pale complexion and lips, Fatigue and weakness
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overwork or fatigue, Skipping meals, Overthinking or worry, Cold or raw foods and drinks
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest, Warmth, Nourishing warm foods, Gentle exercise or movement
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Spleen and Stomach produce Qi and Blood from the food we eat. When they are weak, the body's supply of Qi and Blood runs low. The head, as the highest part of the body, relies on a steady upward supply of these nutrients to function properly. Without enough nourishment, the head feels dull, empty, and achy - a classic Qi and Blood deficiency headache. This type of pain is often lingering and gets worse when you're tired because exertion drains the already-scarce resources.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Rest and sleep conserve Qi and Blood, which are already limited in this pattern, while overwork and fatigue drain them further, making the headache worse. Nourishing, warm foods support the Spleen's ability to produce Qi and Blood, whereas cold, raw foods weaken the Spleen and impair digestion. Excessive thinking and worry consume Spleen Qi directly, so mental rest is just as important as physical rest.

### Wind-Cold
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Tight, constricting head pain, Worse with cold or wind exposure, Strong aversion to cold, chills, No sweating, Stiff neck and upper back
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Cold weather, wind, or drafts, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overwork or fatigue
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warmth, Rest, Warm drinks (like ginger tea), Covering up
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When Wind and Cold invade the body, they attack the surface and block the flow of Qi and blood through the head meridians. Cold has a contracting, tightening nature, so the pain feels like a tight band squeezing the head. The body's defensive Qi rises to fight the invader, causing chills and a strong aversion to cold, while the obstruction in the channels produces stiffness in the neck and upper back.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating and tight.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Cold and Wind contract the channels and block Qi, which is the very mechanism of this headache, so exposure makes it worse. Warmth relaxes the tightness and helps push the pathogen out, while rest conserves the body's defensive Qi so it can fight the invader more effectively.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Keep warm and avoid drafts. Drink warm ginger and scallion tea to help the body push out the Cold. Rest and let yourself sweat lightly under a blanket, but don't overheat. Eat warm, cooked foods and stay away from cold drinks and raw salads until the headache passes.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### Can acupuncture really help nerve-related headache pain?
Yes, many patients find significant relief. Acupuncture works by stimulating specific points to release natural painkillers (endorphins) and improve blood flow to the head. It also calms the nervous system and helps regulate the overactive nerve signals that cause neuropathic pain. The effect is often cumulative - you may notice some relief after the first session, but lasting change typically requires a course of 6-12 treatments.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
For most neuropathic headaches, we recommend 1-2 sessions per week for 4-8 weeks. Acute or excess patterns (like Wind-Cold or Liver Yang Rising) often respond faster - sometimes in just 2-4 sessions. Chronic patterns with underlying deficiency or long-standing Blood Stagnation may need 2-3 months of weekly treatment to rebuild reserves and fully resolve the pain. Your practitioner will reassess after 4-6 sessions and adjust the plan.

### Can I take herbs with my nerve pain medication?
In most cases, yes - but it's essential to tell both your TCM practitioner and your doctor what you're taking. Some herbs can interact with anticoagulants or sedatives, so your practitioner will choose a formula that's safe alongside your current medications. Never stop or reduce your prescription medication without your doctor's guidance. Many patients find that as TCM treatment progresses, they can gradually reduce their medication dose under medical supervision.

### Will the headache come back after treatment stops?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance, not just suppress symptoms. When treatment successfully resolves the pattern - for example, clearing stagnant blood, nourishing deficient Qi, or subduing rising Yang - the headaches often stay away. However, if the lifestyle or emotional triggers return (chronic stress, poor diet, overwork), the pattern can re-emerge. Your practitioner will give you dietary and lifestyle advice to help maintain the results long-term.

### What changes should I make to my diet?
While specific advice depends on your pattern, a few general rules help most neuropathic headache sufferers: avoid cold, raw foods and iced drinks (they constrict blood flow and create dampness), reduce greasy or spicy foods (they can stir Liver Yang and generate phlegm), and eat warm, cooked meals that are easy to digest. Ginger tea can help warm the channels, and a regular eating schedule prevents blood sugar dips that can trigger headaches.

### Is TCM safe for neuropathic headache during pregnancy?
Acupuncture is generally considered safe during pregnancy when performed by a qualified practitioner, and many points are specifically avoided to protect the pregnancy. Herbal medicine during pregnancy requires extra caution - some herbs are contraindicated. Always inform your practitioner if you are pregnant or trying to conceive, and they will tailor the treatment accordingly. It's also wise to consult your obstetrician before starting any new therapy.

## การวิจัยทางวิทยาศาสตร์สมัยใหม่ (Modern Scientific Research)
1. **Acupuncture for the prevention of tension-type headache** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007587.pub2))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Acupuncture for the prevention of tension-type headache. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2016;4:CD007587.*
   Cochrane systematic review of 12 trials with 2349 participants. Found that adding acupuncture to usual care or acupuncture compared with sham reduces headache frequency; acupuncture was at least as effective as prophylactic medication with fewer adverse events.

2. **Acupuncture for migraine prophylaxis** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001218.pub2))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Acupuncture for migraine prophylaxis. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2009;(1):CD001218.*
   Cochrane review of 22 trials with 4419 participants. Concluded that acupuncture is effective for reducing migraine frequency and can be recommended as a prophylactic option, with benefits persisting over 6 months.

3. **A comprehensive review of traditional Chinese medicine in treating neuropathic pain** ([ลิงก์](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11407996))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Yang M, et al. A comprehensive review of traditional Chinese medicine in treating neuropathic pain. Front Pharmacol. 2024;15:1445530.*
   Narrative review summarizing TCM formulas for neuropathic pain, including headache. Discusses Tian Ma Gou Teng Yin, Xue Fu Zhu Yu Tang, and others, with evidence from animal models and clinical studies on analgesic and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.
