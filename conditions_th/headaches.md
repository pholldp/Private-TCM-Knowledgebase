## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
Western medicine categorizes headaches into primary types (tension‑type, migraine, cluster) and secondary headaches caused by underlying conditions like sinus infections, high blood pressure, or head injury. Diagnosis relies on a detailed history of the pain - its location, quality, timing, and triggers - along with a neurological exam. Imaging (CT or MRI) may be ordered if red flags suggest a more serious cause. Most primary headaches are managed with over‑the‑counter analgesics, prescription medications for acute attacks or prevention, and lifestyle advice.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Common treatments include NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), acetaminophen, and combination analgesics for tension‑type headaches. Migraines are treated with triptans, CGRP antagonists, and preventive medications like beta‑blockers, anticonvulsants, or antidepressants. Cluster headaches often require oxygen therapy or injectable triptans. Physical therapy, stress management, and trigger avoidance are also recommended.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
In TCM, a headache is never just a headache - it’s a signal that the flow of Qi and blood to and through the head has been disrupted. The head is the meeting point of all the Yang channels, and it requires a constant, unobstructed supply of clear Yang Qi to think clearly and feel comfortable. Any factor that blocks this flow, creates heat, or fails to nourish the head can trigger pain. TCM therefore reads the headache’s location, quality, and accompanying symptoms as a map pointing to the organ system or pathogen responsible.
The Liver plays a central role in many chronic headaches. The Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body, and emotional stress - frustration, anger, resentment - can cause Liver Qi to stagnate and transform into Fire or Yang that rises to the head, producing a throbbing, distending pain, often on the sides or top. This is why stress is such a universal headache trigger: it directly disrupts the Liver’s core function.
External pathogens like Wind, Cold, and Heat can also invade the body’s surface and lodge in the head and neck channels. A Wind‑Cold headache feels tight and constricting, like a band around the head, and gets worse with cold. A Wind‑Heat headache feels distending and hot, often with fever and sore throat. These patterns are typically acute and linked to a recent exposure or a cold.
Other patterns include Blood Stagnation, where a fixed, stabbing pain results from old injury or long‑standing Qi stagnation, and Phlegm‑Dampness, where a heavy, foggy headache comes from poor digestion and fluid metabolism. Even a deficiency of Yin fluids can create a low‑grade empty heat that rises to the head, causing a dull, afternoon headache. Because each of these mechanisms requires a different treatment, TCM’s first task is to identify the pattern behind the pain.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner starts by asking what the headache actually feels like and when it strikes. The quality of the pain, its location, and the circumstances that make it better or worse are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another. They then check the tongue and pulse to confirm the picture.
If the headache arrived after exposure to cold or wind and is accompanied by a stiff neck, chills, and an aversion to drafts, that suggests a Wind‑Cold pattern. The tongue usually looks pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels floating and tight. These headaches often feel heavy and constricted, as if a tight band is wrapped around the head.
When the headache is more of a distending or throbbing pain, with a flushed face, thirst, and perhaps a mild fever, a Wind‑Heat pattern is likely. Here the tongue appears redder with a yellow coating, and the pulse is rapid and floating. This type often comes on suddenly during a cold or flu and feels worse with heat.
Headaches that flare up with stress, anger, or frustration and feel like a pounding or distending sensation on the sides or top of the head point to Liver Yang Rising. Irritability, dizziness, a bitter taste in the mouth, and a red tongue with a thin yellow coating are common. The pulse is typically wiry and rapid, reflecting the upward surge of Liver Yang.
When the pain is fixed in one spot and feels sharp or stabbing, Blood Stagnation is the likely culprit. The complexion may appear dark or dull, and the tongue often shows purple spots or a dusky color. The pulse feels choppy. This pattern can follow a head injury or develop after a long period of emotional constraint.
A heavy, stuffy headache that makes the whole head feel foggy and is accompanied by nausea or a sensation of muzziness suggests Phlegm in the channels. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. These headaches often worsen in damp weather and are linked to sluggish digestion.
If the headache is mild but persistent, worse in the afternoon or evening, and comes with a dry mouth, night sweats, or a feeling of heat in the palms and soles, it may be Empty‑Heat from Yin Deficiency. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thready and rapid. This pattern reflects a deep, chronic depletion.

### Liver Yang Rising
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Throbbing or distending pain, Located at temples or top of head, Worse with stress, anger, or frustration, Irritability and quick temper, Dizziness and vertigo, Flushed face and red eyes, Bitter taste in the mouth
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Stress and anger, Alcohol and spicy food, Overwork and lack of sleep, Hot environments, Premenstrual phase
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest and quiet, Cool compress on forehead, Lying down in a dark room, Gentle neck stretches, Deep breathing or meditation
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Liver is responsible for keeping Qi flowing smoothly. When stress, frustration, or long-term overwork deplete the Liver's Yin and Blood, the cooling, anchoring force of Yin weakens. Without that anchor, Liver Yang surges upward like a kettle boiling over, carrying heat and pressure to the head. This is what produces the throbbing, distending pain-often at the temples or top of the head-along with dizziness and irritability.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red body, redder at the sides, with a thin dry yellow or scanty coating; may tremble slightly. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), forceful at the left middle position; may also be fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is prominent.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Anger and stress directly stir Liver Qi to surge upward, which is the very mechanism of this pattern, so calming the mind and resting ease it. Alcohol and spicy foods are heating and add fuel to the rising Yang, while a cool, quiet environment helps the Yang descend. Overwork and poor sleep deplete Yin, weakening the anchor that holds Yang down; premenstrually, blood moves to the uterus, leaving the Liver with less Yin to restrain Yang, which is why headaches often spike at that time. Gentle movement releases tension without overheating, and cooling compresses help settle the heat in the head.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Keep alcohol, coffee, and spicy or greasy foods to a minimum-they add heat and fuel the upward surge. Prioritize winding down before bed and getting enough sleep to protect Liver Yin. Gentle, regular movement like walking or tai chi helps Qi flow without overheating. Practice stress-management techniques such as deep breathing or meditation to prevent anger and frustration from flaring the Liver.

### Wind-Cold
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Band-like or tight headache, Worse with cold or wind, Aversion to cold, chills, Stiff neck
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Exposure to cold wind, Cold drinks
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm compress, Warm drinks, Rest in a warm room, Covering head and neck
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Wind and Cold are external pathogens that attack the body's surface first. When they invade the head and neck, they obstruct the flow of Qi and blood in the channels, creating a tight, constricting pain. The body's defensive Qi rushes to the surface to fight, causing chills, a stiff neck, and an aversion to cold.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin, white, moist coat. Pulse: floating and tight.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Wind-Cold invades the body's surface and obstructs the flow of Qi in the head. Cold constricts, so warmth relaxes the channels and helps the body's defensive Qi push the pathogen out. Rest conserves energy for the fight, while cold and drafts allow more pathogen to enter.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Keep your neck and head covered when it's windy or cold. Drink warm ginger tea with a little brown sugar to help your body sweat lightly and expel the pathogen. Rest in a warm room without drafts.

### Wind-Heat
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Distending headache, Fever with mild chills, Sore throat, Thirst with desire to drink, Red face and eyes
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Windy weather, Spicy and greasy foods, Overexertion, Hot environments
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest, Cool environment, Drinking plenty of water, Light, bland foods
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Wind-Heat is an exterior pathogen that enters through the nose and mouth. It rises to the head, where the Heat causes distention and the Wind disrupts the smooth flow of Qi, producing a distending headache. This headache usually appears along with other signs of a cold or flu, such as fever, sore throat, and thirst.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: Red tip and edges with a thin yellow coating (may be white in early stages). Pulse: Floating and rapid.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Wind-Heat is an external pathogen that thrives in hot, windy conditions and is aggravated by internal heat from spicy foods. Rest and cooling fluids help the body's defensive Qi expel the pathogen, while light foods avoid adding internal heat that would worsen the headache and fever.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Rest in a cool, well-ventilated room and drink plenty of water or herbal teas like peppermint or chrysanthemum. Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, which can generate internal heat. Eat light, easily digestible meals such as congee or steamed vegetables.

### Blood Stagnation
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Fixed, stabbing pain that does not move, Pain worse at night, Pain worse with pressure, Dark or purplish lips, nails, or complexion, Dark menstrual blood with clots
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Cold weather or cold foods, Prolonged inactivity, Pressure on the head, Emotional suppression
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Gentle movement, Warm compress, Blood-moving spices (turmeric, ginger)
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When blood flow becomes stagnant in the channels of the head, often due to an old injury, chronic stress, or long-standing Qi stagnation, the congealed blood blocks the free movement of Qi. This creates a fixed, boring or stabbing pain that does not move because the obstruction is localized. The pain typically worsens at night, when circulation naturally slows, and is aggravated by pressure because pressing on the stagnant area further obstructs flow.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: dark purple or dusky body with stasis spots; sublingual veins distended and dark. Pulse: choppy (Se), rough and uneven, possibly wiry.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Cold and inactivity cause blood to congeal and stagnate further, so warmth and gentle movement help disperse stasis and restore flow. Pressure directly aggravates a fixed obstruction, while emotional suppression constrains Qi, which is the force that moves blood; releasing emotions and moving the body keeps Qi and blood circulating.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Incorporate gentle daily movement like walking or stretching to keep blood flowing. Avoid prolonged sitting and cold, raw foods that can congeal blood. Include warming spices like turmeric, ginger, and a little black pepper in meals. Manage stress with breathing exercises, as emotional suppression worsens stagnation.

- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Damp weather, Greasy, sweet, or cold foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Overthinking or worry, Overeating

- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Phlegm-Dampness forms when the Spleen fails to transform fluids properly, often due to poor diet or overthinking. If the Phlegm stagnates for a long time, it can block Blood flow, leading to fixed stabbing pain as well.

- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Phlegm-Dampness forms when the Spleen is weakened by poor diet or overthinking. Damp environments and greasy foods add to the burden, while movement and warmth help the Spleen transform and transport fluids. Clearing the head with acupressure and avoiding phlegm-generating habits allows clear Yang to ascend, relieving the heavy headache.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Avoid dairy, greasy, sweet, and cold foods that create Phlegm. Eat warm, cooked meals with mild spices like ginger. Regular gentle exercise like walking or yoga helps the Spleen transform Dampness and keeps Qi moving.

### Empty-Heat caused by Yin Deficiency
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/empty-heat-caused-by-yin-deficiency)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Dull, empty headache, Worse in the afternoon or evening, Night sweats, Heat in the palms, soles, and chest (five-palm heat), Dry mouth and throat, thirst for small sips, Malar flush (flushed cheekbones)
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Staying up late, Spicy and greasy foods, Stress and overthinking, Hot environments
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Adequate sleep and rest, Cool environment, Moistening foods (pear, lily bulb, black sesame), Avoiding overwork
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When the body's cooling, nourishing Yin fluids are depleted, a relative excess of Yang emerges, creating a low-grade 'empty heat.' This heat rises upward and disturbs the head, producing a dull, empty headache that lacks the throbbing intensity of Liver Yang rising. Because Yin is naturally weaker in the afternoon and evening, the pain and other heat signs tend to worsen at those times.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red, thin, dry body with cracks and little or no coating. Pulse: fine (thready) and rapid, often weak at the left chi (kidney) position.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Yin deficiency is the root of this pattern. Activities that consume Yin-like staying up late, overwork, and emotional strain-worsen the empty heat, making the headache and other heat signs more pronounced. Spicy and fried foods add internal heat, further depleting Yin. In contrast, rest and sleep allow the body to regenerate Yin, while cool environments and moistening foods help replenish fluids and calm the empty fire, easing the headache.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Prioritize rest and avoid staying up late, as sleep is the body's best Yin restorer. Eat moistening, cooling foods such as pears, lily bulbs, black sesame, and tofu; limit spicy, greasy, and fried foods that consume Yin. Gentle exercise like walking or qigong is fine, but avoid intense sweating. Keep a cool, calm environment, especially in the evening.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### How does acupuncture help headaches?
Acupuncture relieves headaches by restoring the free flow of Qi and blood in the channels that run through the head and neck. Fine needles placed at specific points - often on the head, hands, and feet - signal the body to release natural pain‑relieving substances and relax tight muscles. Many patients feel a reduction in pain during or immediately after a session, and regular treatments can reduce headache frequency over time.

### Can Chinese herbs stop a headache quickly?
Yes, especially for acute headaches caused by Wind‑Cold or Wind‑Heat, where the right formula can bring relief within hours to a day. For chronic patterns, herbs work more gradually to correct the underlying imbalance, so a single dose may not stop a severe migraine, but consistent use over weeks reduces attacks. Always consult a practitioner for a formula tailored to your pattern - random herbs from a health store are unlikely to match your needs.

### Is TCM safe for headaches during pregnancy?
Acupuncture can be very safe and effective for headaches during pregnancy when performed by a qualified practitioner who avoids certain points that could stimulate contractions. Herbal medicine, however, requires extra caution - many herbs are contraindicated during pregnancy, so never self‑prescribe. Always inform your practitioner if you are pregnant or trying to conceive, and work with someone experienced in prenatal TCM.

### How long does it take to see results?
Acute headaches may improve after 1-3 sessions. For chronic or recurrent headaches, most patients notice a decrease in frequency and severity within 4-8 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs. Deficiency‑based headaches take longer - 3-6 months - because the body needs time to rebuild depleted reserves. Your practitioner will reassess your progress every few weeks and adjust the treatment plan accordingly.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a supporting role. General advice includes avoiding icy drinks and excessive raw foods, which can constrict Qi and worsen Wind‑Cold or Phlegm patterns. Spicy, greasy, and fried foods may aggravate Liver Yang or Wind‑Heat headaches. Your practitioner will give you specific dietary recommendations based on your pattern, but even simple changes - eating warm, cooked meals and staying hydrated - can make a noticeable difference.

### Can I take painkillers while using Chinese herbs?
Yes, in most cases, Chinese herbs can be taken alongside over‑the‑counter painkillers, but you should space them apart by at least two hours to avoid any potential interaction. However, if you are on daily preventive medications or blood thinners, you must inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor, because some herbs (like Dāng Guī or Chuān Xiōng) can affect blood clotting. Never stop or adjust prescription medications without consulting your doctor.

## การวิจัยทางวิทยาศาสตร์สมัยใหม่ (Modern Scientific Research)
1. **Acupuncture for the prevention of tension-type headache** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007587.pub2))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Acupuncture for the prevention of tension-type headache. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2016;4:CD007587.*
   Cochrane systematic review finding that acupuncture reduces headache frequency in patients with frequent episodic or chronic tension-type headache, with effects lasting at least 6 months.

2. **Acupuncture for the prevention of episodic migraine** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001218.pub3))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Acupuncture for the prevention of episodic migraine. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2016;6:CD001218.*
   Cochrane systematic review concluding that acupuncture is at least as effective as prophylactic drug treatment for migraine and has fewer adverse effects.

3. **Acupuncture for chronic pain: individual patient data meta-analysis** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2012.3654))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Vickers AJ, Cronin AM, Maschino AC, et al. Acupuncture for chronic pain: individual patient data meta-analysis. Arch Intern Med. 2012;172(19):1444-53.*
   Meta-analysis of individual patient data showing that acupuncture is effective for chronic headache, with a significant difference between true and sham acupuncture.

4. **Chinese herbal medicine for migraine: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials** ([ลิงก์](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2011.0364))
   *วารสารวิชาการ: Li X, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for migraine: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials. J Altern Complement Med. 2012;18(4):311-7.*
   Systematic review suggesting that Chinese herbal medicine may reduce migraine frequency and intensity, though trial quality is generally low.
