## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
In conventional medicine, a persistent low-grade fever that worsens with physical or mental exertion often falls into the category of ‘fever of unknown origin’ or is associated with chronic fatigue syndrome, post-viral syndromes, or functional somatic syndromes. Standard workups - blood tests, imaging - may return normal, leaving both patient and physician searching for an explanation. The fever is real, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Without a clear organic cause, treatment is often supportive: rest, hydration, and over-the-counter antipyretics like acetaminophen or ibuprofen, though these provide only temporary relief. If an underlying condition is suspected, treatment targets that, but for many patients, the fever persists despite normal test results.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
In TCM, the Spleen is the engine that turns food into Qi and raises clear energy upward. When Spleen Qi is weakened - by overwork, poor diet, or chronic illness - it can no longer lift the clear Yang. Instead, that energy sinks, stagnates, and generates a smoldering internal heat called Yin Fire (阴火). This heat rises to the surface as a low-grade fever. Because the Qi is already fragile, any physical or mental exertion drains it further, fanning the Yin Fire and making the fever spike.
The fever’s rhythm is key: it worsens with effort and improves with rest. Unlike fevers from external pathogens (like a cold), this internal heat is not fought with cooling herbs. Instead, TCM uses the ‘sweet-warmth eliminates heat’ principle - warming and tonifying the Spleen Qi to anchor the Yang. This is why formulas like Bu Zhong Yi Qi Tang, rich in Qi-boosting herbs, are the mainstay, not fever-reducing drugs.
Depending on the depth of the deficiency, TCM distinguishes several patterns. In pure Qi Deficiency Fever, the fever is the main complaint, accompanied by fatigue, spontaneous sweating, a pale tongue, and a weak pulse. In broader Qi Deficiency, the fever is just one symptom among many, like poor appetite and loose stools. In Spleen Qi Sinking, the sinking Qi causes a bearing-down sensation and possibly prolapse, alongside the exertion-induced fever. All share the root of Spleen Qi weakness but require nuanced treatment to lift, warm, and replenish.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner starts by asking about the fever’s rhythm. If it’s a low-grade warmth that clearly spikes after physical or mental exertion and eases with rest, that points toward a Qi deficiency root. The practitioner then looks for the specific pattern by exploring how the whole body is functioning.
When the fever is the main concern and comes with marked fatigue, spontaneous sweating, a pale tongue, and a weak or thready pulse, the picture fits Qi Deficiency Fever (气虚发热). This is the classic pattern where Spleen Qi is too weak to contain the body’s warmth, allowing a “Yin Fire” to flare up, especially after activity.
If the person complains more about overall exhaustion, poor appetite, and loose stools than about the fever itself, the diagnosis may settle on a broader Qi Deficiency. Here the low-grade heat is just one sign of a system running on empty. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels forceless, but the fever may be milder or less consistent.
When the fatigue comes with a heavy, sinking sensation in the abdomen, a feeling of bearing down, or even prolapse, the practitioner considers Spleen Qi Sinking. The low-grade fever in this pattern is also worse after exertion, but the telltale clue is that the Qi is not just weak - it has lost its ability to hold organs and clear Yang upward. The tongue is pale and the pulse is weak and may feel particularly deep.

### Qi Deficiency Fever
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-fever)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Low-grade fever that worsens with exertion, Fatigue and lack of strength, Shortness of breath with reluctance to speak, Spontaneous sweating, Pale or sallow complexion, Loose stools and reduced appetite
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Physical or mental overexertion, Skipping meals or irregular eating, Cold, raw, or greasy foods and iced drinks, Stress and worry
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest and sleep, Warm drinks and soups, Small, frequent meals, Warm compress on abdomen
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When the Spleen Qi is weakened, it cannot transform food into clear, ascending energy. Instead, that energy sinks, stagnates, and generates a smoldering internal heat called Yin Fire (阴火 yīn huǒ). Because the Qi is already fragile, any physical or mental effort drains it further, fanning the Yin Fire and making the low-grade fever spike.
At the same time, the body's protective Qi is too weak to hold the pores closed, so spontaneous sweating appears. The result is a cycle of fatigue, fever, and dampness that flares up whenever you try to push through.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: pale, puffy, teeth-marked body with a thin white coating. Pulse: weak (Xu) and fine (Xi), often feeling large but forceless (Hong Da Wu Li).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Overexertion and irregular eating directly drain the already weak Spleen Qi, causing the Yin Fire to flare up and the fever to spike. Rest and warm, cooked nourishment gently rebuild that Qi and allow the Yang to rise properly. Warmth on the abdomen supports the Spleen's transforming function, while cold and raw foods act like a wet blanket, dousing the digestive fire and making the deficiency worse.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible meals - think soups, congees, and stews - and avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Rest is medicine; schedule short breaks and avoid pushing through fatigue. Gentle movement like walking or taiji can gently lift Qi, but stop before you feel drained.

### Qi Deficiency
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Low-grade fever that worsens with exertion, Fatigue and lack of strength, Shortness of breath on mild exertion, Spontaneous sweating, Pale or sallow complexion, Loose stools and reduced appetite
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Physical or mental overexertion, Stress and worry, Cold, raw, or greasy foods and iced drinks, Exposure to cold or damp environments, Skipping meals or irregular eating
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest and sleep, Warm, easily digestible meals, Gentle movement like walking or qigong, Warmth and avoiding cold drafts, Regular meals at set times
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When the body's Qi is deficient, it lacks the strength to keep Yang energy anchored inside. Exertion drains Qi further, causing Yang to float upward and outward as low-grade fever. At the same time, a weak Spleen fails to transform food and fluids properly, leading to dampness that can smolder into heat.
This combination of floating Yang and damp-heat accumulation makes the fever worse after activity and leaves you feeling exhausted and heavy.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks; thin white coating. Pulse: Empty (Xu) or weak (Ruo), especially at the right Guan position.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Qi is the body's vital energy that maintains warmth and holds Yang in place. Exertion, stress, and inadequate nutrition drain Qi, causing Yang to float upward as low-grade fever. Rest conserves Qi, while warm, nourishing foods support the Spleen in generating new Qi. Gentle movement circulates Qi without exhausting it, and avoiding cold prevents further damage to the Spleen's transformative function. By building and protecting Qi, the body can anchor Yang and keep temperature stable.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and stews with easy-to-digest ingredients such as rice, sweet potato, and lean meats. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion; gentle practices like qigong or walking can help build Qi without depleting it.

### Spleen Qi Sinking
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-sinking)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Bearing-down sensation in the abdomen, Rectal or uterine prolapse, Low-grade fever that worsens with exertion, Fatigue and lack of strength, Poor appetite, Loose stools or diarrhea
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Physical or mental overexertion, Standing for long periods, Heavy lifting, Cold, raw, or greasy foods and iced drinks, Stress and worry, Large meals
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Rest and sleep, Warm drinks and soups, Small, frequent meals, Gentle abdominal massage, Warm compress on abdomen
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  The Spleen holds organs in place and raises clear Yang upward. When Spleen Qi becomes deficient and sinks, the clear Yang fails to ascend, and the resulting Yin Fire (or damp-heat from poor transformation) rises to the surface, creating a low-grade fever. Because the Qi is already weak, any physical exertion further depletes it, making the sinking and the fever more pronounced. This pattern is characterized by a fever that worsens with activity, along with a distinct bearing-down sensation in the abdomen and a tendency toward organ prolapse.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: weak, especially at the right middle position (Guan), may be deep.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Rest and lying down allow the Qi to recover and stop sinking, while warm drinks and foods support Spleen Yang and prevent further Qi depletion. Small meals reduce the digestive burden on a weak Spleen, and abdominal massage or warmth helps Qi ascend. Overexertion and heavy lifting deplete Qi further, causing more sinking and fever. Cold foods damage Spleen Yang, while large meals and emotional stress strain the already weak Spleen, exacerbating the condition.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews, and avoid raw, cold, or heavy foods that burden the Spleen. Small, frequent meals are better than large ones. Rest after eating and avoid lying down immediately, but gentle walking can help Qi move. Avoid heavy lifting and prolonged standing, which worsen the sinking. Manage stress and get adequate sleep to conserve Qi.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### Why does my fever get worse when I’m tired?
In TCM, your vital energy (Qi) is responsible for keeping your body temperature stable. When Qi is weak, it can’t anchor your body’s warmth, which then floats up as a low-grade fever. Exertion drains Qi further, so the fever spikes. Rest allows Qi to recover, so the fever subsides. This pattern is a hallmark of Qi deficiency.

### Can Chinese herbs really cure a low-grade fever?
Rather than ‘cure’ like an antibiotic, TCM herbs aim to correct the underlying imbalance that causes the fever. By strengthening the Spleen and boosting Qi, formulas like Bu Zhong Yi Qi Tang help your body generate enough energy to contain its own heat. Many patients find the fever resolves as their energy returns, often within a few weeks.

### Is it safe to take TCM herbs with my usual medications?
Generally yes, but always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor. Some Qi-tonifying herbs like Huang Qi (Astragalus) are well-tolerated, but if you take blood thinners or immune-suppressing drugs, specific interactions need to be checked. Never stop prescribed medication without medical advice.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
Most patients begin with weekly sessions for 4-6 weeks. As the fever stabilizes, sessions may be spaced to every two weeks, then monthly for maintenance. The total number depends on how deeply the Qi deficiency is rooted, but many notice a difference after 3-5 treatments.

### Will the fever come back after treatment?
If the root deficiency is fully corrected, the fever should not return. However, if you consistently overwork, skip meals, or endure chronic stress, the Qi can become depleted again. TCM aims to build resilience, but maintaining a balanced lifestyle is essential for lasting results.

### What should I eat to help with this kind of fever?
Favour warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and stews. Ingredients like ginger, sweet potato, and small amounts of lean protein support Spleen Qi. Avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that tax digestion. Eat small, frequent meals rather than large ones.

### Can this fever pattern affect children?
Yes, children with weak digestion or after a prolonged illness may develop Qi deficiency fever. TCM treatment for children uses gentler doses and often focuses on diet and pediatric tui na (massage) before herbs. Always consult a pediatric TCM specialist.

### How is this different from a fever caused by an infection?
An infectious fever usually comes with chills, body aches, and a higher temperature, and responds to rest and fluids. Qi deficiency fever is typically low-grade, fluctuates with energy levels, and is accompanied by deep fatigue, pale complexion, and a weak pulse. It does not respond to cooling antipyretics in the same way; it needs Qi tonification.
