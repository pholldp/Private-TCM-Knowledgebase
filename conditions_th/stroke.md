## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนปัจจุบัน (Conventional Medicine View)
### คำอธิบาย (Description)
A stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is suddenly interrupted, either by a clot blocking a vessel (ischemic stroke) or by a ruptured vessel causing bleeding (hemorrhagic stroke). Brain cells deprived of oxygen begin to die within minutes, leading to sudden neurological deficits such as weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking or understanding speech, vision problems, dizziness, and severe headache. Diagnosis is confirmed by brain imaging (CT or MRI), and rapid treatment is critical to minimize permanent damage.

### การรักษาหลัก (Treatments)
Acute ischemic stroke is treated with clot-dissolving medications (tPA) when given within a few hours, or with mechanical clot retrieval. Hemorrhagic stroke may require surgery to relieve pressure and repair vessels. After the acute phase, secondary prevention includes antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel), anticoagulants (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants), blood pressure management, and cholesterol-lowering statins. Rehabilitation - physical, occupational, and speech therapy - is the cornerstone of recovery, aiming to restore lost function and adapt to disabilities.

## มุมมองทางการแพทย์แผนจีน (Traditional Chinese Medicine View)
### ความเข้าใจเกี่ยวกับการแพทย์แผนจีน (TCM Understanding)
In TCM, stroke is called 'Wind-stroke' (中风, zhòng fēng) - an apt name for a condition that strikes suddenly, like a gust of wind, and leaves devastation in its wake. But this 'wind' is not an external weather phenomenon; it is an internal disturbance, born from long-standing imbalances in the body's vital substances. The Liver, which governs the smooth flow of Qi and blood, is most often the source. When Liver Yin is too weak to anchor its Yang, the Yang energy surges upward, generating internal Wind that rushes to the head and disrupts the brain's orifices. This is the classic Liver Yang Rising pattern, often triggered by anger or stress.
But not all strokes are fiery and explosive. The Spleen, responsible for transforming food into usable energy and fluids, can become weakened by a poor diet, overwork, or aging. When it fails, it produces a sticky, turbid substance called Phlegm - not the phlegm you cough up, but a systemic obstruction that can clog the channels. Combined with internal Wind, this Phlegm-Wind pattern causes numbness, heaviness, and slurred speech rather than the red-faced anger of a Liver Yang stroke. If the Phlegm is further heated by rich foods or alcohol, it becomes Phlegm-Heat, which blocks the bowels, causes constipation, and sends toxic heat upward to the brain.
These three patterns - Liver Wind, Wind-Phlegm, and Phlegm-Heat - are the most common acute presentations. They are excess conditions, meaning there is something present that shouldn't be: excessive Yang, Phlegm, or Heat. But stroke can also arise from deficiency, especially in older or frail individuals. When the body's Yin (the cooling, nourishing essence) is severely depleted, it can no longer hold the Yang down, and a slow, smoldering internal Wind stirs. This Empty-Wind pattern often appears during recovery, with tremors, wasting, and a dry, crimson tongue.
Finally, after the acute crisis passes, many patients face a different challenge: Qi Deficiency with Blood Stagnation. The stroke itself depletes the body's Qi, leaving it too weak to move blood through the channels. The result is heavy, weak limbs, fatigue, and a pale-purple tongue. This pattern is the most common during rehabilitation, and TCM's approach shifts from expelling wind to powerfully boosting Qi and invigorating blood to rebuild the damaged pathways.
In every case, the same Western diagnosis of 'stroke' unfolds from a different internal landscape. TCM doesn't just see a clot or a bleed - it sees the unique pattern of wind, phlegm, heat, deficiency, and stasis that led to the crisis and that will shape the recovery. This is why two stroke patients with identical-looking brain scans may receive completely different herbal formulas and acupuncture point prescriptions.

### การวินิจฉัยโดยแพทย์แผนจีน (Practitioner Diagnosis)
A TCM practitioner first looks at how the stroke began and what the person was feeling right before it happened. Sudden collapse with anger or extreme stress points toward Liver Yang rising, while a quieter onset with confusion and heavy limbs suggests Phlegm. The quality of speech, the tongue, and the pulse then narrow the picture down to one of the five core patterns.
When Liver Wind agitates internally because Liver Yang rises violently, the person often falls suddenly, may lose consciousness, and shows marked irritability or a red face. The tongue is red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid. This is an acute, forceful pattern that demands immediate cooling and calming.
Wind-Phlegm presents with numbness, slurred speech, and a sensation of heaviness rather than explosive anger. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. There is little sign of heat, so the practitioner rules out the red tongue and constipation that would point to Phlegm-Heat instead.
Phlegm-Heat adds a strong element of internal heat: foul breath, constipation, and a red tongue with a thick yellow greasy coating. The pulse is rapid and slippery. These digestive signs and the intense yellow coating distinguish it from Wind-Phlegm, where the coating is greasy but not necessarily yellow or accompanied by bowel symptoms.
Empty-Wind agitating in the Interior typically appears in the recovery phase or in older, Yin-deficient people. The key clues are tremor, a dry mouth, a red tongue with little or no coating, and a thready rapid pulse. Unlike the acute patterns, there is no thick coating or constipation, just signs of dryness and internal stirring.
Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation is the hallmark of post-stroke rehabilitation. The limbs feel weak and heavy, fatigue is prominent, and the tongue is pale with purple spots. The pulse is thready and uneven. This pattern lacks the acute Wind or Heat signs and instead shows a slow, stagnant quality that requires gentle building and moving.

## กลุ่มอาการการแพทย์แผนจีนและการรักษา (TCM Patterns and Treatment)

### Liver Wind agitating Internally due to Liver Yang Rising
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-wind-agitating-internally-due-to-liver-yang-rising)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Severe dizziness with a sensation of falling, Throbbing or distending headache, Tremor of hands or head, Stiff neck, Flushed face and red eyes, Irritability and easy anger
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Anger or frustration, Alcohol or spicy food, Overwork or lack of sleep, Hot weather or saunas, Sudden loud noises
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Lying down in a dark quiet room, Cool compresses on forehead, Deep breathing and meditation, Eating pears or celery, Gentle neck stretches
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  When Liver and Kidney Yin are too weak to anchor the body's Yang, Liver Yang surges upward like an unmoored flame. This upward rush generates internal Wind, which travels the channels and strikes the brain, causing sudden dizziness, tremor, and even collapse.
The Wind disrupts the flow of Qi and blood in the head, producing throbbing pain, stiff neck, red eyes, and slurred speech. The more Yin is depleted, the more violently Yang ascends, making this a classic acute stroke pattern.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red body, stiff or trembling, possibly deviated, with a thin yellow or scanty coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), fine (细 xì), and often rapid (数 shuò).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Anger, stress, alcohol, and heat all surge Liver Yang upward, which is exactly the mechanism that generates Wind and threatens the brain. Rest, darkness, and cooling foods anchor Yang and preserve Yin, while gentle movement helps Qi flow without provoking the upward rush. Calming the mind and cooling the body are direct ways to extinguish the internal Wind.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Avoid alcohol, spicy foods, and excessive caffeine, all of which fan Liver Yang. Eat cooling, Yin-nourishing foods like pears, celery, seaweed, black sesame, and chrysanthemum tea. Prioritize rest and keep a regular sleep schedule; overwork depletes Yin and fuels the Wind.

### Wind-Phlegm
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-phlegm)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Sudden numbness or tingling in the limbs, Slurred or difficult speech, Copious white, frothy sputum, Heavy sensation in the body or limbs, Chest stuffiness or fullness, Dizziness with a heavy or distending feeling
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Stress and frustration, Cold, damp environment or weather, Greasy, fatty, or heavy foods, Overwork, Sedentary lifestyle
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Warm, dry environment, Light, easily digestible meals, Gentle movement, Rest, Ginger tea
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Phlegm is a thick, sticky byproduct that forms when the Spleen fails to transform fluids, often due to poor diet or weak digestion. When internal Wind stirs-commonly from Liver imbalance or emotional stress-it sweeps this Phlegm upward to block the brain's channels and orifices. This sudden obstruction causes the classic stroke symptoms of numbness, slurred speech, and heavy limbs, while the copious white sputum and greasy tongue coating reveal the underlying Phlegm accumulation.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Stress and frustration stir up Liver Qi and provoke internal Wind, which carries Phlegm upward to block the brain's channels. Cold and damp environments promote Phlegm formation by weakening the Spleen's transforming function, as do heavy, greasy, or dairy-rich foods. Rest and a light, warm diet support the Spleen, while gentle movement helps move Qi and dry Dampness, preventing Phlegm from accumulating.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-rich foods that create Dampness and Phlegm. Eat warm, cooked meals like congee with ginger and a little rice. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi helps keep Qi and fluids moving. Keep the neck and shoulders warm, especially in damp weather.

### Phlegm-Heat
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Constipation with foul-smelling stools, Foul breath and a sticky, unpleasant taste, Thick, yellow, greasy tongue coating, Copious yellow, sticky phlegm or sputum, Restlessness, irritability, and a flushed face
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Greasy, fatty, or heavy foods, Alcohol and smoking, Prolonged constipation, Emotional stress and anger
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Light, easily digestible meals, Regular bowel movements, Drinking warm water, Gentle walking
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  Phlegm-Heat forms when rich foods, alcohol, or emotional stress impair digestion, causing dampness and heat to bind together into a thick, sticky, hot obstruction. This turbid Phlegm-Heat blocks the Stomach and Intestines, so the bowels become stuck and constipation sets in. The trapped heat and phlegm then surge upward, clouding the brain's orifices and obstructing the collaterals, which can suddenly trigger a stroke.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: red, swollen body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Greasy, spicy, or heavy foods directly generate dampness and heat, thickening phlegm and worsening the bowel blockage-this is the very root of the pattern. Alcohol and emotional stress flare up internal heat, pushing turbid phlegm upward. Gentle movement and warm water help keep the bowels open and phlegm from accumulating, while light, bland meals starve the phlegm-heat of its fuel.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Focus on light, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of lean protein. Avoid dairy, fried foods, sugar, and alcohol, which create more phlegm and heat. Stay well hydrated with warm water and keep the bowels moving daily-gentle walking and abdominal massage can help.

### Empty-Wind agitating in the Interior
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/empty-wind-agitating-in-the-interior)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Trembling or twitching of hands and feet, Severe fatigue and listlessness, Wasting and emaciation, Dry mouth and throat, Night sweats, Dizziness and blurred vision
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overwork and exhaustion, Emotional stress, Spicy, fried, or drying foods, Hot, dry environment, Excessive sweating
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Adequate rest, Cool, quiet environment, Moistening foods (pears, congee), Gentle stretching, Hydration
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  This pattern arises when long-term Yin deficiency, especially of the Liver and Kidneys, fails to anchor the body's Yang. Without enough Yin to hold it down, Yang floats upward and stirs up internal Wind, which disturbs the brain and sinews. In stroke, this often appears during recovery or in older individuals, causing lingering tremors, muscle weakness, and wasting rather than a sudden collapse.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: crimson, thin, dry, cracked, with little or no coating (mirror-like) and may tremble. Pulse: fine and rapid, possibly wiry at the left Guan position, but weak upon deeper pressure.
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Overwork and emotional stress consume Yin and stir up Yang, which generates more internal Wind and worsens tremors. Rest and a cool, calm environment allow Yin to recover and anchor Yang. Moistening foods and hydration replenish depleted fluids, while spicy and drying foods further deplete Yin, aggravating the condition.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Prioritize rest and avoid overwork, which depletes Yin. Eat moistening foods such as pears, black sesame, spinach, and congee, and avoid spicy, fried, or drying foods. Gentle activities like Tai Chi can help, but avoid excessive sweating. Keep the living environment cool and humidified.

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*ลิงก์รายละเอียดกลุ่มอาการ: [หน้าหลักข้อมูลกลุ่มอาการ (ภาษาอังกฤษ)](https://www.meandqi.com/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **สัญญาณและอาการทางคลินิก (Clinical Signs)**: Fixed stabbing pain in the affected limbs, Persistent limb weakness and heaviness, Dark or purplish complexion, Fatigue that worsens even with mild activity, Shortness of breath and reluctance to speak
- **สิ่งกระตุ้นให้อาการแย่ลง (Worse with)**: Overexertion and prolonged standing, Cold or raw foods, Emotional stress and worry, Cold, damp environment or weather
- **สิ่งช่วยให้อาการดีขึ้น (Better with)**: Gentle, consistent movement, Warm, easily digested meals, Adequate rest between activities
- **กลไกการเกิดอาการ (Why this happens)**:
  After a stroke, the body's Qi is often severely depleted. Qi is the force that moves blood through the vessels, so when it is weak, the circulation slows and blood stagnates. This stagnation clogs the channels that nourish the limbs, leaving them weak, numb, and heavy, while the underlying Qi deficiency causes deep fatigue and breathlessness.
- **ลักษณะลิ้นและชีพจร (Tongue & Pulse)**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body with possible stasis spots, teeth marks, and thin white coat. Pulse: deep, fine or weak, and choppy (涩, sè).
- **อธิบายสิ่งกระตุ้นและการทุเลาอาการ (Triggers & Relief Explanation)**: Qi deficiency means the body's engine is weak, so overexertion quickly drains it further, worsening fatigue and stagnation. Cold and raw foods chill the Spleen and congeal blood, making stasis denser. Gentle movement, on the other hand, coaxes Qi to flow without exhausting it, while warm, nourishing foods supply the fuel needed to push blood through the vessels. Rest allows the body to rebuild its Qi reserves, preventing the breathlessness and heaviness that come with depletion.
- **คำแนะนำด้านอาหารและการดำเนินชีวิต (Diet & Lifestyle)**: Eat warm, easily digested foods that gently build Qi, like congee with a little lean meat, stewed dark leafy greens, and a few goji berries. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that further weaken digestion and congeal blood. Gentle daily movement such as supported walking or chair-based exercises keeps blood moving without exhausting you, but stop before you feel breathless.

## คำถามที่พบบ่อย (Frequently Asked Questions)
### Can acupuncture help after a stroke?
Yes, acupuncture is widely used in post-stroke rehabilitation to improve motor function, reduce spasticity, and aid speech recovery. It works by stimulating specific points that reawaken the brain's connection to the limbs and clear the channels of obstruction. Most patients begin acupuncture as soon as they are medically stable, often while still in the hospital. Research and clinical experience suggest that regular sessions over several months can enhance the gains from physical and occupational therapy.

### How soon after a stroke should I start TCM?
TCM can be started very early, often within days of the stroke once the patient is stable. In many Chinese hospitals, acupuncture and herbal medicine are integrated into acute stroke care alongside conventional treatments. The key is to begin as soon as possible to support the brain's natural plasticity and prevent complications like muscle contractures. Your TCM practitioner will coordinate with your medical team to ensure safety, especially if you are on blood-thinning medications.

### Can TCM prevent a second stroke?
Yes, TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalances - such as Liver Yang rising, Phlegm accumulation, or Qi deficiency - that contributed to the first stroke. By using herbs, acupuncture, dietary changes, and lifestyle guidance, TCM helps stabilize blood pressure, reduce phlegm, and strengthen the body's constitution. This holistic approach can lower the risk of recurrence, but it should always be combined with conventional preventive medications as prescribed by your neurologist.

### Will TCM interact with my blood thinners?
Yes, there is a potential interaction. Many Chinese herbs commonly used for stroke recovery, such as Dang Gui (Angelica sinensis), Chuan Xiong (Ligusticum wallichii), and Dan Shen (Salvia miltiorrhiza), have blood-moving properties that can enhance the effect of anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs like warfarin, aspirin, or clopidogrel, increasing bleeding risk. It is essential that both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor know all medications and herbs you are taking. A qualified TCM practitioner will adjust the formula and dosage accordingly, and may monitor your clotting times more frequently.

### What dietary changes can help stroke recovery?
A diet that supports the Spleen and reduces phlegm is crucial. Favor warm, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid greasy, fried, and excessively sweet foods that create dampness and phlegm. If you have heat signs (red tongue, constipation), add cooling foods like celery, mung beans, and pears. If you have cold or deficiency signs (pale tongue, fatigue), warming foods like ginger, cinnamon, and bone broths are beneficial. Limit alcohol and smoking, which generate internal heat and damage vessels.

### Does TCM work for speech problems after a stroke?
Yes, acupuncture can be particularly helpful for aphasia and dysarthria. Points on the scalp (speech area), as well as local points like Lianquan (REN-23), Jinjin, and Yuye, are commonly used to stimulate the tongue and throat. Combined with speech therapy, acupuncture may help improve articulation and language function by enhancing blood flow to the affected brain regions and reconnecting neural pathways.

### Is TCM safe for elderly stroke patients?
Yes, TCM is generally very safe for elderly patients when administered by a qualified practitioner. In fact, because TCM treatments are tailored to the individual's constitution, they can be especially gentle for frail patients. Herbal formulas can be adjusted to be milder, and acupuncture techniques can be modified to use fewer needles or lighter stimulation. However, elderly patients are often on multiple medications, so careful coordination with their medical team is essential to avoid interactions.
