## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted or a blood vessel bursts, depriving brain tissue of oxygen. The two main types are ischemic (clot) and hemorrhagic (bleed). Typical symptoms include sudden numbness or weakness on one side of the body, confusion, trouble speaking or understanding, vision problems, dizziness, and severe headache. Diagnosis is usually confirmed with CT scans or MRI, and the immediate priority is restoring blood flow or controlling bleeding.

### Treatments
Acute ischemic stroke is treated with clot-dissolving drugs (tPA) or mechanical thrombectomy to remove the clot. Hemorrhagic stroke may require surgery to repair the vessel and relieve pressure. After stabilization, treatment shifts to rehabilitation - physical, occupational, and speech therapy - and long-term medications to manage risk factors: antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs), statins, and blood pressure drugs.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, stroke is understood as a sudden surge of internal Wind that disrupts the brain's channels. This Wind rarely arises alone - it is usually stirred up by an underlying imbalance.
The Liver, which governs the smooth flow of Qi and Blood, is the organ most closely associated with Wind. When Liver Yang rises uncontrollably (often due to Kidney Yin deficiency failing to anchor it), it can generate fierce internal Wind that shoots upward, causing the classic sudden collapse, paralysis, and loss of consciousness.
The Spleen also plays a key role. If it is weakened by poor diet or overwork, it fails to transform fluids properly, leading to the accumulation of Phlegm. This sticky substance can combine with Wind and clog the brain's channels, resulting in numbness, slurred speech, and a heavy sensation. In other cases, Phlegm can combine with Heat, creating a hot, obstructive mixture that clouds the mind and leads to a more agitated, constipated presentation.
During the recovery phase, the body's Qi is often severely depleted. When Qi is too weak to move Blood, stagnation develops, blocking the collaterals and preventing full neurological recovery. This explains the persistent weakness, fatigue, and a purplish tongue seen in many stroke survivors. TCM therefore does not see stroke as a single event but as a dynamic process - the acute phase is dominated by excess patterns (Wind, Fire, Phlegm), while the chronic phase shifts to deficiency patterns (Qi and Yin deficiency, Blood Stasis).

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When a person shows signs of a stroke (中风, zhòng fēng), a TCM practitioner first asks about the suddenness of onset, the specific sensations, and any emotional or dietary triggers. The tongue body, coating, and pulse quality are then used to confirm which internal imbalance is driving the symptoms.
If the collapse was dramatic, with a red face, irritability, a bitter taste in the mouth, and constipation, the pattern is Liver Wind agitating Internally due to Liver Yang Rising. The tongue is red with a yellow greasy coating and the pulse feels wiry and rapid, pointing to a surging of Yang that stirs internal wind.
When the onset is abrupt but the main features are numbness, a deviated mouth, slurred speech, and a sensation of phlegm in the throat, Wind-Phlegm obstructing the collaterals is likely. The tongue coating is greasy (often white or yellow) and the pulse is slippery and wiry, showing that wind and phlegm are tangled together.
In cases where foul breath, chest oppression, and severe constipation accompany the stroke symptoms, Phlegm-Heat has accumulated in the interior. The tongue appears red with a thick, yellow, greasy coating, and the pulse is rapid and slippery, signaling that heat has bound with phlegm and needs to be drained.
If the person is in the recovery phase or has a history of Yin deficiency, you might see Empty-Wind agitating in the Interior. This presents with tremor, dizziness, dry mouth, and a red tongue with little coating. The pulse is fine and rapid, reflecting Yin failing to anchor Yang, allowing empty wind to stir.
In the subacute or recovery stage, fatigue and weak limbs often dominate, along with a pale or purplish tongue and a thready, hesitant pulse. This is Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation, where the body lacks the energy to move blood properly, so neurological recovery is slow unless Qi is boosted.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Wind agitating Internally due to Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-wind-agitating-internally-due-to-liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Severe dizziness, as if about to fall, Stiff, trembling, or deviated tongue, Throbbing or distending headache, Bitter taste in the mouth, Irritability and easy anger before the stroke, Flushed face and red eyes, Constipation
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress and frustration, Alcohol, spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Hot weather or stuffy environment, Overexertion and lack of sleep
- **Better with**: Rest and quiet environment, Cool compresses on the head, Light, bland meals, Gentle breathing and meditation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Years of stress, overwork, or aging can wear down the Kidney and Liver Yin - the body's cooling, anchoring force. When Yin is too weak to hold Yang down, Liver Yang surges upward like a flame, generating internal Wind. This Wind suddenly rushes to the head, agitating the brain and channels, causing the collapse, loss of consciousness, and one-sided paralysis of a stroke.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, stiff or trembling, may deviate to one side, with a thin yellow or scanty coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), fine (细 xì), and often rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Anger and frustration surge Liver Qi upward, which directly feeds the rising Yang and internal Wind at the heart of this pattern. Alcohol, spicy food, and hot weather add heat to an already overheated system, while overwork depletes Yin further. Rest, cool compresses, and calming practices help anchor Yang and allow Yin to recover, breaking the cycle that leads to a stroke.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid alcohol, coffee, and spicy or greasy foods that add heat. Eat cooling, yin-nourishing foods like celery, spinach, cucumber, and chrysanthemum tea. Prioritize rest and keep stress low - gentle tai chi or meditation helps anchor Yang without overstimulating.

### Wind-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, Slurred or difficult speech, Chest stuffiness or feeling of fullness, Copious white or frothy sputum, Heaviness in the body or limbs, Dizziness or vertigo with a heavy sensation
- **Worse with**: Alcohol, spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Damp weather, Sedentary lifestyle, Emotional stress and frustration, Cold, raw foods
- **Better with**: Light, warm meals, Gentle exercise or movement, Dry, airy environment, Keeping the head warm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In Wind-Phlegm pattern, a weakened Spleen fails to manage fluids, so dampness accumulates and congeals into phlegm. At the same time, an internal stirring of wind - often triggered by emotional upset or rising Liver Yang - sweeps that phlegm upward into the head. When phlegm and wind together clog the brain's channels, the smooth flow of Qi and blood is suddenly interrupted, producing numbness, slurred speech, and facial drooping.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, may be swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: wiry and slippery (弦滑, xián huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Phlegm arises when the Spleen fails to transform fluids, creating dampness and phlegm, and Liver wind stirs it upward. Greasy, damp-producing foods and a sedentary lifestyle worsen phlegm; stress and frustration aggravate wind. Light, warm meals and gentle movement support Spleen Qi and help resolve phlegm, while protecting from cold and damp prevents external wind from combining with internal phlegm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, light, easily digestible foods such as congee and steamed vegetables. Avoid dairy, greasy, fried, and sugary foods that create dampness and phlegm. Gentle daily movement, like walking, helps circulate Qi and resolve phlegm. Keep the neck and head protected from cold wind and dampness.

### Phlegm-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Coughing up thick, sticky, yellow phlegm, Constipation with foul breath, Feeling of fullness or oppression in the chest, Restlessness and irritability, Flushed red face
- **Worse with**: Alcohol, spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Emotional stress and frustration, Hot weather or stuffy environment
- **Better with**: Cool, well-ventilated room, Light, bland meals, Rest and quiet environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When diet, stress, or illness create an accumulation of Phlegm and Heat in the body, this sticky, hot obstruction can suddenly block the channels that supply the brain. The Phlegm-Heat rises upward, clouding the mind and causing loss of consciousness, slurred speech, or paralysis. In the acute phase, the Heat often dries the bowels, leading to constipation and foul breath, while a thick, yellow, greasy tongue coating reveals the internal struggle.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, swollen, with a thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy and greasy foods directly generate Phlegm and Heat, thickening the sticky obstruction that blocks the brain's channels. Emotional stress stirs up internal Heat, worsening restlessness. A cool environment, light meals, and rest help clear Heat and allow the body's Qi to resolve Phlegm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods that create more Phlegm and Heat. Eat light, easily digestible meals like congee and steamed vegetables. Stay hydrated with warm water, and strictly avoid alcohol and smoking.

### Empty-Wind agitating in the Interior
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/empty-wind-agitating-in-the-interior)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Trembling or twitching of the hands and feet, Deep red (crimson) tongue with little or no coating, Fine, rapid pulse that lacks force, Severe fatigue and listlessness
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and lack of sleep, Alcohol, spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Hot weather or stuffy environment
- **Better with**: Rest and quiet environment, Cool, nourishing foods, Gentle exercise or movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body's Yin is deeply depleted, the Liver and Kidneys lose their ability to anchor Yang. This creates a vacuum that stirs internal Wind, leading to trembling, dizziness, and in some cases, a stroke. It often appears in the recovery stage or in people with long-standing Yin deficiency.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Crimson, thin, dry, cracked, little or no coating (mirror-like). Pulse: Fine (Xi), rapid (Shu), wiry at left Guan, lacks force.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin deficiency is like a low reservoir of cooling fluids; overwork, stress, and spicy foods further consume Yin and stir Wind, while rest and moistening foods replenish Yin and calm the internal Wind.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat moistening, Yin-nourishing foods like pear, lily bulb, black sesame, and congee. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods, as well as alcohol and coffee. Prioritize rest and gentle movement like Tai Chi to conserve Yin.

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Extreme fatigue even after mild activity, Fixed stabbing pain in the chest or affected limbs, Pale or dusky complexion with purplish spots
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and lack of sleep, Emotional stress and frustration, Cold, raw foods, Cold weather
- **Better with**: Rest and gentle movement, Warm cooked meals, Acupuncture and moxibustion, Consistent sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After a stroke, the body's Qi is often severely depleted. When Qi is too weak to propel blood, circulation stagnates, forming blood stasis that blocks the brain's collaterals. This stagnation prevents the full restoration of neurological function, leaving persistent weakness, numbness, and fatigue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body with stasis spots, teeth marks, thin white coat. Pulse: deep, fine, and choppy (涩 sè).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi deficiency is the root of this pattern, so anything that depletes Qi-overexertion, stress, poor diet-worsens the condition. Rest and nourishing foods rebuild Qi, enabling blood to move and stasis to resolve. Gentle movement and warmth encourage circulation, while cold and inactivity cause blood to congeal, aggravating stasis.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like congee, soups with astragalus root (Huang Qi), chicken, and yam to build Qi. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that weaken digestion. Gentle daily exercise like Tai Chi or walking helps move Qi and blood without depleting energy. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help after a stroke?
Yes. Acupuncture is widely used in post-stroke rehabilitation to improve motor function, reduce spasticity, and support speech recovery. Points are selected to unblock the channels, move Blood, and calm internal Wind. Many patients notice subtle improvements within 4-6 weeks of weekly sessions, with gains accumulating over months alongside physical therapy.

### How soon after a stroke can I start TCM treatment?
As soon as you are medically stable and discharged from acute care, or even while still hospitalized with the approval of your medical team. In China, acupuncture often begins within days of a stroke. The earlier the intervention, the better the potential for channel reconnection, but TCM can still offer significant benefits even years after the event.

### Are Chinese herbs safe to take with blood thinners like warfarin or aspirin?
Many herbs used for stroke, such as Dang Gui (Angelica sinensis) and Chuan Xiong (Ligusticum wallichii), have mild blood-moving properties that could theoretically enhance the effect of anticoagulants or antiplatelets. It is essential to inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor about all medications and herbs. A qualified herbalist will adjust the formula and monitor you closely; never self-prescribe.

### What dietary changes can support my recovery?
In general, avoid greasy, fried, or overly spicy foods that create Phlegm and Heat. Focus on warm, cooked meals that are easy to digest - soups, congees, steamed vegetables, and lean proteins. Foods like oats, yam, and small amounts of red dates can gently strengthen Qi and Blood. Alcohol and smoking should be strictly avoided as they stir Wind and damage the vessels.

### Does TCM help with post-stroke depression or fatigue?
Absolutely. Post-stroke depression and crushing fatigue are often signs of Qi and Blood deficiency or lingering Phlegm misting the mind. Herbal formulas like Bu Yang Huan Wu Tang can be modified to lift the spirit, and acupuncture points such as Baihui (DU-20) and Zusanli (ST-36) are used to boost energy and mood. Many patients report feeling more hopeful and less exhausted within a month of treatment.

### Will I need to stop my regular medications?
No. TCM is designed to complement, not replace, your conventional stroke prevention medications. Never discontinue antiplatelets, anticoagulants, or blood pressure drugs on your own. Work with your doctors to monitor your condition; if your health improves, they may adjust dosages over time.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for stroke rehabilitation**
   *Journal/Publication: Yang A, Wu HM, Tang JL, Xu L, Yang M, Liu GJ. Acupuncture for stroke rehabilitation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2016, Issue 8. Art. No.: CD004131.*
   Cochrane systematic review including 31 trials (2257 participants). Acupuncture plus conventional rehabilitation was superior to conventional rehabilitation alone in improving neurological deficit, motor function, and overall disability at the end of treatment and at follow‑up.

2. **Efficacy and safety of Buyang Huanwu Decoction for ischemic stroke: a systematic review and meta‑analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Wei RL, Teng HJ, Yin B, Xu Y, Du YH, He FP, et al. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine. 2017;2017:8423827.*
   Meta‑analysis of 19 RCTs (1806 patients). Buyang Huanwu Tang significantly improved neurological deficit scores and activities of daily living compared with Western medicine alone, with a favorable safety profile.
