## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Esophagitis is inflammation of the esophagus, the tube that carries food from your mouth to your stomach. It most commonly results from stomach acid flowing back up (gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD), but can also be caused by infections, medications that irritate the lining, or allergies. Typical symptoms include heartburn, chest pain, difficulty swallowing, and a sensation of food getting stuck. Diagnosis is usually confirmed with an upper endoscopy, where a camera examines the esophageal lining.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment focuses on reducing stomach acid and protecting the esophagus. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) like omeprazole are the mainstay, often combined with antacids or H2 blockers for quick relief. Lifestyle changes - elevating the head of the bed, avoiding trigger foods, losing weight - are also recommended. In severe cases, surgery to reinforce the lower esophageal sphincter may be considered.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the esophagus is seen as the passageway through which the Stomach receives food and descends Qi. The Stomach is meant to send its contents downward - when this downward movement is disrupted, Qi rebels upward, carrying acid and food back into the esophagus. This 'rebellious Stomach Qi' is the central mechanism behind esophagitis, but what causes it to rebel varies from person to person.
The Liver is often the culprit. When stress, frustration, or anger are held in, the Liver's Qi stagnates, creating pressure that attacks the Stomach. This is why many people notice their reflux flares during tense times. If stagnation persists, it can generate Heat, adding a burning quality to the pain. So a stressful day can lead to a fiery, bitter-tasting reflux - a pattern TCM calls Liver Qi Stagnation transforming into Heat.
Sometimes the root is weakness, not excess. A weak Spleen - the organ system that transforms food into energy - fails to manage fluids, allowing dampness and phlegm to accumulate. This sticky obstruction blocks the Stomach's descent, causing a sensation of a lump in the throat, chest oppression, and fatigue. This pattern often appears in people with poor digestion and a heavy, sluggish feeling.
Other patterns include a simple Qi-Phlegm knot where emotional stress congeals fluids into a lump sensation, and Stomach Yin Deficiency, where chronic dryness and lack of fluids create a subtle, burning pain without much acid. Because the root cause can be so different, the same Western diagnosis of esophagitis can require opposite treatments in TCM - cooling for Heat, warming and drying for Dampness, or moistening for Yin Deficiency.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner starts by asking what the discomfort feels like-burning, pressure, or a lump-and when it occurs. The quality of the sensation, along with emotional and dietary triggers, is the first clue that separates one pattern from another.
If the main complaint is a burning pain behind the breastbone that flares with anger or frustration, and you feel irritable and restless, the pattern is likely Liver Qi Stagnation that has turned into Heat. The tongue is red with a yellow coating and the pulse is wiry and rapid, confirming Heat rising from the Liver to attack the Stomach.
When belching, acid regurgitation, and a distended upper abdomen dominate after stress, but without strong burning, the picture shifts to Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. Here the tongue is pale red with a thin white coating and the pulse is wiry but not rapid-signs that Qi is stuck rather than heated.
If you experience chest oppression, nausea, a heavy sensation, fatigue, and loose stools, the root is a weak Spleen that fails to manage fluids, creating Dampness. The tongue is pale and swollen with a greasy white coat, and the pulse is deep and weak. This Spleen Deficiency with Dampness often coexists with phlegm patterns.
A distinctive feeling of a lump in the throat, difficulty swallowing, and frequent belching points to Qi-Phlegm, where stagnant Qi and phlegm bind in the esophagus. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is wiry and slippery. Stress and worry often worsen this sensation, which may come and go.
When heartburn and regurgitation are the main issues, accompanied by poor appetite and fatigue with a normal-colored tongue and a wiry, possibly slippery pulse, the core problem is Rebellious Stomach Qi. This usually stems from an underlying Spleen deficiency that fails to keep Stomach Qi moving downward, so it rebels upward.
In long-standing cases, a dry, burning pain that is worse at night, along with dry mouth and thirst, suggests Stomach Yin Deficiency. The tongue is red, dry, and may have cracks or no coating at all; the pulse is thin and rapid. This indicates the stomach’s nourishing fluids are depleted, leaving the tissues irritated and inflamed.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation that transforms into Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-that-transforms-into-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning chest pain (heartburn), Acid reflux or sour belching, Bitter taste in the mouth, Irritability and explosive anger, Distending pain in the ribcage
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol, Overwork or lack of sleep, Hot weather
- **Better with**: Deep breathing, Cool drinks, Gentle walking or exercise, Chrysanthemum tea, Avoiding alcohol
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress and frustration are held in, the Liver's Qi becomes stuck. Over time, this stagnation generates Heat, which rises and attacks the Stomach. The Stomach's normal downward movement is reversed, pushing acid and bile up into the esophagus. This causes the burning pain, sour regurgitation, and bitter taste typical of esophagitis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, especially redder at the sides, with a yellow coating. Pulse: Wiry and rapid (xian shu), often forceful.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger are the primary cause of Liver Qi stagnation; they directly fuel the Heat that drives acid upward. Spicy, greasy foods and alcohol create more Heat and Dampness in the body, aggravating the Stomach. Cooling foods and calming activities soothe the Liver and clear Heat, allowing the Stomach to resume its normal downward movement. Gentle movement helps Qi flow smoothly, preventing stagnation from building up again.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat cooling, easy-to-digest foods like cucumber, celery, and pear. Avoid spicy, greasy, or fried foods, and limit alcohol and coffee. Practice stress-reducing activities like tai chi, meditation, or deep breathing. Maintain regular meal times and avoid eating late at night.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain in the upper abdomen that radiates to the ribs, Frequent belching and acid reflux, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress, Irritability and frequent sighing, Sensation of a lump or blockage in the throat
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Large or heavy meals, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Eating late at night, Sedentary lifestyle with bottled-up emotions
- **Better with**: Stress management, Small, frequent meals, Gentle walking or exercise, Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress, frustration, or emotional upset becomes chronic, the Liver’s natural function of keeping Qi flowing smoothly gets stuck. This stagnant Liver Qi then attacks the Stomach, disrupting its downward movement. Instead of descending, Stomach Qi rebels upward, carrying acid and food back into the esophagus and causing belching, acid reflux, and a feeling of fullness or distension in the upper abdomen.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Normal or slightly red body, may have redder sides, thin white coat. Pulse: Wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly stagnates Liver Qi, which then attacks the Stomach and causes Qi to rebel upward as acid reflux and belching. Soothing the mind and managing stress therefore calms the Liver and protects the Stomach. Heavy, greasy, or spicy foods burden the Spleen and Stomach, creating more stagnation and heat that worsen reflux, while warm, cooked foods are gentle on digestion and support the Stomach’s natural downward movement. Gentle movement helps unblock stagnant Qi, preventing it from building up and flaring into symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals of warm, cooked foods and avoid eating within three hours of bedtime. Minimize spicy, greasy, and fried foods that can aggravate reflux. Manage stress with gentle exercise like walking, Tai Chi, or meditation, as emotional tension directly fuels this pattern.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of a lump in the throat (globus), Chest oppression and fullness, Greasy or sticky sensation in the mouth, Loose stools or diarrhoea, Heaviness in the body and limbs, Poor appetite and bloating after eating
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Dairy products, Overeating, Damp, humid weather, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods, Ginger tea, Light, easily digestible foods, Rest after meals, Gentle walking or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it struggles to transform and transport fluids, so dampness builds up in the middle burner like a slow, sticky swamp. This dampness blocks the Stomach's natural downward movement, and the trapped Qi rebels upward into the esophagus. The result is a heavy, oppressive sensation in the chest, acid reflux, and the classic feeling of a lump in the throat, all layered over the Spleen deficiency signs of bloating and loose stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: Soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), may be slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods damage the Spleen's Yang and promote dampness, while greasy foods overwhelm its ability to transform and transport, worsening the obstruction. Warm, cooked meals and ginger support Spleen Yang and help it digest properly, preventing the accumulation of dampness. Gentle exercise moves Qi and helps the body metabolize fluids, reducing the heavy, stuck feeling.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and congees to support Spleen digestion. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that generate dampness. Sip ginger tea to warm the middle burner. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow and dries dampness.

### Qi-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of a lump in the throat that cannot be swallowed, Chest stuffiness and oppression, Frequent belching or sighing, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress, Moodiness or irritability
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Dairy products, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overeating
- **Better with**: Light, easily digestible foods, Stress management, Gentle walking or exercise, Sipping warm water, Deep breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi stagnates - often from emotional stress or frustration - the body's fluid metabolism slows, and fluids condense into Phlegm. The stuck Qi and the sticky Phlegm bind together and lodge in the esophagus, creating a persistent sensation of a lump or obstruction.
This Phlegm-Qi knot disrupts the normal downward movement of the Stomach and esophagus, leading to chest stuffiness, frequent throat clearing, and a feeling that something is stuck but cannot be swallowed or coughed up. It often comes with belching and nausea as the body tries to relieve the pressure.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Normal-coloured body, possibly slightly swollen, with a white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress is the main trigger because it directly causes Qi to stagnate, which is the first step in forming the Phlegm-Qi knot. Greasy, cold, and dairy foods tax the Spleen and create Dampness and Phlegm, adding more obstruction. Warm, light meals and gentle movement help Qi flow and support the Spleen, while stress reduction prevents the cycle from restarting.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods as well as dairy, which all encourage Phlegm formation. Favor warm, cooked meals with mild spices like ginger and cardamom to support digestion. Eat smaller, more frequent meals and chew thoroughly.
Manage stress with gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi, and practice deep breathing or meditation to keep Qi flowing smoothly.

### Rebellious Stomach Qi
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/rebellious-stomach-qi)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Belching that is difficult to control, Hiccups, Sensation of Qi rising from the stomach to the throat, Acid regurgitation after eating, Nausea, Feeling of fullness or blockage in the upper stomach area
- **Worse with**: Large or heavy meals, Lying down after eating, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Sitting upright after meals, Gentle walking or exercise, Warm, bland foods, Deep breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Stomach is designed to send Qi downward, not upward. When the Spleen is weak or when stress tightens the Liver, the Stomach's downward movement fails and Qi rebels upward instead. This counterflow directly pushes acid and food back into the esophagus, creating the burning sensation and sour taste of reflux.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body color with a white coating (may become yellow if Heat is present). Pulse: wiry and possibly slippery, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Lying down removes gravity's help, making it easier for rebellious Qi to surge upward. Large meals overburden the Stomach, overwhelming its ability to keep Qi down. Spicy and greasy foods stir up Heat and Dampness, which push Qi the wrong way. Stress tightens the Liver, and a tight Liver often invades the Stomach, forcing Qi upward. Sitting upright, gentle walking, and deep breathing all encourage Qi to flow downward, which is the natural direction for the Stomach.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and chew thoroughly. Avoid lying down for at least two hours after eating. Gentle walking after meals helps Qi descend. Limit spicy, greasy, and very cold foods, which can aggravate the upward rush.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning pain in chest and throat, worse at night, Dry mouth and throat, Thirst with a preference for small sips, Red, dry tongue with little or no coating, Sensation of hunger but no desire to eat, Dry stools
- **Worse with**: Spicy, fried, or dry foods, Eating late at night, Emotional stress, Overwork or lack of sleep, Hot weather
- **Better with**: Cool, moist foods like pear or congee, Sipping warm water, Small, frequent meals, Rest and early bedtime, Gentle walking or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Stomach Yin is depleted, the stomach loses its natural moisture and cooling. This generates a subtle 'empty heat' that rises up into the esophagus, causing a dry, burning sensation rather than heavy acid reflux. The lack of fluids also means the esophageal lining is poorly lubricated, making swallowing uncomfortable and pain worse at night when Yin energy is lowest.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, dry, especially in the centre, with little or no coating and possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò), especially weak at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin deficiency means the body lacks the cooling, moistening fluids needed to protect the esophageal lining. Spicy or fried foods are heating and drying, which makes the burning worse. Eating late at night forces the Stomach to work when it should be resting, further depleting Yin. Emotional stress and overwork consume Yin energy. Conversely, moist, cooling foods replenish fluids, and rest allows the body to rebuild its Yin reserves, which is why these measures bring relief.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on moist, cooling foods like pear, apple, congee, tofu, and steamed vegetables. Avoid spicy, fried, or dry foods that further deplete Yin. Eat smaller, more frequent meals and avoid eating late at night. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork; going to bed early helps the body conserve Yin fluids.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help with acid reflux?
Yes. Acupuncture works by redirecting the rebellious Stomach Qi downward and calming the Liver when stress is a trigger. Points on the abdomen, legs, and wrists are selected to restore the natural downward flow. Many patients feel a noticeable easing of heartburn and regurgitation after a few sessions, especially when combined with herbal medicine.

### What does TCM treatment involve - just herbs or needles too?
Treatment usually combines both. You'll typically have weekly acupuncture sessions and take a personalized herbal formula daily, often as a granule dissolved in warm water. The acupuncture addresses the energetic imbalance in the moment, while the herbs work more deeply to correct the underlying pattern over time.

### Will I have to change my diet completely?
You won't need a drastic overhaul, but some adjustments are essential for healing. TCM emphasizes eating warm, cooked foods and avoiding raw, cold, greasy, and spicy items that burden digestion. Small, frequent meals and not lying down after eating are simple but powerful changes that support your treatment.

### How long until I feel better?
Many people notice their symptoms become less intense within the first two weeks. Excess patterns, like those driven by stress and heat, often respond faster - sometimes in 2-4 weeks. Deficiency patterns, where the body's reserves need rebuilding, can take 3-6 months for lasting improvement, though you'll likely feel gradual progress along the way.

### Can I use TCM alongside my PPI medication?
Yes, TCM can safely complement acid-suppressing drugs. Herbs and acupuncture may eventually reduce your need for medication, but never stop prescribed drugs abruptly - work with your doctor to taper if symptoms improve. Always inform your TCM practitioner about all medications you're taking, especially blood thinners, as some herbs can interact.

### Is esophagitis in TCM linked to emotions?
Absolutely. The Liver is strongly affected by stress, frustration, and anger. When emotions are bottled up, the Liver Qi stagnates and can attack the Stomach, causing reflux. This is why many people experience worse symptoms during tense periods. Treatment often includes calming the mind and smoothing the Liver's energy.

### What if I have both burning and a lump feeling?
Mixed patterns are common, especially in chronic conditions. You might have Liver Heat causing burning plus Spleen Dampness creating a lump sensation. A skilled TCM practitioner will diagnose the dominant pattern and adjust your formula to address both aspects, often starting with the most acute symptom and then treating the root.

### Are there any side effects from the herbs?
TCM herbal formulas are generally well tolerated when prescribed by a qualified practitioner who matches the formula to your pattern. Occasionally, a formula may cause mild digestive changes as your body adjusts, but this is usually temporary. If you experience any discomfort, your practitioner can modify the prescription. Serious side effects are rare when herbs are used correctly.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for gastroesophageal reflux disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhu J, et al. Acupuncture for gastroesophageal reflux disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine. 2020.*
   This meta-analysis pooled data from multiple randomized controlled trials and found that acupuncture significantly reduced heartburn and regurgitation scores compared to sham acupuncture or standard medication. The effect was particularly notable for improving quality of life and reducing relapse rates.

2. **Efficacy of modified Ban Xia Hou Pu Tang for non-erosive reflux disease: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li Y, et al. Efficacy of modified Ban Xia Hou Pu Tang for non-erosive reflux disease: a randomized controlled trial. Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2019.*
   In a trial of 120 patients, modified Ban Xia Hou Pu Tang significantly improved symptoms of globus sensation and acid regurgitation compared to omeprazole alone, with fewer side effects. The herbal group also showed greater improvement in the lump-in-throat sensation.
