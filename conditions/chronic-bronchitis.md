## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Chronic bronchitis is a form of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) defined by a persistent, productive cough that lasts for at least three months and recurs for two consecutive years. The bronchi become inflamed and narrowed, leading to excessive mucus production, wheezing, chest discomfort, and shortness of breath. It is most commonly caused by long-term exposure to irritants like cigarette smoke, air pollution, or occupational dust and chemicals. Diagnosis typically involves a clinical history, lung function tests (spirometry), and chest imaging to assess airway damage and rule out other conditions.

### Treatments
Standard treatment includes bronchodilators (inhalers) to open the airways and inhaled corticosteroids to reduce inflammation. During acute flare-ups, oral steroids or antibiotics may be prescribed if bacterial infection is suspected. Pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen therapy, and lifestyle changes - most critically smoking cessation - are cornerstone management strategies. These approaches help control symptoms but do not reverse the underlying, progressive lung damage.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, chronic cough is primarily a disorder of the Lung Organ System. The Lungs are responsible for 'descending and dispersing' Qi - they pull air in and spread it through the body, while also sending Qi and fluids downward. When this descent is disrupted, Qi rebels upward as coughing. But what causes that disruption? That's where TCM begins to think in patterns rather than a single disease.
The most common culprits are Phlegm and deficiency. Phlegm is a pathological thick fluid that accumulates when the Spleen is weak or when fluids are not properly transformed. It can lodge in the Lungs, blocking the airways and prompting a phlegmy cough. Depending on whether the body also has cold or heat, this Phlegm can be either cold-damp (white, watery) or hot-damp (yellow, sticky).
On the other hand, long-term coughing can deplete the Lung Qi itself, making the cough weak and inadequate, or burn the Lung Yin, leading to a dry, irritating cough. In advanced cases, the Kidneys - the 'root of Qi' - become too weak to grasp the inhaled air, causing severe breathlessness.
This is why two people with chronic bronchitis can present so differently: one has a loud, productive cough with thick white sputum and feels worse in damp weather (Damp-Phlegm), while another has a constant dry hack that intensifies at night with a sensation of heat (Lung Yin Deficiency). TCM identifies these as distinct imbalances requiring opposite strategies - expelling phlegm versus nourishing Yin - which would be disastrous to confuse. The diagnostic process uses tongue and pulse to pinpoint which type of cough you have.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by listening to the cough itself and asking about the sputum. A loud, rattling cough with copious white phlegm that feels heavy in the chest points toward Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs. The tongue typically shows a thick, greasy coating, and the pulse feels slippery. This pattern reflects a digestive weakness that allows fluids to congeal into phlegm, which then settles in the Lungs.
If the sputum changes to yellow or green and feels sticky, and the person feels hot or feverish, the picture shifts to Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. This often happens during an acute flare-up. The tongue becomes red with a yellow, greasy coating, and the pulse turns rapid and slippery. The body is fighting an infection, but the phlegm has turned hot, creating a more urgent situation.
A weak, low cough that leaves the person breathless and sweating spontaneously suggests Lung Qi Deficiency. The cough is feeble because the Lungs lack the strength to expel phlegm properly. The tongue looks pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels weak, especially at the Lung position. This often develops after many years of chronic coughing that has gradually depleted the Lung energy.
When the cough is dry and hacking with very little sticky sputum, and the person complains of a dry throat and night sweats, the diagnosis is Lung Yin Deficiency. The tongue appears red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. Chronic inflammation has consumed the cooling, moistening resources of the Lungs, leaving them dry and irritated.
If the cough is accompanied by bloating, a heavy sensation in the body, and a poor appetite, the root may lie in a Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency. The Spleen fails to transform fluids, which then become phlegm that rises to clog the Lungs. The tongue is pale and swollen with tooth marks, and the pulse is weak. Here the practitioner treats both organs together to break the cycle.
In advanced, longstanding cases where the person cannot catch their breath and the cough worsens with any exertion, the Kidneys are failing to receive Qi. The Lungs depend on the Kidneys to anchor the breath downward. A deep, weak pulse and a pale tongue confirm that the root of the problem has moved deeper into the body's constitutional energy reserve.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious white or grey-white sticky phlegm, easy to cough up, Heavy, turbid cough sound, Chest congestion and a heavy, full sensation, Poor appetite, abdominal bloating, loose stools, Fatigue and a heavy, sluggish body feeling, Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and a thick white greasy coating
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Dairy, cheese, and greasy fried foods, Damp, humid weather, Overeating or eating late at night, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals (soups, congee), Ginger tea or ginger in cooking, Gentle walking or light exercise, Dry, well-ventilated environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly. These fluids accumulate into dampness, which then thickens into phlegm. The phlegm settles in the Lungs, clogging the airways and blocking the normal downward movement of Lung Qi. The result is a persistent, phlegmy cough - the hallmark of chronic bronchitis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks; thick white greasy coating that looks sticky. Pulse: slippery (滑, huá), like beads rolling smoothly under the finger.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods damage Spleen Yang, making it even harder to transform fluids - so dampness and phlegm build up. Dairy and greasy foods are inherently phlegm-producing. Warm, cooked meals and ginger gently strengthen the Spleen and help dry internal dampness. Gentle movement moves Qi and resolves dampness, while a dry environment prevents external dampness from adding to the internal burden.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Warm, cooked foods are your allies - think congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Add a little fresh ginger to meals to help transform dampness. Strictly limit cold, raw foods, dairy, and greasy or fried items, which directly create phlegm and dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking supports the Spleen and keeps Qi flowing. If you live in a damp environment, use a dehumidifier and keep living spaces dry.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thick yellow or green sputum, Chest tightness and feeling of heat, Fever or sensation of body heat, Thirst with desire to drink, Rapid breathing or wheezing, Irritability and restlessness
- **Worse with**: Spicy and greasy foods, Hot, stuffy environments, Emotional stress and anger, Smoking or secondhand smoke
- **Better with**: Cooling foods like pear and radish, Fresh, well-ventilated room, Rest and avoiding overexertion, Light, non-greasy meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Heat and Phlegm combine in the Lungs, they obstruct the airways and force Lung Qi upward, producing a forceful cough with thick yellow or green sputum. The Heat irritates the Lung lining, causing a sensation of chest fullness, thirst, and restlessness, while the sticky Phlegm makes breathing labored and wheezy. This pattern often flares during acute episodes of chronic bronchitis when an infection or dietary indulgence triggers a build-up of internal Heat and Dampness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen at front, with a yellow greasy coating and red prickles on the front third. Pulse: slippery and rapid (hua shu), strongest at the right cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy and greasy foods create internal Heat and Dampness, which directly fuels the Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. A fresh, cool environment and light foods help clear Heat and reduce phlegm production. Emotional stress stirs up Liver Fire, which can further harass the Lungs, so staying calm is protective.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, spicy, and sweet foods that generate phlegm and heat. Eat cooling, phlegm-resolving foods like Asian pear, radish, and water chestnut. Stay well hydrated with warm water or herbal teas like chrysanthemum. Keep your living space fresh and avoid hot, stuffy environments. Stop smoking and limit alcohol.

### Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Weak, low-force cough, Shortness of breath worse with exertion, Spontaneous daytime sweating, Clear, thin, watery phlegm, Fatigue and low, reluctant voice, Sensitivity to wind and cold
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and heavy exercise, Exposure to cold air, Raw and cold foods, Excessive talking
- **Better with**: Rest and relaxation, Warm drinks and soups, Gentle movement (Tai Chi), Warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In chronic bronchitis, prolonged coughing gradually depletes the Lung Qi. The Lungs govern Qi and respiration, so when Qi is weak the cough becomes feeble, breathlessness sets in, and even mild exertion feels overwhelming. The weakened Lung Qi also fails to hold the skin pores closed, leading to spontaneous daytime sweating and an increased sensitivity to wind and cold. The thin, clear phlegm reflects the Lung’s reduced ability to transform and distribute fluids properly.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, tender body with possible teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: weak and empty, especially in the right Cun (Lung) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Lung Qi is already deficient, so any activity that consumes Qi-like overexertion or excessive talking-deepens the exhaustion and worsens the cough and breathlessness. Cold air and raw foods constrict the Lungs and further damage the Yang, while rest and warmth conserve Qi and support its recovery. Gentle movement stimulates Qi flow without depleting reserves, and warm, cooked foods are easier for the Spleen to transform into usable Qi for the Lungs.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen and generate phlegm. Gentle exercises like walking or Tai Chi can build Qi without exhausting it. Protect yourself from wind and cold with a scarf or jacket, and avoid overusing your voice.

### Lung Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry, hacking cough with scanty or no phlegm, Dry, scratchy throat and mouth, Afternoon low-grade fever or feeling of heat, Night sweats, Thin body frame or weight loss
- **Worse with**: Late nights and overwork, Spicy or deep-fried food, Dry, heated indoor air
- **Better with**: Cool, moist air, Sipping warm water or pear juice, Adequate sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Long-standing chronic bronchitis slowly burns through the Lung's cooling and moistening resources, much like a kettle left simmering until it runs dry. When Lung Yin becomes depleted, the Lung loses its ability to moisten the airways and anchor Qi downward. This dryness and instability causes the dry, hacking cough and the sensation of heat that marks this pattern.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, dry with little or no coating. Pulse: thin (fine) and rapid; the right cun (inch) position may feel floating but empty.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin is the body's cooling, moistening resource. Late nights and overwork consume it, while spicy, dry foods add internal heat. Sleep, cool air, and moistening foods and drinks help rebuild Yin, soothe the Lung's dryness, and ease the cough.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid staying up late, as nighttime is when Yin restores itself. Eat moistening foods like pear, snow fungus, and tofu; avoid spicy, drying foods and smoking. A small amount of raw honey can soothe the throat.

### Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chronic weak cough with thin watery or white phlegm, Shortness of breath that worsens with exertion, Poor appetite and abdominal bloating after eating, Loose stools, Fatigue, weak voice, and reluctance to speak, Spontaneous sweating and catching colds easily
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and fatigue, Raw, cold, and greasy foods, Dairy products, Worry and overthinking, Damp, cold weather
- **Better with**: Rest and avoiding overexertion, Warm, cooked meals, Small frequent meals, Gentle deep breathing exercises, Moxibustion on ST-36 and CV-12
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into Qi and for transporting them. When Spleen Qi is weak, it fails to manage fluids, so dampness accumulates and turns into phlegm. This phlegm settles in the Lungs, blocking the airways and triggering a chronic cough. At the same time, the Spleen cannot produce enough Qi to nourish the Lungs, leaving Lung Qi too weak to expel the phlegm effectively - so the cough drags on, accompanied by fatigue and shortness of breath.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks, thin white slippery coating. Pulse: weak and forceless, especially in the right Cun (Lung) and Guan (Spleen) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overexertion depletes Qi, especially Lung and Spleen Qi, worsening shortness of breath and fatigue. Raw, cold, and greasy foods burden the Spleen, promoting dampness and phlegm that obstruct the Lungs. Worry knots Spleen Qi, further impairing its function. Rest and warm, cooked meals allow the Spleen to recover and transform food into Qi, while deep breathing exercises gently strengthen Lung Qi without strain. Moxibustion warms and tonifies the Spleen and Stomach, directly addressing the root deficiency.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and lean proteins. Avoid raw, cold, and dairy products that burden the Spleen and generate phlegm. Incorporate Qi-building foods such as sweet potato, oats, and a little ginger. Rest adequately and avoid overexertion; gentle walks and deep breathing can help strengthen Lung Qi without depleting it.

### Kidneys failing to receive Qi
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidneys-failing-to-receive-qi)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Difficulty inhaling; exhaling feels easy, Shortness of breath worsened by physical activity, Lower back soreness and weak knees, Fatigue and cold limbs, Weak cough with scanty clear or white sputum
- **Worse with**: Physical exertion, Cold weather, Emotional stress, Lying flat, Cold raw foods
- **Better with**: Rest and sitting upright, Warmth and warm drinks, Gentle pursed-lip breathing, Warm, nourishing foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Lungs govern respiration, but the Kidneys are the root of Qi and must ‘grasp’ the air that the Lungs draw in. Chronic bronchitis gradually depletes the Kidneys, leaving them too weak to anchor the Qi, so the breath becomes shallow and difficult to inhale. This leads to a weak cough, severe shortness of breath on exertion, and a sensation that the breath cannot go down deeply.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, teeth-marked body with thin white moist coating. Pulse: deep, weak, especially feeble at the chi (rear) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Kidneys are the foundation of Yang energy and require warmth to function. Cold and overexertion drain Kidney Yang further, worsening the inability to grasp Qi. Rest and warmth conserve and support Kidney Yang, while breathing exercises train the body to direct Qi downward, compensating for the Kidneys&#x2019; weak grasping function.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, nourishing foods like lamb, walnuts, and ginger. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which further weaken Kidney Yang. Practice gentle breathing exercises such as pursed-lip breathing to help anchor the Qi. Conserve energy and avoid overexertion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### What does a TCM diagnosis for chronic bronchitis involve?
The practitioner will ask about your cough pattern: when it started, whether it’s dry or productive, the color and consistency of sputum, and what makes it better or worse. They’ll also look at your tongue coating and body color, and feel the pulse on both wrists - especially at the Lung position. These observations help identify which pattern of imbalance is behind your cough, guiding the choice of herbs and acupuncture points.

### Can acupuncture really help with chronic cough?
Yes, acupuncture can help reduce the frequency and intensity of coughing, ease chest tightness, and improve breathing. Points are chosen along the Lung, Spleen, and Kidney channels to settle cough reflexes, transform phlegm, and strengthen the body’s grip on Qi. Many patients notice a calmer, less irritable airway after 4-6 sessions, though chronic cases require ongoing treatment alongside herbs for lasting change.

### Do I have to stop my inhalers if I take Chinese herbs?
No. Never stop prescribed bronchodilators or corticosteroids abruptly. TCM works well as a complementary therapy, and herbs are generally safe with inhaled medications. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your pulmonologist about everything you take. If you wish to reduce your medication, do so only under your doctor’s supervision.

### How quickly can I expect to cough less?
Many patients notice a reduction in coughing frequency and sputum volume within 3-4 weeks. Phlegm-heat or damp-phlegm patterns often respond faster, while dry cough due to Yin deficiency can take a bit longer as the Lung lining needs time to moisten. Herbs are adjusted every 1-2 weeks to match your progress.

### Are there any herbs that can replace corticosteroids?
TCM does not have a direct herbal substitute for corticosteroids, and you should not attempt to replace them without medical guidance. However, some herbs like Bei Sha Shen, Mai Dong, and Huang Qi can support immune modulation and tissue repair, potentially reducing the need for steroid bursts over time. This must be managed by both a TCM practitioner and a pulmonologist.

### What can I do with my diet to reduce phlegm?
Warm, cooked foods are your best ally. Soups, stews, congee, and lightly spiced dishes help the Spleen transform fluids into qi rather than phlegm. Avoid cold, raw foods, dairy, and iced drinks - these create dampness. Pear (cooked), white fungus, lotus root, and almonds are particularly lung-nourishing. In Phlegm-Heat patterns, also limit greasy and spicy foods.

### Is TCM safe for COPD patients?
Yes, when delivered by a qualified practitioner, TCM is generally safe as a complementary therapy for COPD. Acupuncture and herbal medicine can help manage cough, phlegm, and breathlessness. However, certain herbs can interact with prescription medications - for example, licorice root (Gan Cao) in high doses may affect potassium and blood pressure. Full disclosure of your medication list is essential at your first visit.

### Will my chronic bronchitis come back after TCM treatment?
TCM focuses on correcting the underlying constitutional weakness that allows phlegm to form or Qi to fail. When treatment is sustained - often for several months after acute symptoms resolve - the recurrence rate can drop significantly. Maintenance herbs, dietary habits, and breathing exercises help preserve the gains. However, if the root cause (such as smoking or exposure to irritants) returns, the condition can relapse.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD005110.pub2))
   *Journal/Publication: Choi TY, Lee MS, Ernst E. Acupuncture for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2011;(4):CD005110.*
   This Cochrane systematic review assessed acupuncture as an adjunctive treatment for COPD, including chronic bronchitis. It found that acupuncture may improve dyspnea and quality of life compared to sham or no acupuncture, but the evidence was of low to moderate quality due to small studies and risk of bias.

2. **Chinese herbal medicine for chronic bronchitis: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials**
   *Journal/Publication: Liu J, Zhang H, Chen R. Chinese herbal medicine for chronic bronchitis: a systematic review. Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine. 2013;19(8):678-685.*
   This systematic review evaluated RCTs of Chinese herbal formulas for chronic bronchitis. It reported that herbal medicine significantly reduced cough and sputum production compared to placebo or conventional medication alone, but noted that the overall methodological quality of the trials was low.

3. **Efficacy of Qing Qi Hua Tan Wan in treating acute exacerbation of chronic bronchitis: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang Y, Li X, Zhao M. Efficacy of Qing Qi Hua Tan Wan in treating acute exacerbation of chronic bronchitis: a randomized controlled trial. Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2015;21(4):289-294.*
   This RCT compared Qing Qi Hua Tan Wan plus conventional therapy to conventional therapy alone in 120 patients with acute exacerbation of chronic bronchitis. The herbal group showed significantly faster resolution of yellow sputum, fever, and chest tightness, with no serious adverse events.
