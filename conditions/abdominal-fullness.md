## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, abdominal fullness is most often associated with functional gastrointestinal disorders such as functional dyspepsia or irritable bowel syndrome, where the gut appears structurally normal but its motility or sensation is altered. It can also result from excess gas production, slow stomach emptying, constipation, or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. Diagnosis typically involves a symptom history and may include breath tests, endoscopy, or imaging to rule out structural or inflammatory causes.

### Treatments
Standard approaches include dietary modifications (such as a low-FODMAP diet), probiotics, and over-the-counter remedies like simethicone or activated charcoal. For underlying motility issues, prokinetic agents or laxatives may be prescribed. Antispasmodics can help when cramping accompanies the bloating, and psychological therapies are sometimes recommended when stress is a prominent trigger.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands abdominal fullness primarily through the Spleen and Stomach, the organ pair responsible for transforming food into Qi and blood and transporting the resulting energy throughout the body. When the Spleen is weak - often from irregular eating, overwork, or constitutional tendency - it fails to manage fluids and food, leading to a buildup of Dampness that creates a heavy, persistent bloated sensation. This is why the Spleen is central to so many digestive complaints.
But the Liver plays an equally important role. The Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi, and emotional stress directly disrupts this flow. When Liver Qi stagnates, it can “invade” the Spleen, causing the digestive system to seize up and produce episodic bloating, belching, and rib-side distension that flares with frustration. This connection between emotions and digestion is one of TCM's most clinically useful insights.
Beyond these two organs, other patterns can produce fullness: undigested food simply sitting and fermenting in the Stomach (Food Stagnation), a combination of Dampness and Heat creating a swollen, sticky feeling, or a deep coldness from deficient Spleen Yang that makes the abdomen feel better with warmth. Even simple dryness in the Large Intestine can cause obstruction and bloating. That's why the same Western diagnosis of “functional bloating” can have six different TCM roots - and why treatment must be tailored to the pattern.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the nature of your fullness - when it appears, what makes it better or worse, and what other sensations accompany it. This history helps distinguish whether the root is an excess pattern like stagnation or a deficiency pattern like weak digestion.
If the bloating flares with stress and is accompanied by belching, sighing, or a sensation of distension under the ribs, it points toward Liver Qi Stagnation. The tongue may look normal or slightly red with a thin white coat, and the pulse often feels wiry, reflecting the constrained energy.
When the fullness is a constant, dull puffiness after meals, along with poor appetite, loose stools, and a heavy tired feeling, Spleen Deficiency with Dampness is likely. The tongue appears pale and swollen with a greasy white coat, and the pulse is soft or slippery - signs that the Spleen is too weak to manage fluids.
Food Stagnation in the Stomach creates a very different picture: a sensation of stuffiness that worsens after eating, foul-smelling belches, acid reflux, and sometimes nausea. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse feels slippery, indicating that undigested food is fermenting in the digestive tract.
Damp-Heat in the Stomach and Spleen adds a layer of heat to the fullness. Along with bloating, you may notice a bitter taste in the mouth, nausea, a sticky yellow tongue coating, and a pulse that is both slippery and rapid. This pattern often arises from rich, greasy foods combined with a hot, humid environment.
Spleen Yang Deficiency makes the abdomen feel cold and full, and the discomfort eases when you press a warm hand against it or wrap up. Cold hands and feet, a pale tongue with a thin white coat, and a slow, deep pulse confirm that the digestive fire is too weak to warm and move things.
Large Intestine Dryness is a less common cause where the fullness comes from backed-up, dry stools. The key clues are constipation with hard, pellet-like bowel movements, a dry mouth, and a tongue that looks dry with little to no coating, signaling a lack of fluids in the gut.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating that worsens after eating, Loose, unformed stools, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Poor appetite or lack of taste, Fatigue and lack of energy
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Overeating, Damp or humid weather, Sitting for long periods or sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle walking after meals, Warm compress on the abdomen, Rest and adequate sleep, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is meant to transform food and fluids into Qi and blood. When Spleen Qi is weak, this process falters and fluids build up as heavy, turbid Dampness. That Dampness sits in the middle burner, blocking the normal flow of Qi and creating a persistent, dragging sense of abdominal fullness, especially after meals.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), may feel slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>The Spleen dislikes cold and dampness. Cold and raw foods, dairy, and greasy meals weaken Spleen Yang and generate more Dampness, directly aggravating the root imbalance. Overeating overwhelms the Spleen's capacity to transform, while damp weather adds external dampness. Sedentary habits cause Qi to stagnate, further impeding the middle burner.</p><p>Warmth, light meals, and gentle movement support the Spleen's transformation and transportation functions. Warm foods and abdominal heat dispel cold and dry dampness, while walking moves Qi and helps the Spleen process fluids. Rest allows the Spleen to recover its energy.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like rice porridge, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of lean protein. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and sweet foods that create dampness. Drink ginger tea to warm the middle burner. Eat small, frequent meals and avoid overeating. Gentle daily movement, like walking, helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation. Keep the abdomen warm and get adequate rest.

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distension that moves or radiates to the ribs and flanks, Worse with stress, frustration, or emotional upset, Frequent sighing and belching, Irritability or mood swings, Feeling of a lump in the throat (plum pit sensation)
- **Worse with**: Stress and emotional upset, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Eating in a rush or while upset, Alcohol
- **Better with**: Gentle movement or stretching, Warm peppermint or citrus peel tea, Deep breathing and relaxation, Talking through frustrations
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or suppressed feelings build up, the Liver's ability to spread Qi becomes impaired - this is Liver Qi Stagnation. The stagnant Qi then "invades" the Spleen and Stomach, disrupting their ability to move food and energy downward. The trapped Qi accumulates in the abdomen, creating a sensation of distension and fullness that moves around, often accompanied by belching and sighing as the body tries to release the pressure.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body with red or slightly purple sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, most noticeable at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress is the root cause of Liver Qi Stagnation - it directly constrains the Liver's ability to spread Qi, which then backs up into the abdomen. Greasy, heavy foods further burden the Spleen and Stomach, which are already compromised by the invading Liver Qi. Gentle movement and aromatic herbs help disperse stagnant Qi, while deep breathing physically moves the diaphragm and encourages Liver Qi to flow again. Peppermint and citrus peel are especially useful because they enter the Liver channel and have a dispersing, moving nature.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, regular meals and avoid rushing through them. Include gentle aromatic spices like fennel, peppermint, and citrus peel in your cooking. Regular exercise - especially walking, yoga, or tai chi - helps keep Liver Qi flowing. Most importantly, find healthy outlets for stress and frustration, such as journaling, talking with a friend, or creative hobbies.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Upper abdominal bloating, worse after meals, Sour, rotten-smelling belching, Acid regurgitation, Thick greasy tongue coating, Relief after vomiting, Aversion to food or its smell
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Eating late at night, Lying down after meals, Alcohol
- **Better with**: Fasting or skipping a meal, Gentle walking after meals, Warm ginger tea, Small, frequent meals, Vomiting (relieves stagnation)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you eat too much or consume rich, greasy foods that are hard to digest, the Stomach cannot process them properly. The undigested food sits and ferments, producing gas, foul-smelling belching, and a sensation of bloating and fullness in the upper abdomen. This is an excess condition where the Stomach's descending function is blocked by the stagnant food mass.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy, curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), full, and most prominent at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating and heavy foods overwhelm the Stomach, causing food to stagnate and ferment, which produces gas and bloating. Fasting, light meals, and gentle movement allow the Stomach to clear the backlog and restore its downward movement, relieving the fullness and sour belching.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, lighter meals and chew food thoroughly. Avoid greasy, fried, and rich foods until the bloating subsides. Stop eating before you feel full. Gentle walking after meals helps move Qi downward.

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bitter or sticky taste in the mouth, Loose, sticky, or incomplete bowel movements, Thirst with little desire to actually drink, Heavy, foggy sensation in the head or limbs, Thick, yellow, greasy coating on the tongue
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Hot and humid weather, Alcohol, Overeating, Sitting for long periods or sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Eating light, bland meals, Moderate daily exercise, Cool, dry weather, Drinking warm water
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat combine in your digestive tract like a humid, stagnant swamp. The Dampness weighs everything down and blocks the normal movement of Qi, while the Heat agitates and creates a sensation of fullness and bloating. Because the Spleen’s job of transforming food and fluids is impaired, you get that heavy, stuck feeling in the upper belly, often with nausea and a bitter or sticky taste in your mouth.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, and a thick yellow greasy coating that is heaviest in the centre and back. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), often also slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, sweet, and heavy foods directly generate Dampness, while alcohol and spicy foods add Heat, worsening the swampy internal environment. Hot, humid weather mirrors the pattern and makes you feel worse. Light, bland meals and warm water gently support the Spleen without overloading it, while regular movement activates Qi to help dry out Dampness and clear Heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, sweet, and heavily spiced foods-these create more Dampness and Heat. Stick to light, easily digested meals like congee, steamed vegetables, and modest amounts of lean protein. Sip warm water or barley water throughout the day, and skip alcohol and iced drinks. Gentle daily movement such as walking helps your Qi circulate and drains Dampness.

### Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating worse after eating, Bloating relieved by warmth and pressure, Cold hands and feet, Loose stools with undigested food, Fatigue and heaviness, Bland taste in the mouth
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overeating, Cold weather exposure, Overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Warm compress on the abdomen, Gentle pressure or massage, Warm, cooked meals, Rest and adequate sleep, Warm ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Spleen Yang is the digestive fire that warms and transforms food. When it is deficient, cold accumulates in the middle burner, and the Spleen loses its ability to ascend clear Qi and descend turbid Qi. This cold stagnation causes the abdomen to feel full and bloated, especially after eating when the weakened Yang is further taxed.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Yang Deficiency means the body's internal warming and transforming function is low. Cold foods and environments further drain that warmth, worsening the cold stagnation that causes bloating. Warmth, whether from food, drinks, or external heat, supplements the deficient Yang and helps the Spleen move Qi, relieving the full sensation. Gentle pressure and rest also support the Spleen's function by conserving energy, while overeating and fatigue overwhelm it.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easy-to-digest foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, and iced foods and drinks that further damage Spleen Yang. Use warming spices like ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper in cooking. Keep the abdomen warm with a belly band or hot water bottle, especially in cold weather. Gentle exercise like walking or tai chi can help move Qi without exhausting your energy.

### Large Intestine Dryness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/large-intestine-dryness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry, hard, pellet-like stools, Fullness that improves after bowel movement, Dry mouth and throat, Thin body or weight loss, Bad breath
- **Worse with**: Spicy and fried foods, Dehydration, Overwork and fatigue, Stress and emotional upset
- **Better with**: Warm water and herbal teas, Moistening foods (pear, honey), Gentle movement or stretching, Bowel movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Normally the Large Intestine has just enough moisture to move waste along smoothly. When body fluids run low - often from aging, chronic illness, or too many drying foods - the intestine becomes parched. The stool turns dry, hard, and difficult to pass, and this physical obstruction creates a stubborn feeling of abdominal fullness that only eases once the bowels move.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin, and dry with cracks, often a thin dry yellowish coat. Pulse: fine (thready), possibly slightly rapid or choppy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is rooted in a depletion of fluids, so anything that adds moisture to the body helps the intestine move waste and relieves the pressure. Drying habits like spicy food, dehydration, or overwork deepen the fluid deficit, making the stool harder and the fullness worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Sip warm water throughout the day, and include moistening foods like pear, honey, sesame seeds, and spinach. Avoid spicy, fried, and overly dry roasted foods that steal moisture. A gentle daily walk helps the bowels move without straining.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help with bloating?
Yes, many people find acupuncture very effective for abdominal fullness. By inserting fine needles at specific points like Zusanli ST-36 and Zhongwan REN-12, acupuncturists can help regulate the motility of the stomach and intestines, reduce gas, and calm the nervous system. The effect is often felt within a few sessions, especially when combined with herbal medicine.

### What should I eat to reduce abdominal fullness?
Generally, favour warm, cooked foods and avoid cold, raw, or greasy items that can further weaken the Spleen. Small, frequent meals are better than large ones. Ginger tea, fennel seeds, and gently cooked vegetables like carrot and pumpkin can help move Qi. Your practitioner will give you specific dietary advice based on your pattern, but these basics apply to most people with bloating.

### How long until I see results?
If your bloating is due to an acute excess condition like food stagnation, you might notice improvement in just a few days. For stress-related Liver Qi stagnation, many people feel better within 2-4 weeks. Chronic deficiency patterns that have built up over years take longer - often 4-8 weeks to see a meaningful shift, with continued progress over months as your digestive energy rebuilds.

### Can I combine TCM with my current medications?
In most cases, yes. Herbal formulas and acupuncture can be safely combined with over-the-counter remedies and prescription medications. However, if you are taking blood thinners or have a complex medication regimen, always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor. Some herbs can have mild blood-moving effects, so coordination is important.

### Is abdominal fullness related to stress?
Absolutely. In TCM, the Liver is highly sensitive to emotional strain, and when its Qi stagnates, it directly impacts the Spleen and Stomach, causing bloating, belching, and distension. If your fullness flares during tense periods and eases when you relax, stress is likely a major factor. Treatment will focus on smoothing the Liver Qi alongside supporting digestion.

### Are there self-massage points I can use for bloating?
Yes. Gently massaging the point Zusanli ST-36 (four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shinbone) for a few minutes on each leg can help stimulate digestive function. You can also try circular clockwise massage on the abdomen around the navel, which follows the natural direction of the large intestine and can encourage movement of gas.
