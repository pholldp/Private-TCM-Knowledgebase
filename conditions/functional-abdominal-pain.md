## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, functional abdominal pain is diagnosed when a person experiences persistent or recurrent abdominal discomfort without any identifiable structural, infectious, or biochemical cause. It is a disorder of gut-brain interaction, where the nerves in the digestive tract become hypersensitive and the normal movement of the intestines is disrupted. Common examples include irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional dyspepsia. Diagnosis is made after tests like endoscopy, imaging, and blood work come back normal, ruling out conditions such as ulcers, gallstones, or inflammatory bowel disease.

### Treatments
Treatment usually focuses on symptom management. Options include antispasmodic medications to relax the gut muscles, low-dose antidepressants to modulate pain signaling, and dietary adjustments such as a low-FODMAP diet. Psychological therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and gut-directed hypnotherapy are also used to address the brain-gut connection.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands functional abdominal pain primarily through the flow of Qi in the middle burner - the Stomach, Spleen, and Intestines. Pain is a signal that Qi is not moving freely.
This can happen for many reasons: emotional stress can cause the Liver to stagnate and disrupt the smooth flow of Qi through the abdomen; eating too much or the wrong foods can overload the Stomach and create a traffic jam of undigested food; external cold can invade and cause the intestinal Qi to contract and spasm; or a long-term weakness in the Spleen's digestive fire can leave the abdomen cold, sluggish, and undernourished.
Each pattern produces a different quality of pain and a different set of accompanying signs. A cramping pain that worsens with cold and improves with a hot water bottle points to Cold invading the Large Intestine. A distending, wandering pain that flares up with stress and eases with a good cry suggests Liver Qi Stagnation.
A heavy, bloated ache after a rich meal with sour belching is classic Food Stagnation. A burning, urgent lower abdominal pain with foul-smelling sticky stools indicates Damp-Heat. A fixed, stabbing pain that worsens at night signals Blood Stagnation. And a dull, persistent ache that feels better with gentle pressure and warmth, accompanied by cold hands and loose stools, is Spleen Yang Deficiency.
Because the same Western diagnosis can stem from such different roots, TCM treatment is never one-size-fits-all. The practitioner uses the tongue, pulse, and a detailed symptom history to identify which pattern is dominant, and then selects herbs, acupuncture points, and dietary advice that directly target that imbalance.
This is why two people with the same diagnosis of IBS might receive completely different herbal formulas - and why TCM can succeed where a single conventional protocol has failed.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner begins by asking what the pain feels like and when it strikes. Sharp, cramping pain that comes on suddenly after cold exposure or eating icy foods points to Cold invading the Large Intestine. The abdomen feels tight, the person craves warmth, and a warm compress brings quick relief. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels tight like a taut rope.
If the pain is more about distension and bloating, and it flares up or eases with emotional ups and downs, Liver Qi Stagnation is likely. People with this pattern often sigh frequently, feel irritable, and describe a wandering ache rather than a fixed spot. The tongue may look normal or have a thin white coating, but the pulse has a distinctive wiry quality, like a guitar string.
When the discomfort follows a heavy meal or overindulgence, Food Stagnation in the Stomach is the usual suspect. The pain is accompanied by a sensation of fullness, sour belching, nausea, and an aversion to eating. The tongue coating becomes thick and greasy, and the pulse feels slippery, as if beads were rolling under the fingers. Relief often comes after a bowel movement or vomiting.
A different picture emerges when the pain is accompanied by a heavy, stuffy sensation and the abdomen feels tender to the touch. Damp-Heat in the Large Intestine often brings loose, foul-smelling stools or constipation, a feeling of incomplete emptying, and a burning sensation around the anus. The tongue is red with a yellow, greasy coat, and the pulse is rapid and slippery.
For a fixed, stabbing pain that does not move and may be worse at night, the practitioner considers Blood Stagnation. This pattern can follow a long history of Qi stagnation or injury. The pain is precisely localized, and the person may notice dark stools. The tongue often shows purplish spots or a dusky color, and the pulse feels choppy, like a knife scraping bamboo.
A dull, persistent ache that feels better with gentle pressure, warmth, or eating suggests Spleen Yang Deficiency. This is a slow-burning pattern, often accompanied by fatigue, loose stools, and a preference for lying down. The tongue is pale and puffy with tooth marks on the sides, and the pulse is deep and weak, reflecting an internal coldness that lacks the strength to cause sharp pain.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Abdominal distension and bloating that moves around, Pain worsens with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent sighing, Irritability or mood swings, Belching and gas
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Suppressing emotions, Greasy, fried, or rich foods, Alcohol, Prolonged sitting or inactivity
- **Better with**: Emotional release (talking, crying), Gentle movement like walking, Peppermint or fennel tea, Deep breathing and relaxation, A calm, stress-free environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or suppressed anger disrupts this flow, Qi stagnates in the abdomen, leading to distension and pain that comes and goes with mood changes. The stagnant Qi can also impair the Spleen's digestive function, causing bloating, belching, and poor appetite.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body, red or slightly purple sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and suppressed emotions are the root cause of Liver Qi stagnation; they directly block the free flow of Qi in the abdomen. Emotional release, deep breathing, and gentle movement help unblock that Qi and relieve pain. Aromatic herbs like peppermint and fennel gently move Liver Qi, while greasy, heavy foods and alcohol further congest the Liver and worsen stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Practice stress-reducing activities like walking, yoga, or meditation. Avoid greasy, spicy, or heavy foods that burden the Liver. Eat smaller, more frequent meals and include foods that gently move Qi, such as peppermint tea, fennel, and citrus peel. Express emotions rather than suppressing them.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and pain worse with pressure, Sour, rotten-smelling belching, Pain worsens after eating, Relief after vomiting, Thick, greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Overeating or large meals, Greasy, fried, or rich foods, Lying down immediately after eating, Eating late at night
- **Better with**: Light, easily digestible foods, Gentle movement like walking, Vomiting (temporary relief)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you eat too much or consume rich, greasy foods, your Stomach struggles to break everything down. Instead of moving downward, food sits and ferments, creating a traffic jam of Qi that leads to bloating, distension, and pain. The rotting food produces sour-smelling gas and acid that rise up as belching or reflux.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: body normal, with a thick, greasy or curd-like coating thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and full (实 shí), most prominent at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating or heavy foods overwhelm the Stomach's ability to 'rot and ripen' food, leading to stagnation and Qi rebellion. Vomiting expels the stagnant mass, temporarily relieving pressure. Gentle movement encourages the Stomach Qi to descend, while light, warm foods are easier for a weakened digestive fire to process.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and avoid heavy, greasy, or fried foods. Stop eating at least three hours before bed. A gentle walk after meals helps your Stomach digest. Try simple congee with a little ginger when your digestion feels sluggish.

### Cold invading the Large Intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-invading-the-large-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden cramping or gripping pain, Cold sensation in the lower abdomen, Pain relieved by warmth or pressure, Watery diarrhea with little odour, Preference for warm drinks, no thirst, Cold hands and feet
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Cold weather or drafts on the belly, Overexertion when already chilled
- **Better with**: Warmth (hot water bottle, heating pad, warm drinks), Warm drinks (ginger tea), Gentle abdominal pressure or massage, Rest in a warm room
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Cold invades the Large Intestine, it causes the smooth muscle and Qi to contract and spasm, much like how cold weather makes you tense up. This sudden constriction blocks the normal flow of Qi and blood through the intestines, producing intense, cramping pain. At the same time, the Cold dampens the digestive fire, so fluids are not properly absorbed, leading to urgent, watery diarrhea with little smell.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body with a thick, white, slippery coating that looks moist. Pulse: deep (沉 chén) and tight (紧 jǐn), like a tightly stretched rope.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold is a contracting, obstructing force in TCM. When it enters the abdomen, it freezes the flow of Qi, causing the intense, knot-like cramps. Warmth does the opposite: it relaxes and expands the channels, allowing Qi to move freely again and stopping the pain. Cold foods and environments add more Cold to the body, making the constriction worse, while warm drinks and external heat gently restore normal flow.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep your abdomen warm with a belly band or hot water bottle, especially in cold weather. Sip warm ginger tea throughout the day and avoid all raw, cold, or iced foods and drinks. Gentle abdominal massage with a warm palm can help move Qi and ease cramps.

### Damp-Heat in the Large Intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-large-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cramping lower abdominal pain with a heavy sensation, Burning feeling at the anus, Urgent, foul-smelling, sticky stools, Feeling of incomplete evacuation after bowel movements, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Worse after greasy, spicy food or alcohol
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or rich foods, Spicy foods, Alcohol, Hot and humid weather, Overeating or large meals
- **Better with**: Eating light, cooling foods like mung beans, Staying in a cool, dry place, Drinking barley water to drain dampness, Rest or lying down
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Dampness and Heat combine and settle in the Large Intestine, they block the normal flow of Qi, creating a heavy, cramping pain in the lower abdomen. The Heat causes a burning sensation and urgency, while the Dampness makes the stools sticky, foul, and difficult to fully pass. This pattern often flares after eating rich, greasy, or spicy foods, or during hot, humid weather.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating, thickest in the centre and root. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat is a sticky, inflammatory mixture that clogs the Intestines. Greasy, fried, and spicy foods, as well as alcohol, directly add Heat and Dampness to the body, worsening the pain and diarrhea. Hot, humid weather does the same from the outside. Cooling, light foods and a dry, cool environment help drain Dampness and clear Heat, easing the pressure and allowing the bowel to function smoothly again.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sugary foods, as well as alcohol and dairy, which fuel Dampness and Heat. Favour light, easily digestible meals with cooling, damp-draining foods like mung bean soup, cucumber, watermelon, and barley. Drink plenty of water and stay in cool, dry environments.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing abdominal pain that does not move, Pain worsens at night, Pain is worse with pressure, Dark or purplish complexion, lips, or nails, Dark menstrual blood with clots (in women), Dark stools
- **Worse with**: Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Emotional stress, Nighttime
- **Better with**: Gentle movement like walking, Warm compress on the abdomen, Deep breathing and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi flow stalls for a long time, or after a physical injury, the blood can become sluggish and pool in the abdomen. This creates a fixed, knotted obstruction that blocks the channels. The pain is sharp and stabbing because the blood is literally stuck in one place, and it feels worse at night when circulation naturally slows down.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè) like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦 xián) if Qi stagnation is involved.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis is a condition of sluggish flow. Gentle movement and warmth help to invigorate circulation, while cold and inactivity cause further congealing. Stress tightens the Liver Qi, which constricts vessels and deepens the stagnation, making the pain worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which can congeal blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or stretching helps keep circulation flowing. Incorporate small amounts of turmeric or saffron into cooking to gently invigorate blood.

### Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull persistent abdominal pain, Pain relieved by warmth and pressure, Cold hands and feet, Loose watery stools with undigested food, Poor appetite and bloating worse after eating, Feeling cold easily and disliking cold, Tiredness and heaviness in the limbs
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or drafts on the belly, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overwork and fatigue, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Warmth (hot water bottle, heating pad, warm drinks), Gentle abdominal pressure or massage, Rest or lying down, Eating small warm meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Spleen Yang is the digestive fire that transforms food into Qi and warmth. When it weakens, Cold and Dampness build in the middle burner, slowing everything down. The intestines lose their warm driving force, so Qi stagnates and causes a dull, persistent ache that feels better with external warmth and gentle pressure.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak, especially soft at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Yang is like a digestive fire that transforms food into energy and warmth. Cold and exhaustion douse this fire, causing the dull ache and sluggish digestion. Warmth, rest, and gentle nourishment stoke the fire, easing pain and restoring function.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like congee, soups, and stews. Avoid raw, cold, and icy foods and drinks. Keep your abdomen warm with a hot water bottle or a belly band. Gentle daily movement such as walking supports digestion, but rest when you feel exhausted.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Does acupuncture hurt for abdominal pain?
Most people find acupuncture deeply relaxing, not painful. The needles are hair-thin and inserted quickly. You may feel a mild ache, warmth, or tingling around the point - this is a normal sign that Qi is responding. The abdomen itself has fewer nerve endings than the hands or feet, so points on the belly usually feel gentle. Many patients even fall asleep during treatment.

### Can I use TCM alongside my current medications?
Yes, TCM is generally safe to combine with conventional treatments for functional abdominal pain. Herbs and acupuncture do not interfere with antispasmodics or low-dose antidepressants. However, if you are taking blood-thinning medications like warfarin or aspirin, tell your TCM practitioner - some herbs that move Blood may need to be adjusted. Always bring a full list of your medications to your first consultation.

### What foods should I avoid while having treatment?
Across all patterns, it's best to avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, as well as excessive spicy or sugary items. Cold foods directly contract the intestines and slow digestion, while heavy, rich meals can overwhelm a weak Spleen. Instead, favor warm, cooked meals like soups, congee, and steamed vegetables. Your practitioner will give you more specific advice based on your pattern - for example, someone with Damp-Heat will need to avoid alcohol and fried foods, while someone with Cold invasion should steer clear of iced drinks and raw salads.

### Will the pain come back after I stop treatment?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance, not just mask the pain. If the root cause is fully resolved, the pain should not return. However, functional abdominal pain is often tied to lifestyle factors like chronic stress or dietary habits. Your practitioner will teach you self-care strategies - including acupressure, dietary guidelines, and stress management - to help maintain the results long after formal treatment ends. Occasional flare-ups can happen, but they are usually milder and shorter-lived.

### Is TCM safe for children with recurrent tummy aches?
Yes, TCM is very gentle and well-suited for children. Pediatric abdominal pain often responds quickly to simple dietary changes and gentle herbal formulas like Bao He Wan for food stagnation. Acupuncture can be replaced with acupressure or pediatric tui na massage for younger children. Always work with a practitioner experienced in treating children, and see our Safety section for red-flag symptoms that need immediate medical attention.

### How soon after eating can I have acupuncture?
It's best to have a light meal about an hour before acupuncture, so you are neither hungry nor overly full. An empty stomach can make you feel lightheaded, while a very full stomach can be uncomfortable when lying down. A small, warm snack like soup or congee is ideal.
