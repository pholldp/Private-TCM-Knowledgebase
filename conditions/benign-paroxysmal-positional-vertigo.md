# Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (良性阵发性位置性眩晕)
**Pinyin**: liáng xìng zhèn fā xìng wèi zhì xìng xuàn yūn | **Kind**: Condition | **Category**: Dizziness & Balance, Ear & Hearing Problems  
**Synonyms**: Benign Positional Vertigo, BPPV, Dizziness Associated With Change In Head Position, Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) | **Body Regions**: Ear

## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, BPPV is understood as a mechanical problem of the inner ear. Tiny calcium carbonate crystals, called otoconia, become dislodged from their usual location and migrate into one of the semicircular canals. When you change head position, these loose crystals move, sending false signals to the brain that the room is spinning.
The vertigo is brief - usually less than a minute - and is provoked by specific head movements like rolling over in bed, tilting the head back, or bending forward.
Diagnosis is typically made with the Dix-Hallpike maneuver, where a doctor moves your head into certain positions to trigger the vertigo and observe characteristic eye movements (nystagmus). The condition is called 'benign' because it is not life-threatening, and 'paroxysmal' because it comes in sudden, short bursts.

### Treatments
The first-line treatment is canalith repositioning maneuvers, such as the Epley or Semont maneuvers, which use a series of head and body movements to guide the loose crystals back to their proper chamber. These are performed in the doctor's office and can be taught for home use.
Medications like meclizine or dimenhydrinate may be prescribed for nausea, but they do not fix the underlying crystal problem. In rare, persistent cases, surgery to plug the affected canal may be considered.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, BPPV falls under the category of 'vertigo' (眩晕, xuàn yūn). The condition is viewed not as a mechanical ear problem alone, but as a disturbance of the clear orifices of the head - the spaces where the senses and balance are governed. The inner ear is part of this system, and when something rises up to cloud or agitate these orifices, vertigo results. The key players are internal Wind, Phlegm, and deficiency states.
The Liver is often at the center. When Liver Yang rises too forcefully - often from stress, anger, or underlying Yin deficiency - it generates internal Wind that rushes upward, shaking the balance mechanism. This pattern produces sudden, intense vertigo with a throbbing headache and irritability.
The Spleen is another common culprit: if it is weakened by poor diet or overwork, it fails to transform fluids, and phlegm-dampness accumulates. This phlegm can then be stirred upward by Wind, leading to the heavy, foggy dizziness with nausea that many BPPV patients describe.
Deficiency patterns also play a role. When Qi and Blood are insufficient, the brain - known as the 'Sea of Marrow' - is not properly nourished. Head movements momentarily disrupt the already weak supply, causing brief spinning.
Similarly, a decline in Kidney Essence, which is the foundation for all marrow and brain function, can leave the inner ear and balance centers undernourished, resulting in chronic, hollow-feeling dizziness. Because the same symptom of positional vertigo can arise from such different roots, TCM treatment is never one-size-fits-all.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner first asks what the dizziness feels like and what triggers it. With BPPV, the vertigo is brief and tied to head movements like rolling over or looking up. The character of the dizziness and the symptoms that come with it point toward one pattern rather than another, so the practitioner listens carefully to how you describe the sensation.
When the vertigo strikes suddenly, feels intense, and comes with irritability, a flushed face, or a bitter taste in the mouth, the picture suggests Liver Yang Rising. The tongue tends to be red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and possibly rapid. These signs reveal that upward-rushing Liver energy is disturbing the head.
If the dizziness brings a heavy, cloudy feeling as though the head is wrapped in a cloth, along with nausea or a sensation of phlegm in the throat, the pattern leans toward Wind-Phlegm or Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. Here, phlegm has risen to clog the senses.
Dizziness that worsens when you are tired or after prolonged standing, accompanied by a pale face and a feeling of weakness, points to Qi and Blood Deficiency. The tongue is pale and the pulse is thin and weak. This pattern reflects insufficient nourishment reaching the head, leaving the brain under-supplied and easily unsettled.
Chronic, low-grade dizziness with tinnitus and a sore, weak lower back suggests Kidney Essence Deficiency. The vertigo is less dramatic but persistent, and the tongue may be pale or red with little coating, while the pulse is thin and deep. The root here is a deep lack of the vital essence that supports the brain and inner ear.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden spinning vertigo triggered by head movement, Throbbing or distending headache at the temples, Irritability and quick temper, Flushed face and red eyes, Ringing in the ears (tinnitus), Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Stress and anger, Alcohol and spicy food, Hot, stuffy environments, Overwork and lack of sleep, Premenstrual phase
- **Better with**: Resting in a quiet, dark room, Cooling foods and drinks, Gentle movement like walking or Tai Chi, Reducing stress and staying calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Liver's Yang energy loses its anchor and surges upward, it rushes to the head like a wave, disturbing the delicate balance organs in the inner ear. This sudden upward rush is easily triggered by changes in head position, producing the brief, intense spinning sensation of BPPV. The same surge of heat and pressure causes the throbbing headache, flushed face, and irritability that often accompany the dizziness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with redder sides, thin dry yellow or scanty coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), possibly fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is pronounced.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger stir up Liver Qi, which transforms into heat and sends Yang rushing to the head. Alcohol and spicy foods are heating in nature and feed that upward fire. A quiet, dark room removes sensory stimulation that can aggravate the inner ear, while cooling foods help counter the internal heat. Gentle movement keeps the Liver's energy flowing without stoking it further, and adequate sleep nourishes the Yin that normally anchors Yang, preventing it from flaring upward.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize cooling, calming foods like celery, cucumber, and leafy greens, and steer clear of alcohol, coffee, and spicy dishes that add heat. Wind down before bed with a screen-free routine, and try gentle daily movement like Tai Chi or walking to keep Liver Qi flowing smoothly without overstimulation.

### Wind-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness with a heavy, foggy sensation, Nausea or vomiting of phlegm, Copious white frothy sputum, Chest stuffiness or fullness, Heaviness in the body or limbs, Trembling or numbness in hands
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or dairy-heavy foods, Overeating, Stress and frustration, Sudden head movements, Damp or humid weather
- **Better with**: Avoiding greasy, heavy foods, Gentle movement like walking or Tai Chi, Warm, cooked, nourishing meals, Reducing stress and staying calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weakened, it fails to transform and transport fluids, leading to the buildup of phlegm-dampness in the body. This phlegm can then be stirred upward by internal Wind, often arising from Liver imbalance, clouding the clear orifices of the head. The result is paroxysmal vertigo triggered by head movement, accompanied by a heavy, foggy sensation and nausea.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: slightly pale and swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: wiry and slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, cold, and dairy foods create phlegm and dampness, which worsen the phlegm component of this pattern. Stress and frustration stir Liver wind, which carries phlegm upward to the head. Gentle movement helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation, while warm cooked meals support Spleen function to reduce phlegm production.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, dairy-rich, cold, and raw foods, which directly contribute to phlegm and dampness. Favor warm, cooked meals and easily digested grains and vegetables. Gentle, regular movement like walking or Tai Chi helps keep Qi flowing and prevents stagnation, while managing stress reduces the internal wind that drives phlegm upward.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heaviness in the head, as if wrapped in cloth, Nausea, bloating, and poor appetite, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating, Drowsiness and a heavy, sluggish body
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or dairy-heavy foods, Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Lying down immediately after a meal, Damp or humid weather, Excessive worry or overthinking
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked, nourishing meals, Ginger or cardamom tea, Gentle movement like walking or Tai Chi, Warm, dry environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids, and dampness accumulates into phlegm that settles in the middle burner. This turbid phlegm-dampness rises to disturb the clear orifices of the head, making you feel heavy and dizzy. A change in head position stirs the stagnant dampness, triggering brief but intense spinning vertigo.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, with scalloped edges (teeth marks) and a thick, white, greasy coating that looks excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), often soggy (濡 rú) or slow, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is born when the Spleen is too weak to transform food and fluids. Heavy, cold, and raw foods further burden the Spleen, thickening dampness and making it rise to the head. Warm, light meals and gentle movement support Spleen Qi and keep fluids moving, while ginger and cardamom warm the middle burner and dissolve turbid dampness before it can cloud the head.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favor warm, cooked meals and avoid cold, raw, or greasy foods that create dampness. A cup of ginger tea after meals aids digestion. Gentle daily movement like a short walk helps keep Qi and fluids circulating so phlegm does not stagnate.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness triggered by head movement, worse when tired, Persistent fatigue and weakness, Pale or sallow complexion, Heart palpitations, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Standing up too quickly, Skipping meals or poor diet, Excessive worry or overthinking
- **Better with**: Lying down and resting, Warm, cooked, nourishing meals, Gentle movement and fresh air
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi and Blood are the body's fundamental nourishment. When they are deficient, the brain-known as the Sea of Marrow-is not properly fed. Head movements momentarily disrupt the already weak supply of Qi and Blood to the head, causing the room to spin. This type of vertigo tends to come in brief spells but feels worse when you are tired, because there is simply not enough energy and nourishment to steady the senses.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are like the body's fuel. Rest and nourishing food refill the tank, so the brain gets the steady supply it needs and dizziness eases. Overwork, skipping meals, and stress drain the tank faster, leaving the head undernourished and making the spinning worse. Gentle movement helps circulate what Qi and Blood you have without burning them up.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digestible, nourishing foods like bone broth, congee, dates, and goji berries. Avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that weaken the Spleen. Rest when tired and avoid overwork. Gentle practices like Tai Chi or walking help build Qi without depleting it.

### Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, chronic dizziness that feels like the head is empty or hollow, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Tinnitus or gradual hearing loss, Poor memory and difficulty concentrating, Frequent urination at night
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Aging and chronic illness, Excessive sexual activity, Overwork and lack of sleep, Cold, raw foods and iced drinks
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, cooked, nourishing meals, Gentle movement like walking or Tai Chi
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys store the body's deepest vital substance - Essence (Jing). Essence produces marrow, and the brain is known as the "sea of marrow." When Kidney Essence runs low, the sea of marrow becomes empty and the brain and inner ear lose their nourishment. This leaves you with a chronic, hollow-feeling dizziness that appears or worsens when you move your head, often accompanied by a weak, aching back and fading memory.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with little or no coating, possibly with fine cracks. Pulse: fine, deep, and weak at both Chi (rear) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Essence is the body's deepest reserve, replenished slowly and depleted by overwork, aging, and excessive activity. Rest and warm, nutrient-dense foods help rebuild essence, while cold, raw foods and a draining lifestyle further exhaust it. Gentle movement keeps Qi flowing without wasting precious reserves, and acupuncture points like Taixi and Shenshu directly tonify the Kidney's ability to nourish the brain and inner ear.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on deeply nourishing, warm, easy-to-digest foods like bone broths, stews, black sesame, walnuts, and goji berries. Avoid overwork, late nights, and excessive sexual activity, which drain essence. Gentle exercises like Tai Chi or Qigong help preserve essence without depleting it.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture help with BPPV?
Yes, acupuncture can be very helpful for BPPV, both during an acute episode and for preventing recurrences. During a vertigo attack, specific points on the head and neck, like Fengchi (GB20) and Baihui (DU20), can help calm internal wind and settle the dizziness. Between episodes, treatment focuses on correcting the underlying pattern - whether that's subduing Liver Yang, transforming phlegm, or tonifying Qi and Blood - to make the inner ear environment more stable. Many patients report feeling more grounded and less prone to spinning sensations after a course of acupuncture.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
This varies by pattern and chronicity. For an acute BPPV episode, you might feel relief after just 1-3 sessions. However, to address the root imbalance and reduce the chance of recurrence, a course of 8-12 weekly sessions is typical. Deficiency patterns often require a longer commitment - sometimes 3-6 months - to rebuild the body's reserves. Your practitioner will reassess your progress regularly and adjust the frequency as your symptoms improve.

### Can I do the Epley maneuver while taking Chinese herbs?
Absolutely. TCM treatment complements repositioning maneuvers, not replaces them. The Epley or Semont maneuvers are excellent for mechanically moving the crystals back into place. Chinese herbs and acupuncture work on a different level - they aim to correct the internal imbalance that allowed the crystals to dislodge and to clear any residual dizziness. Using both together can give you the best of both worlds: immediate relief from the maneuver and longer-term stability from TCM. Just be sure to inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about all treatments you're receiving.

### Will TCM prevent my BPPV from coming back?
TCM cannot guarantee that BPPV will never recur, but it can significantly reduce the frequency and severity of episodes by addressing the underlying constitutional tendencies. For example, if your BPPV is linked to Liver Yang Rising from chronic stress, herbs and acupuncture that calm the Liver and nourish Yin can make you less susceptible to that upward surge. If phlegm-dampness is the root, dietary changes and Spleen-strengthening herbs can reduce the phlegm load over time. Many patients find that after a course of TCM treatment, their episodes become less frequent and less intense, and their overall sense of balance improves.

### What foods should I avoid if I have BPPV?
Dietary advice depends on your TCM pattern, but some general guidelines apply. For most BPPV patients, it's wise to avoid greasy, fried, and heavily processed foods, as these create dampness and phlegm that can cloud the head. Dairy, sugar, and cold raw foods can also burden the Spleen and promote phlegm. If your pattern involves Liver Yang Rising, also limit spicy foods, alcohol, and caffeine, which can stir up internal wind. Focus on warm, cooked meals with plenty of vegetables, lean proteins, and whole grains. Your practitioner will give you specific recommendations based on your diagnosis.

### Is it safe to combine TCM with my vertigo medications?
In most cases, yes. The medications commonly prescribed for BPPV, such as meclizine for nausea, do not typically interact with Chinese herbs. However, if you are taking any daily medications - especially blood thinners like warfarin - it's crucial to inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor, as some herbs that move blood (like Dang Gui or Chuan Xiong) can have additive effects. Never stop a prescribed medication abruptly; if your symptoms improve with TCM, work with your doctor to adjust dosages gradually. Always bring a complete list of your medications and supplements to your TCM consultation.

### How does TCM explain the residual dizziness after the Epley maneuver?
Even after the crystals are repositioned, some people feel a lingering unsteadiness or mild dizziness. In TCM, this is often due to a remaining disturbance of Qi and Blood flow in the head, or to an underlying deficiency that wasn't corrected. The maneuver cleared the mechanical blockage, but the internal environment - whether it's phlegm-dampness, Liver Yang, or Qi deficiency - still needs attention. Acupuncture and herbs can help settle this residual dizziness by restoring proper circulation and nourishment to the balance organs, helping you feel fully steady again.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for residual dizziness after successful canalith repositioning maneuver in benign paroxysmal positional vertigo: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, et al. Chinese Acupuncture & Moxibustion. 2020;40(3):239-243.*
   This trial found that adding acupuncture significantly reduced residual dizziness scores on the Dizziness Handicap Inventory compared to standard repositioning alone, with no serious adverse events.

2. **Systematic review and meta-analysis of acupuncture for benign paroxysmal positional vertigo**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang Y, et al. Medicine. 2021;100(15):e25432.*
   A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs concluded that acupuncture as an adjunct therapy improved vertigo resolution rates and reduced recurrence compared to repositioning maneuvers alone, though most trials had moderate risk of bias.

3. **Effect of modified Ban Xia Bai Zhu Tian Ma Tang on residual dizziness in BPPV patients: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang H, et al. Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine. 2019;39(2):234-240.*
   Patients receiving the herbal formula for 4 weeks after successful repositioning had significantly faster resolution of residual dizziness and lower anxiety scores than the control group, supporting the phlegm-drying and wind-calming approach.
