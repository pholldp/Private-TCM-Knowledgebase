## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, vomiting is the forceful expulsion of stomach contents through the mouth, usually preceded by nausea. It is a reflex controlled by the brain's vomiting centre and can be triggered by many things - infections like gastroenteritis, food poisoning, motion sickness, pregnancy, migraines, certain medications, or even strong emotions. Diagnosis relies on your history, a physical exam, and sometimes blood tests or imaging to rule out more serious causes like obstruction or neurological problems.

### Treatments
Acute vomiting is often managed with rest, rehydration, and a temporary switch to bland foods. Anti-emetic medications such as ondansetron or promethazine may be prescribed to suppress the symptom, especially if dehydration is a concern. When a specific cause is identified - for example, a bacterial infection or migraine - treatment targets that underlying issue. Most episodes of acute vomiting resolve on their own within a day or two.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, all vomiting is a sign of rebellious Stomach Qi (胃气上逆, wèi qì shàng nì). The Stomach's natural job is to send food and fluids downward; when it rebels, Qi rises and carries stomach contents up and out. This upward rebellion is never the root problem - it is the final common pathway. The real question is: what is pushing the Stomach to rebel?
The trigger can be an external invader, like a sudden chill that directly attacks the Stomach, or an internal accumulation, such as undigested food sitting and fermenting after a heavy meal. It can also come from another organ - most often the Liver. When stress or frustration causes Liver Qi to stagnate, it can rush horizontally and disrupt the Stomach, leading to sour or bitter vomiting that flares with emotional upset.
Long-standing vomiting often points to a deeper weakness. If the Spleen is too weak to transform fluids, they gather as phlegm-fluids that slosh in the stomach and are vomited as clear, watery liquid. If the Stomach's warming Yang is depleted, even a small amount of food feels heavy and is rejected. And if the Stomach's cooling, moistening Yin is dried up, the result is dry heaving with a parched mouth.
Because the quality of the vomit, the timing, and the accompanying sensations all point to a specific pattern, a TCM practitioner can select a treatment that targets the root - not just the symptom. This is why one person's vomiting may clear up quickly with digestive herbs, while another needs weeks of warming, tonifying formulas to rebuild a weakened digestive fire.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner starts by asking what the vomit looks and smells like, when it happens, and what makes it better or worse. Vomiting is always a sign of rebellious Stomach Qi (胃气上逆, wèi qì shàng nì), but the quality of the vomit, the accompanying discomfort, and the tongue and pulse reveal which underlying pattern is driving that rebellion.
If the vomit is sour and foul-smelling, with chunks of undigested food, and the person feels bloated and belches after a heavy meal, Food Stagnation in the Stomach is the likely culprit. The tongue coating will be thick and greasy, and the pulse will feel slippery-both signs of accumulated food and dampness clogging the digestive tract.
When vomiting strikes suddenly after exposure to cold, and the vomit is clear and watery with a feeling of chest tightness and an aversion to cold, Cold invading the Stomach is the diagnosis. The tongue coating is typically white, and the pulse may feel tight or slow, reflecting the external cold pathogen that has directly attacked the stomach and disrupted its downward movement.
Emotional stress that triggers sour or bitter vomiting, belching, and a distended feeling in the ribs and chest points to Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. Here the tongue may be normal or slightly red on the sides, and the pulse often has a wiry quality, indicating constrained Liver energy that is lashing sideways at the stomach.
Vomiting of clear, watery, or frothy saliva along with dizziness and a heavy sensation in the chest suggests Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach and Small intestine. The tongue is usually swollen with a thick, white, greasy coating, and the pulse is slippery or wiry-both signs that fluids are not being transformed properly and are pooling as phlegm.
If even a small amount of food triggers vomiting of thin, clear fluid, and the person looks pale, has cold hands and feet, and feels better with warmth and pressure, Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold is the pattern. The tongue is pale with a white coating, and the pulse is deep, slow, and weak, revealing a deep lack of warming energy.
A less common but distinct picture is Stomach Yin Deficiency, where the person dry heaves or vomits only a small amount, with a very dry mouth and throat. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thready and rapid, indicating that the stomach’s moistening fluids have been damaged over time.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of sour, undigested food, Relief after vomiting, Foul, rotten-smelling belching, Thick greasy or curd-like tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy or fried foods, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Lying down after meals
- **Better with**: Vomiting (relieves stagnation), Light, warm congee, Warm ginger tea, Gentle abdominal massage
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you overeat or consume too many greasy, rich, or hard-to-digest foods, the stomach can't process the volume. The undigested mass sits and ferments, blocking the stomach's natural downward movement. This trapped, stagnant food forces stomach Qi to rebel upward, resulting in vomiting of sour, foul-smelling, undigested food-the body's way of forcefully clearing the blockage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body, thick greasy or curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), may be full (实 shí), especially forceful at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating and heavy foods add to the food mass stuck in the stomach, making the Qi rebel upward more strongly - that's why they trigger vomiting. Vomiting is the body's way of clearing the blockage, so it brings temporary relief. Warm, light foods and ginger tea gently support the stomach's downward movement without adding to the stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: After a bout of vomiting, rest the stomach with clear fluids like warm ginger tea, then slowly reintroduce easily digestible foods such as congee. Avoid greasy, fried, rich, cold, and raw foods until digestion recovers. Chew food thoroughly and stop eating before you feel completely full.

### Cold invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden cramping stomach pain, Vomiting of clear watery fluid, Pain and vomiting relieved by warmth, Feeling of cold in the stomach and cold hands and feet
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Cold weather or drafts
- **Better with**: Warmth, Rest, Warm ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a sudden chill - icy wind, cold drinks, or damp cold weather - hits the body, it can directly attack the Stomach. Cold is a contracting force: it tightens the Stomach muscles and freezes the normal downward flow of Qi. Instead of moving food downward, the Stomach rebels and pushes everything upward, causing a sudden bout of vomiting.
The vomit is typically clear and watery because Cold weakens the digestive fire, leaving fluids unprocessed. The cramping pain that comes with it is the physical sensation of cold constricting the Stomach channel.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, moist, with a thin white slippery coat. Pulse: tight (Jin) and possibly slow (Chi), especially tight at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold is a contracting pathogen that freezes the Stomach Qi, forcing it to rebel upward as vomiting. Warmth relaxes the Stomach, disperses Cold, and restores the normal downward movement of Qi, which is why warmth brings immediate relief.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid all cold and raw foods - no ice water, salads, or smoothies. Drink warm ginger tea throughout the day and keep a hot water bottle on your abdomen. Eat small, frequent, easily digestible meals like congee or soup.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of sour or bitter fluid, Worse with stress or frustration, Distending pain in chest and rib-sides, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Irritability and moodiness
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Alcohol, Greasy or spicy food, Skipping meals, Premenstrual tension
- **Better with**: Emotional calm, Gentle walking, Deep breathing, Warm meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress, frustration, or prolonged tension cause the Liver's Qi to stagnate, it can build up and then attack the Stomach horizontally, disrupting its normal downward movement. This rebellious Stomach Qi rises upward, bringing sour or bitter fluid with it and causing vomiting. The pattern is closely tied to mood, so symptoms flare with emotional upset.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, sides may be redder, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly causes Liver Qi to stagnate and invade the Stomach, so calming the mind and releasing tension are the most effective reliefs. Gentle movement helps Qi flow smoothly, while heavy, greasy foods and alcohol further obstruct Qi and provoke vomiting.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Manage stress with gentle exercise like walking, yoga, or tai chi. Avoid alcohol, coffee, and spicy foods that can aggravate Liver Qi. Eat smaller, regular meals and chew thoroughly. Deep breathing and sighing can help release tension.

### Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach and Small intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fluids-in-the-stomach-and-small-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of thin clear watery fluid, Splashing or gurgling water sounds in the stomach, Dizziness and lightheadedness, No thirst or thirst without desire to drink, Feeling of heaviness in the body, Preference for warmth on the abdomen
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Dairy and greasy foods, Overeating, Cold weather or drafts, Overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Warmth, Warm meals, Rest, Gentle walking, Dry, warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and fails to transform and transport fluids, water accumulates and turns into pathological phlegm-fluids. These fluids lodge in the stomach and small intestine, blocking the normal downward movement of Stomach Qi. The result is vomiting of thin, clear watery fluid, often accompanied by gurgling sounds and a feeling of fullness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, excessively moist with a white slippery coating and teeth marks. Pulse: deep, wiry, slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is driven by Spleen Yang deficiency and retained fluids. Cold and raw foods further damage Spleen Yang and promote fluid accumulation, while warmth supports Yang and helps transform fluids. Rest conserves Qi; overeating overwhelms the already weak digestive system. Damp environments add external dampness to the internal dampness, worsening symptoms, whereas dry warmth helps evaporate excess moisture.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and stews. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods, as well as dairy, which can generate phlegm. Eat small, frequent meals and avoid overeating. Gentle movement like walking supports Spleen Qi, while damp living environments should be avoided.

### Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yang-deficient-and-cold)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of clear watery fluid, Dull cold pain in the upper belly, Pain improves with warmth and pressure, Poor appetite with preference for hot foods, Cold hands and feet, Loose stools and tiredness
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or drafts, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overwork and fatigue, Overeating, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Warmth, Rest, Warm meals, Gentle pressure on the abdomen, Warm ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Stomach's Yang is deficient, it lacks the warmth needed to break down food and send Qi downward. Instead of being transformed, even small amounts of food or drink sit heavily and are rejected upward as thin, clear fluid. The internal cold creates a dull ache that feels better with warmth and pressure, because those temporarily supply the heat the Stomach cannot produce on its own.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks; white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, weak, especially weak at the right middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Stomach Yang is the digestive fire. When it is weak, cold things-whether icy drinks, raw salads, or chilly air-snuff it out further, leaving food undigested and triggering vomiting. Warmth on the belly, hot foods, and ginger tea supply the heat the Stomach lacks, gently coaxing it back to work. Rest conserves the body's limited Qi, while overwork and large meals demand more digestive fire than the Stomach can provide, making symptoms worse. Emotional stress can also knot up Qi, further disturbing the Stomach's already fragile downward flow.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals of warm, cooked foods like congee, soups, and stews. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy dishes. Sip warm ginger tea throughout the day. Keep a hot water bottle over your belly when resting, and protect your midsection from drafts. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry retching or vomiting of only small amounts of thin fluid, Dry mouth and throat with a preference for small sips of water, Dull burning pain or gnawing discomfort in the stomach, Feeling hungry but not wanting to eat
- **Worse with**: Spicy or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overwork and stress, Hot, dry weather
- **Better with**: Small sips of warm water, Moistening foods like pear or congee, Cool, quiet environment, Rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Stomach Yin is the cooling, moistening fluid that allows the stomach to properly receive food and send it downward. When Yin is depleted-often after a prolonged fever, chronic illness, or long-term consumption of drying foods-the stomach loses its lubrication and develops empty heat. This dryness and heat cause the stomach Qi to rebel upward instead of descending, resulting in dry heaving, retching, or vomiting of only small amounts of thin fluid.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating with possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin is cooling and moistening, so anything that generates heat or dries fluids-spicy foods, alcohol, coffee, and hot weather-worsens the deficiency and makes the rebellious Qi more intense. Overwork and stress consume Yin directly. Rest, a cool environment, and moistening foods replenish Yin and help the stomach Qi descend naturally. Small sips of water provide moisture without overloading the already weakened stomach.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent, moistening meals such as congee with pear or lily bulb. Avoid spicy, dry, or fried foods, alcohol, and coffee entirely. Sip warm water or herbal teas like chrysanthemum throughout the day. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork, which further consumes Yin.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture stop vomiting immediately?
In many cases, yes - especially for acute vomiting from food stagnation, a sudden chill, or stress. Acupuncture at points like Neiguan (PC-6) and Zusanli (ST-36) can quickly redirect rebellious Stomach Qi downward. However, immediate relief does not mean the underlying pattern is resolved; follow-up treatment is usually needed to prevent recurrence.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs alongside my anti-nausea medication?
Generally, yes. Herbal formulas that harmonise the Stomach are gentle and can be used alongside conventional anti-emetics. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about everything you are taking. Do not stop prescribed medication abruptly. If you are taking medication for a serious underlying condition, your herbal formula will be adjusted to avoid any potential interactions.

### How do I know if my vomiting is from stress or from food?
Notice the timing and the triggers. Food stagnation vomiting happens after overeating and brings up sour, foul-smelling, undigested food, often with relief afterwards. Stress-related vomiting flares with emotional upset, brings up sour or bitter fluid, and is accompanied by rib-side distension and belching. A TCM practitioner will also look at your tongue and pulse to confirm the pattern.

### Can TCM help with morning sickness during pregnancy?
TCM has a long history of safely managing pregnancy-related nausea and vomiting, but it must be done under the care of a qualified practitioner who specialises in pregnancy. Certain herbs and acupuncture points are avoided during pregnancy. Mild, food-based approaches like ginger tea and small, frequent meals are often recommended first. Always consult your midwife or doctor as well.

### What foods should I avoid if I vomit frequently?
Avoid cold, raw, greasy, spicy, and overly sweet foods, as they burden the digestive system. Dairy and heavy, rich meals can also be problematic. Instead, favour warm, easily digested foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and ginger tea. Eating small, regular meals rather than large ones helps prevent overloading the Stomach.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
For acute vomiting, 1-3 sessions may be enough. For chronic or recurrent patterns, weekly sessions for 4-8 weeks are typical. Herbal medicine is usually taken daily between sessions. Your practitioner will reassess your progress and adjust the treatment plan accordingly.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Stimulation of the wrist acupuncture point P6 for preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting**
   *Journal/Publication: Lee A, Fan LTY. Stimulation of the wrist acupuncture point P6 for preventing postoperative nausea and vomiting. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2009; (2): CD003281.*
   This Cochrane systematic review assessed the effect of PC‑6 stimulation (acupuncture, acupressure, or electro‑acupuncture) on post‑operative nausea and vomiting. The review found that PC‑6 stimulation significantly reduced the incidence of nausea and vomiting compared to sham, making it a safe and effective non‑pharmacological option.

2. **Acupuncture-point stimulation for chemotherapy-induced nausea or vomiting**
   *Journal/Publication: Ezzo J, Richardson MA, Vickers A, Allen C, Dibble SL, Issell BF, et al. Acupuncture-point stimulation for chemotherapy-induced nausea or vomiting. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2006; (2): CD002285.*
   This Cochrane review evaluated acupuncture-point stimulation for nausea and vomiting caused by chemotherapy. The analysis showed that electro‑acupuncture and acupressure at PC‑6 reduced the frequency of acute vomiting, providing evidence for acupuncture as an adjunct in cancer care.
