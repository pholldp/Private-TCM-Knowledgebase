## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is an umbrella term for conditions where blood flow to the heart muscle is suddenly reduced or blocked. It includes unstable angina and heart attacks (myocardial infarction). Typical symptoms are chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, and pain that may radiate to the arm, jaw, or back.
Diagnosis usually involves an electrocardiogram (ECG) to detect heart rhythm abnormalities and blood tests for troponin, a protein released when heart muscle is damaged. Coronary angiography can directly visualize blockages in the arteries. Prompt treatment is critical to restore blood flow and limit heart muscle damage.

### Treatments
Standard treatment aims to quickly restore blood flow to the heart. This often includes aspirin and other antiplatelet drugs, blood thinners, and clot-dissolving medications. Many patients undergo angioplasty with stent placement to open blocked arteries, or coronary artery bypass surgery for more extensive disease. After the acute event, long-term medications such as beta-blockers, statins, and ACE inhibitors are prescribed to reduce the heart's workload, lower cholesterol, and prevent future events.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands acute coronary syndrome primarily as an obstruction of the Heart vessels. The Heart governs blood circulation, and when its vessels become blocked, the flow of Qi and blood to the heart muscle stops, producing the severe, crushing chest pain that defines a heart attack. But the obstruction itself is rarely the whole story - it's usually the end result of years of accumulated imbalances.
The most common culprits in that blockage are blood stasis and phlegm. Blood stasis is exactly what it sounds like: blood that has become sluggish, congealed, or stuck, often from a sedentary lifestyle, chronic stress, or a diet heavy in cold and greasy foods. Phlegm is a thick, sticky substance that forms when the Spleen is too weak to transform fluids properly - it rises and settles in the chest, physically clogging the vessels. These two often combine, creating the Heart Vessel obstructed pattern, which is the most typical TCM diagnosis for acute coronary syndrome.
But TCM also recognizes that a heart attack doesn't happen to a fully healthy heart. Underlying deficiencies set the stage. If the heart's Qi and Yin are depleted - from overwork, chronic illness, or aging - the heart muscle is undernourished and the vessels lose their supple elasticity. If the heart's Yang fire is weak, cold congeals in the chest and blood stagnates. Even emotional stress alone, by causing Liver Qi to stagnate and compress the chest, can trigger a milder version of this pain. So while Western medicine sees one disease process (a blocked artery), TCM sees six different landscapes that led to that blockage - and each requires a different treatment strategy to clear the obstruction and restore the heart's resilience.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
In TCM, a practitioner starts by asking about the exact sensation of the chest pain. The quality of the pain - whether it is crushing, stabbing, heavy, or dull - is the first major clue. A severe, constricting pain that comes on suddenly and feels like something is squeezing the heart from all sides points toward a pattern of Heart Vessel obstructed, the core acute blockage where blood stasis and phlegm have clogged the vessels. The tongue is often dark purple and the pulse feels choppy or knotted.
If the pain is a fixed, stabbing sensation that worsens at night or after rest, the practitioner suspects Heart Blood Stagnation. This pattern is less dramatic than a full vessel obstruction but still indicates sluggish, congealed blood. The tongue shows purple spots or a dark purplish hue, and the pulse is choppy or intermittent. A history of prolonged emotional stress or chronic illness often accompanies this picture.
When the main complaint is a heavy, oppressive weight on the chest rather than sharp pain, and the person feels foggy-headed or phlegmy, the diagnosis leans toward Phlegm in the Chest. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse feels slippery, like beads rolling under the finger. A practitioner will ask about diet, digestion, and whether the sensation worsens in damp weather or after eating rich, fatty foods.
A dull, nagging ache with palpitations, breathlessness, and deep fatigue suggests an underlying Qi and Yin Deficiency. This pattern often appears during the recovery phase, when the body’s reserves are depleted. The tongue may be red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. The practitioner will ask about night sweats, dry mouth, and whether the discomfort eases with rest, all signs of yin and qi being too weak to nourish the heart.
In more severe cases, the pain feels like a cold, crushing weight, and the hands and feet are icy, with a pale face and an overwhelming fear of cold. This signals Heart Yang Deficiency, where the body’s warming fire has faded, allowing cold to congeal the vessels. The pulse is deep, slow, and faint, and the tongue is pale and swollen. The practitioner will ask about cold intolerance and whether the pain is triggered by exposure to cold.
Finally, if the pain is distending and moves around, often flaring up with emotional upset, the pattern is likely Qi Stagnation in the Chest. The tongue may look normal or slightly pale, but the pulse is wiry, like a taut guitar string. The practitioner will explore links to stress, frustration, or grief, and whether the discomfort is relieved by sighing or moving around.

### Heart Vessel obstructed
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-vessel-obstructed)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Crushing or suffocating chest pain, Pain radiates to shoulder, inner arm, or upper back, Cold sweating during pain, Dark or purplish lips, Palpitations and shortness of breath
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or heavy lifting, Cold, windy weather, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Intense anger or emotional stress
- **Better with**: Rest and keeping calm, Gentle warmth on the chest, Light, easy-to-digest meals, Deep, slow breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In acute coronary syndrome, the heart vessels become blocked by a combination of stagnant blood and thick, turbid phlegm. This obstruction cuts off the flow of Qi and blood to the heart muscle, producing the sudden, severe, crushing chest pain that defines a heart attack. The pain often radiates to the shoulder, arm, or back because the blocked vessels cannot nourish the surrounding channels.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dark body with stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: knotted (slow with irregular pauses) or intermittent, choppy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Overexertion drains Qi, leaving the heart too weak to push blood through blocked vessels. Cold causes vessels to constrict and congeals blood, worsening the obstruction. Greasy foods create the sticky phlegm that clogs the vessels. Emotional stress makes Qi surge and stagnate, which directly tightens the chest and intensifies pain. Rest, warmth, and light foods do the opposite: they conserve Qi, keep blood moving smoothly, and prevent new phlegm from forming, giving the heart a chance to recover.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid heavy, greasy, and fried foods that generate phlegm. Keep meals light with plenty of cooked vegetables and moderate whole grains. Gentle movement like walking, once stable, helps Qi and blood flow. Manage emotional stress with slow breathing or meditation, as anger and frustration can worsen stagnation.

### Heart Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Stabbing or pricking chest pain in a fixed location, Pain worsens at night or when lying still, Purple or dark discolouration of lips and nails, Pain radiates to the left shoulder, upper back, or inner arm, Cold hands, palpitations, and a feeling of suffocation
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or air conditioning, Emotional stress and anger, Heavy, greasy, or cold meals, Overwork or physical exhaustion, Smoking
- **Better with**: Gentle walking or tai chi, Warmth on the chest, Deep, slow breathing, Small, warm, easily digested meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the flow of Blood through the vessels of the Heart becomes sluggish or obstructed, the result is a stabbing, knife-like pain that stays in one fixed spot. This stagnation often develops gradually from chronic stress, a sedentary lifestyle, or a diet rich in cold and greasy foods that congeal the Blood. Because Blood moves more slowly when the body is still, the pain typically worsens at night or during rest, and the lack of fresh, nourishing Blood reaching the face and extremities causes the lips, nails, and tongue to turn dark or purplish.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩, Sè) or knotted (结, Jié) at the left Cun position, reflecting obstructed Heart Blood flow.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold causes Blood to congeal and move even more sluggishly, so warmth and gentle movement are essential to keep circulation open. Emotional stress tightens the chest and further obstructs Qi and Blood, while deep breathing releases that tension. Heavy meals divert energy to digestion and can worsen stagnation, whereas small, warm meals support the Heart without overburdening it. Rest and relaxation allow Blood to flow more smoothly, while exhaustion depletes the Qi that drives it.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods that gently move the Blood, such as hawthorn berries, turmeric, ginger, and small amounts of black fungus. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that congeal circulation. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps prevent stagnation, but stop before exhaustion. Manage stress with deep breathing or meditation, and if you smoke, seek support to quit - smoking directly damages the Heart vessels.

### Phlegm in Chest
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-in-chest)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, stuffy, oppressive chest sensation, Coughing with abundant white, sticky phlegm, Nausea or a feeling of heaviness in the body, Poor appetite and a slimy taste in the mouth, Thick, white, greasy coating on the tongue
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid weather, Heavy, greasy, or dairy-rich meals, Overeating or eating late at night, Lying flat, Prolonged inactivity or sitting still
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Gentle, consistent movement, Light, cooked, warm meals, Sitting upright or propped up on pillows
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is too weak to transform fluids, those fluids congeal into phlegm-a thick, sticky substance that can clog the body's channels. In acute coronary syndrome, this phlegm rises and settles in the chest, physically obstructing the heart vessels and the flow of Qi and blood. The result is a heavy, suffocating sensation and dull, oppressive pain, as if the heart is being smothered under a wet blanket.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks; thick white greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), possibly wiry (弦 xián).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm is a product of dampness, so anything that adds dampness-like humid air or greasy food-makes the obstruction worse. Lying flat allows phlegm to settle and constrict the chest, while sitting upright uses gravity to ease breathing. Gentle movement activates the Spleen and Lungs, helping to transform and expel phlegm, while warm, dry conditions and light cooked foods prevent new phlegm from forming.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid dairy, greasy, fried, and cold raw foods, which create more dampness and phlegm. Eat warm, cooked, lightly spiced meals like congee with ginger. Gentle daily movement, such as walking, helps the Spleen transform fluids and prevents stagnation.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, lingering chest discomfort, Worse with exertion, Fatigue and shortness of breath, Palpitations, Dry mouth with little desire to drink, Spontaneous sweating during the day
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Stress and worry, Spicy, drying foods, Hot, dry weather, Insufficient sleep
- **Better with**: Rest and relaxation, Warm, easy-to-digest foods, Gentle walking, Short naps during the day
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi provides the energy the heart needs to pump, and Yin is the cooling, moistening fuel that anchors it. When both are depleted, the heart muscle is undernourished and the vessels lose their supple elasticity. This produces a dull, lingering chest ache rather than a sharp stab, because the heart is struggling from lack of resources rather than from a sudden blockage.
Without enough Qi, every effort feels exhausting and the pulse becomes weak. The Yin deficit allows a low-grade heat to rise, creating dry mouth, night sweats, and a restless warmth in the palms and chest. This pattern often emerges during the recovery phase of an acute coronary event, when the body’s reserves have been drained.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red or pale-red body, thin or absent coating, possibly cracked and dry. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, often rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin are the body’s core reserves. Rest and gentle movement conserve them, while warm, moistening foods help rebuild them. Overexertion, stress, and lack of sleep burn through Qi, and spicy or drying foods generate internal heat that consumes Yin, leaving the heart even more undernourished and prone to palpitations and dull pain.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, warm, easily digested meals like congee with a little honey or pear. Avoid raw, cold, and spicy foods that can further deplete Yin and Qi. Gentle daily walks and short daytime rests help rebuild stamina without overexertion.

### Heart Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Crushing or constricting chest pain, Cold hands and feet, feeling deeply cold, Bright pale or white face, Palpitations that worsen with exertion, Profound fatigue and mental listlessness
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or drafts, Cold foods and iced drinks, Overexertion and fatigue, Fear and anxiety, Excessive sweating
- **Better with**: Lying down in a warm room, Warm environment, Warm, cooked meals and ginger tea, Moxibustion on the lower abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Yang is the fire that warms the body and propels blood through the vessels. When this fire grows weak, cold congeals in the chest, blood stagnates, and the heart loses its nourishing warmth. This produces a severe, crushing chest pain, cold limbs, and a pale face.
The pain is often triggered by cold or exhaustion, as the already depleted Yang cannot overcome the extra demand. Without enough Yang, the heart struggles to maintain a steady rhythm, leading to palpitations and a deep, weak pulse.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak, especially at the left Cun position; may be knotted (slow with irregular pauses).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yang is the body's warming and propelling fire. Cold, whether from weather, food, or internal deficiency, directly extinguishes this fire, causing vessels to constrict and pain to worsen. Rest and warmth conserve Yang, while moxibustion and warm foods actively replenish it. Overexertion and emotional stress further deplete the already weak Yang, so gentle care is essential.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews with ginger, cinnamon, and garlic. Avoid all cold, raw, and iced foods. Keep the body warm, especially the chest and lower back. Gentle walking can help Yang flow, but rest during acute episodes. Moxibustion at home on the lower abdomen (below the navel) can be very supportive.

*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-stagnation-in-chest)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending, migratory chest pain, Worse with emotional stress, Frequent sighing, Irritability or depressed mood, Belching relieves discomfort

- **Better with**: Gentle walking, Deep breathing exercises, Peppermint tea, Emotional calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress and frustration cause the Liver Qi to stagnate, which then compresses the chest and disrupts the smooth flow of Heart Qi. This stagnation creates a sensation of distension, stuffiness, and a dull ache rather than a sharp, fixed pain. The discomfort often moves around and intensifies with emotional upset because the underlying Qi is trapped and unable to circulate freely.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body colour, thin white coating, may be slightly dusky on sides. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially at the left Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress causes Liver Qi to stagnate, which then compresses the chest and leads to distending pain. Movement and deep breathing help Qi flow freely, while peppermint tea disperses stagnant Qi. Avoiding heavy foods prevents further obstruction of Qi circulation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps keep Qi flowing. Sip warm peppermint or chrysanthemum tea to soothe the Liver. Avoid greasy, heavy foods that can clog Qi and make distension worse. Practice slow, deep breathing to release tension in the chest.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM help after I've had a heart attack or stent placement?
Yes, absolutely. TCM is particularly valuable in the recovery phase after a heart attack or stent procedure. Acupuncture and herbs can help reduce residual chest discomfort, improve energy levels, and support the heart's healing. Many patients find that TCM helps them regain stamina and reduces anxiety about another event. It is safe to combine with your prescribed cardiac medications - just make sure both your cardiologist and TCM practitioner know everything you're taking.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs with blood thinners like aspirin or clopidogrel?
Some Chinese herbs, such as Dan Shen, Chuan Xiong, and Dang Gui, have mild blood-moving properties that could theoretically increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs. However, when prescribed by a qualified TCM practitioner who knows your full medication list, the formulas are adjusted for safety. Never stop your prescribed blood thinners abruptly, and always inform both your cardiologist and your TCM practitioner about all medications and herbs you are taking.

### How soon after a stent or bypass surgery can I start acupuncture?
You can usually start acupuncture within a few days to a week after a stent procedure, once your doctor confirms you are stable. For bypass surgery, it's best to wait until the surgical incisions are healing well - typically 2 to 4 weeks. Acupuncture needles are not placed near fresh wounds. Always get clearance from your surgeon or cardiologist first, and tell your acupuncturist about the recent procedure so they can select appropriate points.

### Will I need to take herbs forever?
Not necessarily. The goal of TCM treatment is to correct the underlying imbalance so that your body can maintain health on its own. For acute obstruction patterns, you might take herbs for several months and then taper off once your symptoms and tongue/pulse have normalized. For deep deficiency patterns, some patients benefit from a maintenance dose of a gentle formula long-term, much like taking a daily vitamin. Your practitioner will reassess regularly and adjust the plan.

### Does acupuncture hurt when treating chest pain?
Acupuncture needles are extremely thin - about the width of a hair - and most people feel only a brief pinch or a dull ache when the needle is inserted. Points on the chest like Shanzhong (REN-17) are needled very shallowly and gently. Many patients find the treatment deeply relaxing and even fall asleep during the session. The needles are not placed into the heart or deep into the chest cavity; safety is paramount.

### Can TCM prevent another heart attack?
TCM aims to do exactly that by addressing the root causes that led to the first event. By clearing blood stasis and phlegm, strengthening the heart's Qi and Yang, and calming emotional stress, TCM works to make the heart and vessels more resilient. While no treatment can guarantee prevention, many patients who integrate TCM with conventional care find they have fewer episodes of angina and better overall cardiovascular health. Diet and lifestyle changes are a crucial part of this preventive approach.

### What diet changes should I make to support my heart according to TCM?
Generally, avoid heavy, greasy, and excessively cold or raw foods, which create phlegm and dampness that can clog your vessels. Favour warm, cooked, easily digested meals like soups, stews, and congees. Include heart-nourishing foods such as longan fruit, red dates, whole grains, leafy greens, and moderate amounts of lean protein. If you tend to feel cold, add warming spices like ginger and cinnamon. If you tend toward phlegm, limit dairy and sugar. Your TCM practitioner will give you more specific guidance based on your pattern.
