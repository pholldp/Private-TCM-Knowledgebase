## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways that causes recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing. The airways become swollen and sensitive, and when triggered - by allergens, cold air, exercise, or respiratory infections - the muscles around them tighten, narrowing the passage for air. Diagnosis typically involves lung function tests like spirometry and a review of symptom patterns. It affects people of all ages and ranges from mild intermittent to severe persistent.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment focuses on two main strategies: long-term control medications to reduce airway inflammation (inhaled corticosteroids, leukotriene modifiers, long-acting beta-agonists) and quick-relief rescue inhalers (short-acting beta-agonists) to open the airways during an attack. Severe cases may require oral corticosteroids or biologic therapies targeting specific inflammatory pathways. Avoiding triggers and following an asthma action plan are also key.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Lungs are responsible for descending Qi - they draw breath in and send it downward. When something obstructs this downward movement, the Qi rebels upward and outward, causing the wheezing, coughing, and breathlessness we call asthma. The obstruction can be external, like an invasion of Wind-Cold that suddenly tightens the airways, or internal, like accumulated phlegm clogging the passages. Either way, the Lung Qi cannot flow smoothly.
The most common villain in chronic asthma is phlegm. TCM sees phlegm not just as mucus you cough up, but as a thicker, stickier pathological fluid that can lodge anywhere in the body. The Spleen is the source of phlegm - when its digestive function is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly, and dampness congeals into phlegm. The Lungs then become the storehouse where this phlegm accumulates, creating a constant state of airway obstruction and sensitivity. The nature of the phlegm tells the practitioner everything: yellow and sticky means Heat, white and watery means Cold.
But phlegm alone is often not the full story. Underlying the phlegm is often a deeper deficiency that allowed it to form in the first place. The Kidneys, which are the root of all Qi, must grasp the breath and pull it down deep into the body. When Kidney Qi is weak - often after years of illness or with aging - this grasping function fails, and even mild exertion leaves you gasping for air. Similarly, when Lung and Kidney Yin are depleted, the airways become dry, irritable, and prone to spasmodic wheezing. And when Spleen and Lung Qi are both deficient, there is not enough energy to move fluids, so phlegm gradually accumulates and causes chronic, low-grade wheezing that worsens with fatigue.
This is why two people with the same Western diagnosis of asthma may receive completely different TCM treatments. One person's asthma flares after a cold and brings white watery phlegm - that's Cold-Phlegm. Another person's asthma is worse at night with yellow sticky phlegm and a red face - that's Phlegm-Heat. A third person has been wheezing for decades and now feels breathless just walking across the room, with a weak aching back - that's the Kidneys failing to receive Qi. Each pattern requires its own herbal formula, its own acupuncture points, and its own dietary approach.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the phlegm - its color, thickness, and how easily it comes up. The quality of the mucus is like a compass: yellow and sticky points toward heat, while white and watery points toward cold or deficiency. They also listen to the breathing and ask when the wheezing started and what makes it worse.
If the phlegm is yellow, thick, and difficult to cough out, and the person feels hot, restless, or has a red face, Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs is likely. The tongue is red with a yellow greasy coating, and the pulse feels rapid and slippery - signs of heat and phlegm clogging the airways.
When the phlegm is white, watery, and easily coughed up, and the chest feels tight and full, Cold-Phlegm in the Lungs is suspected. This pattern worsens in cold, damp weather. The tongue is pale with a white slippery coating, and the pulse is slow and slippery, reflecting cold and dampness constricting the Lung Qi.
Chronic mild wheezing with very little phlegm, accompanied by constant fatigue, poor appetite, and loose stools, points to Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency. The tongue is pale and swollen, and the pulse is weak. Here the Spleen fails to transform fluids, so phlegm slowly accumulates, and the Lungs lack the Qi to descend properly.
Long-standing asthma that leaves someone breathless after even slight exertion, with a sore lower back, weak knees, and frequent nighttime urination, suggests the Kidneys are failing to receive Qi. The tongue is pale and the pulse is deep and weak, indicating the body’s deepest energy reserves are depleted and cannot grasp the breath.
If the asthma comes with a dry, hacking cough, scanty sticky sputum, night sweats, and a sensation of heat in the palms and soles, the pattern is Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire. The tongue is red and dry with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. The cooling, moistening Yin is insufficient, allowing deficiency heat to flare up.
An acute attack triggered by a cold draft or a change in weather, with sudden wheezing, chills, body aches, and thin white sputum, points to Wind-Cold invading the Lungs. The tongue coating is thin and white, and the pulse is floating and tight, as the body’s defensive Qi battles an external pathogen at the surface.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thick yellow sticky phlegm, Wheezing and chest tightness, Thirst with desire for cold drinks, Irritability and restlessness, Flushed face and feeling of heat
- **Worse with**: Spicy and greasy food, Hot and humid weather, Emotional stress, Lying flat, Smoke and pollution
- **Better with**: Cool fresh air, Sipping warm water, Sitting upright, Breathing exercises, Cooling foods like pear
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When external heat or internal factors like diet and stress generate Phlegm and Heat, they combine in the Lungs. This sticky, hot phlegm blocks the airways, preventing the Lung Qi from descending smoothly. The result is wheezing, a tight chest, and a cough that produces thick yellow mucus.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, possibly swollen at front, with a yellow greasy coating and red prickles on the front third. Pulse: Slippery and rapid (hua shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Heat is a combination of sticky phlegm and internal heat. Spicy, greasy, and dairy foods fuel more phlegm and heat, while emotional stress stirs up internal fire. Hot, humid environments add external heat. Cooling foods and fresh air help clear heat, while sitting upright and breathing exercises assist the Lung Qi in descending, easing wheezing.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and dairy-heavy foods that generate Phlegm and Heat. Eat cooling, moistening foods like pears, radishes, and water chestnuts. Keep your living space cool and well-ventilated, and practice slow, deep breathing to help Lung Qi descend.

### Cold-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with copious white watery or foamy phlegm, Chest tightness and stuffiness, Feeling cold, cold hands and feet, No thirst, preference for warm drinks, Wheezing with a gurgling or rattling sound in the throat
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp weather, Icy or raw foods and drinks, Overexertion in the cold, Dairy and greasy foods that create phlegm
- **Better with**: Warm, dry air, Warm drinks and soups, Ginger or cinnamon tea, Rest and keeping covered
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body’s warming Yang energy is too weak to transform fluids, thin watery phlegm collects in the Lungs. Cold then congeals this phlegm into a sticky obstruction that blocks the airways. The Lung Qi cannot descend smoothly, so it rebels upward, causing wheezing, chest tightness, and a gurgling cough with copious white phlegm.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and swollen, often with teeth marks, and a thick white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and slow (迟 chí), sometimes wiry at the Lung position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and dampness directly feed the internal cold-phlegm, making it thicker and stickier, which blocks the Lungs even more. Warmth, on the other hand, thins the phlegm and helps the Lung Qi descend naturally, easing wheezing and opening the chest.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the chest and back warm, especially in cold or damp weather. Sip warm fluids like ginger tea throughout the day and avoid icy drinks and raw salads. A light but warming diet-think soups, stews, and cooked grains-helps the Lungs stay clear.

### Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Shortness of breath on exertion, Thin watery white phlegm, Poor appetite and bloating, Fatigue and weak voice, Loose stools, Pale face
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Cold raw foods, Damp or cold weather, Worry and overthinking
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm cooked meals, Gentle exercise, Warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is the source of phlegm, and the Lung is its storehouse. When Spleen Qi is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly, leading to dampness and phlegm accumulation. Lung Qi deficiency then fails to descend and disperse, so phlegm obstructs the airways, causing chronic mild wheezing and shortness of breath that worsen with exertion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a thin white slippery coating. Pulse: weak overall, especially empty at the right cun (Lung) and right guan (Spleen) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Qi is easily damaged by cold, raw foods, and overthinking, which creates more dampness and phlegm. Overexertion depletes Lung Qi, worsening breathlessness. Rest, warmth, and gentle exercise preserve and gently stimulate Qi, while warm cooked foods support Spleen's transforming function, reducing phlegm at its source.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews; avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that weaken Spleen Qi. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi helps Qi flow without depleting it. Rest adequately and avoid overexertion.

### Kidneys failing to receive Qi
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidneys-failing-to-receive-qi)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Exhaling feels long, inhaling feels short and insufficient, Breathlessness worsens with any physical activity, Lower back and knee weakness and soreness, Fatigue, low stamina, and quiet weak voice, Frequent urination at night, Spontaneous sweating with exertion
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Physical overexertion, Stress and worry, Late nights and overwork, Raw or cold foods
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warmth on the lower back, Eating warm, easily digestible foods, Gentle deep breathing exercises, Moxibustion on the lower back
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys are the root of Qi, responsible for pulling inhaled air deep into the body. After years of asthma or chronic illness, the Kidney Qi becomes too weak to grasp the breath, so inhaling feels shallow and incomplete while exhaling is easier. This leaves you breathless at the slightest effort, with a constant sense that the breath won't go all the way down.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks on the edges, and a thin white moist coating. Pulse: deep, weak, and especially feeble at the chi (rear) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Yang is the body's foundational warmth; cold depletes it further, while warmth and rest help preserve it. Overexertion drains Kidney Qi, which is already too weak to grasp the breath. Gentle movement and nourishing foods support Kidney function and Qi reception, breaking the cycle of fatigue and breathlessness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like stews, soups, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, and dairy-heavy foods that can weaken digestion and create phlegm. Gentle movement such as Tai Chi or slow walking helps build Qi without draining it. Keep the lower back warm with layers or a heat pack, and prioritize rest to avoid overexertion.

### Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-and-kidney-yin-deficiency-with-empty-fire)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry cough with scanty, sticky phlegm that is hard to expectorate, Wheezing and breathlessness that worsen on exertion, Night sweats and afternoon low-grade fever or heat sensation, Lower back soreness and weak knees, Malar flush (red cheeks) and heat in palms and soles, Dry throat, hoarse voice, and thirst for small sips
- **Worse with**: Late nights and overwork, Spicy, fried, or drying foods, Dry, heated indoor air, Anger and frustration
- **Better with**: Cool, humid air, Rest and early nights, Moistening foods (pear, lily bulb), Gentle diaphragmatic breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Yin is the body's cooling and moistening resource. When Lung and Kidney Yin are both depleted, the Lungs lose their ability to descend Qi smoothly and become dry and irritable, triggering a dry, spasmodic wheeze. The Kidneys can no longer anchor Qi downward, so breathlessness worsens with even mild activity.
The deficiency creates an internal Empty Fire - a smouldering heat that rises to the chest and throat. This heat thickens the little phlegm there is, making it sticky and hard to cough out, and causes the night sweats and afternoon warmth that define this pattern.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin) and rapid, especially weak at both rear (Kidney) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin is the body's coolant and moisture reserve. Late nights, overwork, and emotional strain consume Yin and allow Empty Fire to flare upward, which dries the Lungs and tightens the airways. Spicy and fried foods add heat directly. Cool, moist air and Yin-nourishing foods replenish what is missing, calm the internal fire, and help the Lungs stay supple and open.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Build meals around moistening, Yin-nourishing foods: pears, lily bulb, white fungus, tofu, and a little high-quality pork or duck. Avoid spicy, fried, or roasted foods, alcohol, and coffee - they fan the empty fire. Go to bed early and avoid overwork; gentle breathing exercises like qigong can help the Kidneys grasp the Qi.

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden wheezing and cough after exposure to cold, Chills and aversion to cold, Thin, white, watery phlegm, Nasal congestion with clear runny discharge, Absence of sweating
- **Worse with**: Cold, windy weather, Cold drinks and raw foods, Overexertion in the cold, Drafts and air conditioning
- **Better with**: Staying warm and covered, Rest and sleep, Warm ginger tea, Steam inhalation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Wind and Cold invade the body's surface, they block the Lung's ability to diffuse and descend Qi. The Lungs are the first organ to be affected by external pathogens, and the sudden constriction causes the airways to tighten, triggering acute wheezing and cough. The Cold congeals fluids into thin, white, watery phlegm, making breathing more laboured. This pattern often appears as an acute asthma attack after a chill or exposure to cold wind.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (fu) and tight (jin).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Cold constricts the Lung's Qi and blocks its normal flow, which directly causes the sudden wheezing and cough. Warmth and rest help relax the exterior and restore the Lung's ability to diffuse and descend. Cold, wind, and raw foods reinforce the constriction and keep the pathogen trapped, making the attack worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep warm and avoid drafts. Sip warm ginger tea with a little brown sugar to help release the exterior. Eat light, warm, cooked foods like congee and avoid all raw, cold, or iced items that can worsen the Cold. Rest and allow the body to recover naturally.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM cure asthma?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalances that make your airways reactive. For many people, this means a significant reduction in the frequency and severity of attacks - sometimes to the point where they no longer need daily controller medication. However, 'cure' is a strong word; it's more accurate to say that TCM can bring about long-term remission by strengthening the Lungs, Spleen, and Kidneys so that triggers no longer provoke the same response.

### How long does it take for herbs to work?
During an acute flare-up, herbs can begin to ease wheezing and open the chest within a day or two. For chronic, long-standing asthma, the first signs of improvement - like less morning phlegm or fewer nighttime awakenings - often appear within 2-4 weeks. Full constitutional change takes months, but the trajectory is usually steady improvement.

### Is acupuncture safe during an asthma attack?
Acupuncture is generally very safe, but during a severe acute asthma attack, your priority must be your rescue inhaler and seeking medical help if breathing is labored. Acupuncture points like Dingchuan (EX-B-1) and Lieque (LU-7) can be used to support breathing in mild to moderate situations, but they are not a substitute for emergency medication. See the Safety section for red-flag symptoms that require urgent care.

### Can I still use my inhaler while taking Chinese herbs?
Absolutely. Never stop or reduce your prescribed asthma medications without consulting your doctor. TCM herbs and acupuncture are complementary - they work alongside your inhalers to reduce inflammation and phlegm over time. As your condition improves, your doctor may be able to adjust your medication dosage, but this must be a joint decision.

### What foods should I avoid if I have asthma?
The biggest culprits in TCM are dairy, cold and raw foods, and greasy or fried items, all of which tend to generate phlegm. Ice-cold drinks and ice cream are particularly problematic because they chill the Spleen and Lungs, encouraging phlegm formation. Spicy, hot foods can aggravate Phlegm-Heat patterns. Focus on warm, cooked meals - soups, stews, and lightly steamed vegetables - and see the Diet section for more detail.

### Are Chinese herbs safe for children with asthma?
Yes, when prescribed by a qualified practitioner who adjusts the dosage for the child's age and weight. Pediatric asthma often responds very well to TCM, especially when there is a strong phlegm component. Herbs can be given as powders, teas, or even in honey-based pastes to make them palatable. Always work with a practitioner experienced in pediatric care.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for chronic asthma** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000008.pub2))
   *Journal/Publication: McCarney RW, Brinkhaus B, Lasserson TJ, Linde K. Acupuncture for chronic asthma. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2004, Issue 1. Art. No.: CD000008.*
   Cochrane systematic review evaluating the effects of acupuncture in patients with chronic asthma. The review included 12 randomized controlled trials and found that acupuncture may lead to clinically relevant improvements in symptom scores and quality of life, though the evidence was limited by small sample sizes and variable trial quality.

2. **Ding Chuan Tang, a Chinese herb decoction, could improve airway hyper-responsiveness in stabilized asthmatic children: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.20508))
   *Journal/Publication: Hsu CH, Lu CM, Chang TT. Pediatric Pulmonology. 2006;41(12):1161-1168.*
   This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial assessed the effect of Ding Chuan Tang on airway hyper-responsiveness in 60 children with stable asthma. After 12 weeks, the herbal group showed significant improvement in methacholine challenge test results and reduced asthma symptom scores compared to placebo, with no serious adverse events.

3. **Chinese herbal medicine for asthma: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmed.2010.08.009))
   *Journal/Publication: Shergis JL, Wu L, Zhang AL, Guo X, Lu C, Xue CC. Respiratory Medicine. 2010;104(12):1772-1783.*
   A systematic review of 27 randomized controlled trials involving over 2,400 patients. The review found that Chinese herbal medicine, used alone or as an add-on to conventional therapy, significantly improved asthma symptoms and lung function compared to placebo or standard care. The authors noted that methodological quality was generally low, warranting cautious interpretation.
