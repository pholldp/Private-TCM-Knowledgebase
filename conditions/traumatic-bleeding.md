## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, traumatic bleeding occurs when blood vessels are damaged by physical injury, allowing blood to escape into surrounding tissues or outside the body. The severity depends on the size and type of vessel involved - capillary bleeding oozes slowly, venous bleeding flows steadily, and arterial bleeding spurts with each heartbeat. Beyond the immediate blood loss, the body mounts a complex clotting response to seal the wound, followed by inflammation and tissue repair.
Diagnosis is primarily visual, though imaging like ultrasound or CT scans may be needed to detect internal bleeding. Treatment focuses on controlling hemorrhage through direct pressure, wound closure, and sometimes surgical repair, with blood transfusions for severe loss. While this approach is highly effective for acute management, it often leaves the patient to recover from fatigue, anemia, and lingering pain on their own.

### Treatments
Standard Western treatment begins with direct pressure to stop bleeding, followed by cleaning and dressing the wound. Deeper wounds may require sutures, staples, or surgical closure. If blood loss is significant, intravenous fluids or blood transfusions are given. Pain is managed with over-the-counter analgesics, and antibiotics are prescribed if there is a risk of infection. Tetanus vaccination may be updated. For internal bleeding or large hematomas, surgical drainage may be needed.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, traumatic injury directly damages the blood vessels and channels, causing blood to spill out of its normal pathways. This extravasated blood immediately becomes a form of stagnant blood (瘀血, yū xuè), which blocks the flow of Qi and fresh blood through the local area. That is why bruising appears dark and purple, and why pain after an injury is often fixed and stabbing - the stagnant blood is stuck in the tissues.
The body's response to the trauma determines which pattern develops. If inflammation sets in or external heat enters through broken skin, it can penetrate deep into the blood level.
This heat agitates the blood, making it move recklessly and burst out of the vessels, producing bleeding that is bright red, profuse, and accompanied by feelings of heat, thirst, and restlessness. This is the Heat in the Blood pattern, an excess condition where the blood is being pushed out rather than leaking passively.
When the injury causes significant blood loss, the picture shifts to deficiency. The body loses not only blood but also the Qi that moves with it, weakening the Spleen's ability to produce new blood and to hold blood within the vessels. This results in a slow, persistent ooze rather than a gush, along with extreme fatigue, a pale complexion, and dizziness.
The Spleen is the organ system most responsible for transforming food into blood and for keeping blood in its proper place, so once it is depleted, even minor wounds can bleed longer than they should.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by looking at the wound itself and asking what the bleeding looks like and how the injury feels. The color and consistency of the blood, the nature of any pain, and how the person feels overall are the first clues that separate one pattern from another.
When the blood is dark, purplish, or clotted and the area is swollen with a sharp, fixed pain that does not move, that points toward Blood Stagnation. The tongue often appears dusky or shows purple spots, and the pulse feels choppy or wiry. This pattern reflects blood that has leaked out of the vessels and become stuck in the tissues, blocking the channels.
If the bleeding is bright red and seems quite forceful, and the person feels hot, thirsty, or restless, the picture shifts to Heat in the Blood. The tongue is red with a yellow coating, and the pulse is rapid. Here the injury has stirred up internal heat or inflammation has entered the blood level, making the blood move recklessly and spill out.
When the person looks pale, feels weak, dizzy, or short of breath after losing blood, and the bleeding is a slow ooze rather than a gush, Qi and Blood Deficiency is likely. The tongue is pale and plump, and the pulse is thin and weak. The body's vital energy and blood have been drained, so it can no longer hold blood inside the vessels.
A less common but important picture is Spleen Blood Deficiency, where the bleeding may be chronic or recurrent and comes with poor appetite, bloating, and loose stools. The tongue is pale with a thin coat, and the pulse is weak. Here the spleen's ability to produce and govern blood has been undermined, so even a small wound can keep seeping.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain at the injury site, Dark purplish bruising or swelling, Bleeding that is dark or clotted, Pain worsens with pressure and at night
- **Worse with**: Cold exposure, Prolonged inactivity, Direct pressure on the injury, Cold or raw foods
- **Better with**: Warm compresses (after bleeding stops), Gentle movement, Foods like turmeric and ginger, Rest with periodic repositioning
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a trauma ruptures blood vessels, blood spills out of its normal pathways and pools in the tissues. This extravasated blood becomes stagnant, forming what TCM calls stagnant blood (瘀血 yū xuè). The stagnant blood blocks the flow of Qi and fresh blood through the channels, causing a fixed, stabbing pain that feels worse with pressure. The bleeding that results from this pattern is often dark or clotted, and the area swells with a dusky, purplish bruise.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body, possible stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè) or wiry (弦 xián).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold constricts the vessels and congeals blood, which directly worsens stagnation and prolongs pain and bruising. Warmth and gentle movement, on the other hand, encourage Qi and blood to flow, helping the body reabsorb stagnant blood and heal. Rest is essential for initial recovery, but too much stillness can cause Qi to stagnate further, so a balance of rest and light activity is best.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Once the acute bleeding is controlled, apply warm compresses to promote local blood circulation and resolve bruising. Gentle movement, as tolerated, helps prevent further stagnation. Include blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, hawthorn, and a small amount of black vinegar in your meals. Avoid cold or raw foods that can constrict and congeal blood.

### Heat in the Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heat-in-the-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bright red bleeding, Feeling of internal heat, worse at night, Restlessness and irritability, Thirst with desire for cool drinks, Flushed red face
- **Worse with**: Spicy or fried foods, Alcohol, Hot weather or saunas, Emotional agitation
- **Better with**: Cooling compresses on the wound, Drinking cool water, Rest in a cool room, Eating cucumber or watermelon
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a wound becomes inflamed or when external heat enters the body through broken skin, it can penetrate deep into the blood level. This heat agitates the blood, making it move recklessly and burst out of the vessels, which produces bleeding that is bright red and often profuse. The body also shows signs of internal heat, such as thirst and restlessness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with red spots or prickles, thin yellow dry coating. Pulse: rapid (数 shu) and wiry (弦 xian), forceful.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heat in the Blood is driven by excess heat that makes blood move recklessly. Spicy foods, alcohol, and hot environments add more heat, worsening the condition. Cooling foods, rest, and cool compresses directly counteract that heat, calming the blood and helping it stay within the vessels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the wound clean and avoid spicy, greasy, or heating foods like chilli, lamb, and alcohol. Drink plenty of cool water and eat cooling foods such as cucumber, watermelon, and mung beans. Rest in a cool, calm environment and avoid hot baths or saunas.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, slow-to-stop oozing bleeding, Extreme fatigue and weakness, Pale complexion and lips, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Shortness of breath on slight exertion, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or heavy physical work, Excessive worry and overthinking, Skipping meals or irregular eating, Cold or damp environment, Further blood loss (e.g., donating blood)
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, nourishing and easily digestible foods, Gentle movement like walking, Keeping warm, Avoiding further blood loss
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When an injury causes significant blood loss, the body loses not just the red fluid but also the vital energy (Qi) that moves with it. This double loss leaves the body too weak to properly close and seal the damaged vessels, so bleeding may continue as a slow ooze or the wound takes much longer to stop. The lack of Qi also means the Spleen cannot generate new Blood efficiently, leading to a cycle of ongoing weakness and poor healing.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, may be thin or have teeth marks, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine, weak, and thready.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Rest and warm, nourishing foods directly supply the raw materials to rebuild Qi and Blood, while overexertion and stress consume Qi, making it harder for the body to contain bleeding and heal. Cold constricts and slows circulation, further weakening the Spleen’s ability to generate Blood.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, easily digestible foods like bone broths, congee with red dates and goji berries, and well-cooked vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Gentle movement such as walking or stretching helps Qi circulate without overexertion. Prioritize rest and sleep to allow the body to rebuild.

### Spleen Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale face and lips, Poor appetite and loose stools, Fatigue and weakness, Dizziness or light-headedness, Prolonged oozing rather than profuse bleeding, Scanty or light menstrual blood (in women)
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or heavy physical work, Skipping meals or irregular eating, Raw, cold, or greasy foods, Excessive worry and overthinking
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, nourishing and easily digestible foods, Gentle movement like walking, Eating at regular times
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When an injury causes significant or repeated blood loss, the Spleen's ability to produce new blood and to hold blood within the vessels becomes weakened. The Spleen governs the transformation of food into Qi and Blood, and its Qi is responsible for keeping blood in the channels. Once depleted, the Spleen can no longer contain blood, leading to persistent oozing or easy bruising, along with the classic signs of Spleen deficiency such as poor appetite and fatigue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, possibly thin body, with a thin white coating. Pulse: Fine (thin) and choppy, weak at the right Guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen transforms food into Qi and Blood and relies on a steady supply of warm, cooked nourishment to function. Overwork, stress, and irregular eating deplete Spleen Qi further, making it harder to produce blood and contain it in the vessels. Rest and a gentle, nourishing diet rebuild Spleen Qi and Blood, gradually restoring the body's ability to stop bleeding and recover from injury.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals that are easy to digest - congees with red dates, goji berries, and a little chicken or beef are ideal. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Prioritize rest and gentle walking; overexertion will further drain Qi and blood.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM stop bleeding from a fresh wound quickly?
Yes, certain topical herbal powders like Qi Li San can be applied directly to a clean wound to help stop bleeding and reduce pain. However, for severe or spurting bleeding, apply direct pressure and seek emergency care first. TCM is best used after initial first aid to speed healing and prevent stagnation.

### Is it safe to use Chinese herbs on an open wound?
Many traditional trauma formulas are designed for external use on open wounds to stop bleeding and prevent infection. However, only use sterile, properly prepared herbal powders from a qualified practitioner. Never apply raw herbs or homemade pastes to an open wound, as this can introduce bacteria.

### How does acupuncture help with bleeding?
Acupuncture does not directly stop bleeding from a wound. Instead, it regulates the body's internal systems to address the underlying pattern - for example, clearing heat that makes blood move recklessly, or strengthening the Spleen's ability to hold blood in the vessels. It is also very effective for reducing pain and swelling from bruising and stagnant blood after an injury.

### What if I am already taking blood thinners?
Inform your TCM practitioner immediately. Some herbs used for traumatic bleeding, such as Dang Gui or Hong Hua, can thin the blood further and increase bleeding risk. Your practitioner will adjust the formula to avoid these herbs and focus on tonifying Qi or using milder blood-moving agents. Always tell both your doctor and your TCM practitioner about all medications you are taking.

### Can TCM help with bruising after an injury?
Yes, TCM excels at resolving bruises. Herbs and acupuncture that move stagnant blood can dramatically speed up the fading of dark bruises and relieve the associated pain. Topical liniments and herbal compresses are often used alongside internal formulas to work directly on the injured area.

### Will TCM help if I feel weak and dizzy after losing blood?
Absolutely. This is a classic sign of Qi and Blood Deficiency after an injury. TCM uses nourishing herbs like Dang Gui, Huang Qi, and Bai Zhu to rebuild blood and energy, often in formulas like Ba Zhen Tang. Most patients notice their energy returning within 1-2 weeks of starting treatment, with full recovery depending on the amount of blood lost.

### How soon after an injury should I start TCM treatment?
For acute injuries, TCM can be started immediately after initial first aid and wound cleaning. The sooner stagnant blood is moved and swelling is reduced, the faster the recovery. For chronic slow-healing wounds or persistent fatigue after blood loss, starting TCM at any point can help, but earlier treatment typically yields quicker results.

### Are there any dietary changes I should make after a bleeding injury?
Yes. Focus on warm, easily digestible foods that nourish blood, such as bone broth, dark leafy greens, lean red meat, and cooked root vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, which can weaken the Spleen and slow recovery. Spicy and hot foods should be avoided if the wound is inflamed, as they can aggravate heat in the blood.

   *Journal/Publication: Chen Y, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for acute soft tissue injury: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Complementary Therapies in Medicine. 2013;21(4):381-390.*
   This systematic review included 18 RCTs and concluded that Chinese herbal medicine, particularly formulas containing Xue Jie and Ru Xiang, was more effective than placebo or conventional care alone in reducing pain and swelling after traumatic injury.
