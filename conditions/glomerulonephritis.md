## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Glomerulonephritis is inflammation of the glomeruli, the tiny filtering units inside the kidneys. It can strike suddenly (acute) or develop slowly over years (chronic). Common triggers include infections, autoimmune diseases, and genetic factors. Symptoms often include blood or protein in the urine, swelling (edema) around the eyes and limbs, high blood pressure, and fatigue. Diagnosis relies on urine tests, blood work, and sometimes a kidney biopsy.

### Treatments
Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Antibiotics clear underlying infections. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressants calm the immune attack in autoimmune cases. Blood pressure medications like ACE inhibitors protect the kidneys and reduce protein loss. Diuretics help manage fluid buildup. In advanced cases, dialysis or transplant may become necessary. Dietary changes - low salt, controlled protein - are also prescribed.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Kidney is the foundation of water metabolism and stores essence. When its function is disrupted, fluids overflow, leading to edema and proteinuria. The name 'Kidney Wind' (肾风, shèn fēng) comes from the Huang Di Nei Jing, where it describes a condition of facial puffiness and back pain caused by wind lodging in the Kidney. This classical understanding highlights the sudden onset and the involvement of the Lung and Spleen in water metabolism.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking when the kidney inflammation started and what the urine looks like. Sudden puffiness around the eyes and frothy urine (泡沫尿, pào mò niào) soon after a cold or sore throat points toward a Wind‑Damp invasion. If the urine is dark, cloudy, or visibly bloody, with a burning sensation and a yellow greasy tongue coat, the picture shifts toward Damp‑Heat in the Lower Burner.
When the condition has been simmering for weeks or months, the focus turns to the Spleen and Kidney. Chronic, heavy‑limb edema, a sallow complexion, loose stools, and a pale tongue with a white greasy coat suggest Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. If there is also deep lower‑back soreness, frequent nighttime urination, and a deep weak pulse, the root is more likely Spleen and Kidney Qi Deficiency.
In long‑standing cases, a person may feel drained yet also notice dry mouth, night sweats, and a red tongue with little coating. This combination of fatigue and heat‑dryness signals Qi and Yin Deficiency, where both the body’s vital energy and its cooling, moistening resources have been worn down. The tongue and pulse are essential here: a red tongue with a scanty coat and a thin, rapid pulse confirm the pattern.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Damp
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-damp)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden facial and limb edema, Frothy, bubbly urine (proteinuria), Heaviness and soreness in the limbs, Joint pain that moves from place to place, Symptoms worsen with damp or cold weather, Aversion to wind
- **Worse with**: Damp or cold weather, Exposure to wind, Greasy, rich, or spicy foods, Overwork or lack of rest, Cold, raw foods and drinks
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Rest and avoiding wind, Light, diuretic foods (barley, adzuki), Gentle movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Damp invades the body from the exterior, often after a recent cold or exposure to damp weather. It obstructs the flow of Qi and fluids, particularly affecting the Lung and Spleen, which manage water distribution. When the Kidneys fail to regulate fluids under this external attack, sudden edema develops, especially in the face and limbs, and the urine becomes frothy due to protein leaking out. The heaviness and shifting joint discomfort are classic signs of Dampness and Wind lodging in the channels.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, white greasy coating, may be slightly swollen. Pulse: floating (浮 fú) and soggy (濡 rú) or moderate (缓 huǎn).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Damp invades from the exterior, so avoiding wind and dampness prevents further attack while warmth and dryness help expel it. Diuretic foods and herbs drain Dampness through urine, reducing edema and heaviness. Rest conserves Qi to fight the pathogen, whereas overwork depletes Qi and allows Dampness to linger. Heavy, greasy, or cold foods create more internal Dampness, worsening the condition.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep warm and avoid damp, drafty environments. Eat light, easily digested foods like barley, adzuki beans, and lightly cooked vegetables to help drain dampness. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that create more dampness. Gentle movement like walking can help Qi flow, but rest when fatigued.

### Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-lower-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark, scanty, burning urine, Frothy or turbid urine, sometimes with visible blood, Edema, especially of the face and limbs, Low back soreness and a heavy sensation in the legs, Thirst with no desire to drink much, Body feels hot, especially in the afternoon
- **Worse with**: Greasy, rich, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overwork or lack of rest
- **Better with**: Drinking plenty of water, Cooling foods (mung beans, cucumber), Rest, Cool environment, Light, bland diet
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Dampness and Heat combine and settle in the lower burner-the bladder and kidneys-they obstruct the normal flow of fluids. The Heat agitates the blood, causing blood to leak into the urine (hematuria), while the Dampness creates turbidity and frothy urine. This often follows an unresolved infection, leading to sudden edema and the burning, scanty urination typical of acute glomerulonephritis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, slightly swollen, with a thick yellow greasy coating concentrated at the root. Pulse: slippery (Hua) and rapid (Shu), more pronounced at the Chi position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat is a combination of excess moisture and heat in the body. Spicy, greasy, and sweet foods, as well as alcohol, generate more internal heat and dampness, worsening the condition. Drinking water and eating cooling, bland foods help drain dampness and clear heat. Rest conserves Qi, allowing the body to resolve the pathogen, while a cool environment reduces external heat that can aggravate internal heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, cooling foods like barley, mung beans, cucumber, and watermelon. Avoid spicy, greasy, and sweet foods that create dampness and heat. Drink plenty of water to help flush the urinary tract. Rest adequately to support the body's recovery.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heaviness of the body and limbs, Loose stools or diarrhoea, Poor appetite with abdominal bloating, Fatigue that worsens after eating, Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and white greasy coating
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods and drinks, Greasy, rich, or spicy foods, Damp, humid weather, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Mental overwork and worry
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Rest, Gentle abdominal massage, Warm drinks like ginger tea, Warm, dry environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen transforms and transports fluids. When Spleen Qi is weak, fluids are not processed properly and accumulate as dampness. This dampness settles in the kidneys and tissues, causing chronic puffiness, frothy urine, and heavy limbs. The Spleen's impaired holding function may also allow protein to leak into the urine.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), soft and weak, especially at the right Guan position; may be slippery (Hua) or deep (Chen) and weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods damage Spleen Yang, making it harder to transform fluids, which worsens dampness. Greasy and sweet foods directly create more dampness. Rest and warmth conserve Spleen Qi, while gentle movement helps circulate Qi to transform dampness. Dry weather reduces external dampness burden.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and sweet foods that create dampness. Keep the abdomen warm and get gentle daily exercise like walking to help Qi move, but don't overexert.

### Spleen and Kidney Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-kidney-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Persistent mild edema, often worse in the morning, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Poor appetite and loose stools, Frequent urination, especially at night, Cold hands and feet, Pale or sallow complexion
- **Worse with**: Overwork and prolonged standing, Cold, raw foods and drinks, Cold, damp environments
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Rest, Keeping the lower back warm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming and transporting fluids, while the Kidney governs water metabolism. When both organ systems are Qi-deficient, water cannot be properly handled and begins to accumulate, leading to the persistent mild edema seen in glomerulonephritis.
The Kidney's Qi also fails to secure essence, so protein and other vital substances leak into the urine, creating frothiness. Meanwhile, the Spleen's weakness produces fatigue, poor appetite, and loose stools, and the Kidney's weakness shows up as a deep soreness in the lower back and knees.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale and swollen body with tooth marks, white moist or slippery coating. Pulse: Deep, weak, and fine; the right Guan (Spleen) and both Chi (Kidney) positions are especially weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overwork and standing drain Qi further, especially from the Kidneys, while cold and raw foods extinguish the Spleen's digestive fire, worsening fluid accumulation. Warmth, rest, and cooked nourishment gently restore the Spleen and Kidney's ability to transform and transport water, gradually reducing edema and fatigue.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy items that further weaken the Spleen. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi supports Qi circulation, but rest thoroughly when tired.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Persistent tiredness and weakness, worse with exertion, Dry mouth and throat with little desire to drink, Night sweats and warm palms, soles, and chest, Mild, lingering puffiness that does not pit deeply, Dizziness and a reluctance to speak, Scanty, dark urine
- **Worse with**: Overwork and prolonged stress, Greasy, rich, or spicy foods, Hot, dry weather or overheated rooms, Late nights and insufficient sleep
- **Better with**: Rest, Moistening foods (pears, honey, congee), Gentle movement, Cool environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When glomerulonephritis lingers, the body's foundational Qi and moistening Yin fluids are gradually worn down. Qi deficiency weakens the Kidney's ability to manage water, so mild puffiness persists and fatigue becomes a constant companion. At the same time, Yin deficiency creates an internal dryness and gentle heat that shows up as night sweats, a dry mouth, and a reddish, thinly coated tongue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red or pale-red body with a thin or absent coating, dry surface, possibly cracked. Pulse: fine, weak, and often rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin Deficiency is a state of deep depletion where the body's energy and cooling fluids are running low. Rest and nourishing, moist foods help rebuild these reserves, while overwork, heat, and drying substances burn through them even faster, worsening fatigue, dryness, and the sensation of internal warmth.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on gently moistening, easy-to-digest foods like congee, steamed pears, and honey water. Avoid spicy, fried, or drying foods that steal Yin. Prioritize rest and a consistent sleep schedule; pushing through exhaustion further depletes Qi and Yin.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM really help with proteinuria and blood in the urine?
Yes. In TCM, proteinuria is seen as a leakage of the body's essential substances due to weakened Spleen or Kidney Qi. Blood in the urine often signals Heat in the Blood or Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner. Herbal formulas like Ba Zheng San for Damp-Heat or Shen Ling Bai Zhu San for Spleen Deficiency are specifically designed to stop this leakage by addressing the root cause. Many patients see a reduction in foam and blood within weeks, though complete normalization may take longer.

### How does acupuncture work for kidney inflammation?
Acupuncture helps regulate the flow of Qi and fluids in the body. Points like Fuliu KI-7 and Yinlingquan SP-9 encourage proper water metabolism and drainage, while points like Shenshu BL-23 and Zusanli ST-36 strengthen the Kidney and Spleen. By calming inflammation and supporting organ function, acupuncture can reduce edema, improve energy, and complement herbal treatment.

### Can I take Chinese herbs alongside my prescribed steroids or blood pressure medication?
Yes, but only under the guidance of both your TCM practitioner and your nephrologist. Some Chinese herbs have diuretic or immune-modulating effects that may interact with medications. For example, herbs that drain dampness could amplify the effect of diuretics, potentially causing dehydration or electrolyte imbalances. Always keep both practitioners informed of everything you are taking. Never stop or adjust your conventional medications on your own.

### How long does it take to see results?
Most people notice reduced swelling and increased energy within the first few weeks of treatment. Changes in urine protein and blood levels may take 1-3 months to show on lab tests. Acute cases tend to respond faster; chronic conditions with deep deficiency require patience and consistent treatment over several months.

### What dietary changes should I make?
In general, favor warm, cooked, easy-to-digest foods. Avoid raw, cold, greasy, and overly spicy meals. Limit salt to reduce fluid retention. Lightly diuretic foods like barley, adzuki beans, and cucumber are helpful. If you have edema, your practitioner may also advise restricting fluid intake. For personalized guidance, see the dietary recommendations in the treatment plan.

### Is TCM safe for children with glomerulonephritis?
Yes, when prescribed by a qualified pediatric TCM specialist. Children often recover more quickly from acute patterns. Herbal dosages are carefully adjusted for age and weight, and acupuncture may be replaced with acupressure or gentle pediatric tui na massage. Always ensure the practitioner has experience treating children and communicates with your child's pediatrician.

### Will the results last after I stop treatment?
In acute cases, once the pathogen is cleared and the body's balance restored, recurrence is unlikely unless another infection triggers it. For chronic deficiency patterns, the goal is to rebuild the constitution so that the kidneys remain strong. Some patients may need periodic maintenance treatments or seasonal herbs to prevent relapse, especially during times of stress or illness.

### What should I tell my nephrologist about TCM?
Be open about your decision to use TCM. Share the list of herbs and formulas you are taking. A good nephrologist will want to monitor your kidney function and adjust medications as needed. Most practitioners appreciate a collaborative approach. If your doctor expresses concerns, you can invite them to review the research on TCM for kidney health or suggest a joint consultation.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for primary glomerulonephritis: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, Wang H, Liu Y, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for primary glomerulonephritis: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2012;18(10):731-739.*
   This systematic review evaluated multiple RCTs on Chinese herbal medicine for primary glomerulonephritis. The analysis found that adding herbal formulas to standard care reduced 24‑hour proteinuria and improved creatinine clearance compared to conventional treatment alone. However, the authors noted that the methodological quality of included trials was generally low, and larger, rigorous studies are needed.

2. **Acupuncture for chronic glomerulonephritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang Y, Chen J, Wu H. Acupuncture for chronic glomerulonephritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine. 2019;2019:8613694.*
   This meta-analysis pooled data from several RCTs and concluded that acupuncture as an adjunctive therapy significantly reduced urinary protein excretion and alleviated fatigue in patients with chronic glomerulonephritis. The effect on renal function preservation was less clear, and the authors called for more high‑quality trials with longer follow‑up periods.
