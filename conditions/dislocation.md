## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
A joint dislocation occurs when the ends of two connected bones are forced out of their normal position, often by a sudden impact, fall, or extreme twisting motion. This can stretch or tear the surrounding ligaments, and in some cases damage nearby nerves and blood vessels.
The result is immediate, severe pain, visible deformity, swelling, and an inability to move the joint. The shoulder, fingers, knee, and hip are the most commonly affected sites.
Diagnosis is usually made by physical examination and confirmed with an X-ray to rule out an associated fracture. Prompt reduction-manually guiding the bones back into place-is essential to relieve pain and restore function, followed by a period of immobilization and then rehabilitation.

### Treatments
Standard treatment begins with closed reduction, where a doctor manipulates the joint back into alignment, often under sedation or anesthesia. The joint is then immobilized with a splint, sling, or brace for several weeks to allow the soft tissues to heal.
Pain is managed with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or, for severe pain, short-term opioids. Once the initial healing is underway, physical therapy is introduced to restore range of motion, rebuild muscle strength, and improve joint stability. In cases of recurrent dislocation or when there is significant ligament or bone damage, surgical repair may be recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, a joint dislocation is seen as a violent disruption of the flow of Qi and Blood in the local channels. The sudden force-whether from a fall, a wrench, or a blow-creates an acute blockage where Qi becomes trapped and Blood pools. This is the immediate cause of the intense swelling, stabbing pain, and complete loss of movement that define the first week or two after the injury. At this stage, the body is in a state of pure excess: the problem is not a lack of something, but something that is stuck where it shouldn't be. The Liver, which governs the smooth flow of Qi and stores Blood, is the organ system most acutely affected, as its function of ensuring free movement is directly assaulted.
As the acute crisis passes and the joint is reduced and stabilized, the body begins a repair process that consumes a great deal of Blood and Qi. This can leave a person in a mixed state of lingering stagnation and new deficiency. The swelling and pain are less intense, but the joint feels stiff and weak rather than locked. The Spleen, which produces Qi and Blood from food, must work hard to supply the raw materials for healing. If the Spleen is already weak, or if the injury was severe, the repair can stall, leaving behind a dull, persistent ache and a joint that never quite feels stable. This is the subacute phase, where the tongue and pulse begin to show signs of both stasis and emptiness.
Beyond the injury itself, TCM looks at the terrain that made the dislocation possible. The Liver governs the sinews and ligaments, while the Kidneys govern the bones. When Liver Blood and Kidney Essence are depleted-often from overwork, chronic stress, aging, or a constitutional tendency-the sinews lose their tensile strength and the bones become brittle. The joints then lack the firm anchoring they need, making them prone to slipping out even with minimal strain. Similarly, a generalized deficiency of Qi and Blood, often rooted in a weak Spleen, means the muscles and ligaments are too slack to hold the joint securely. In these cases, the dislocation is not just an accident but a sign of a deeper, long-term vulnerability that must be addressed to prevent recurrence.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking when the dislocation happened and what the pain feels like right now. The healing timeline is the most reliable guide, because the body moves through predictable stages after a joint injury. Early on, the signs are all about acute obstruction; later, they shift toward a picture of depletion and poor repair.
In the first one to two weeks, intense pain, obvious swelling, and near-total loss of movement point to Qi and Blood Stagnation (气滞血瘀, qì zhì xuè yū). The tongue is dark purple with stasis spots and a thin white coat, and the pulse feels wiry and choppy. This pattern reflects traumatic force that has jammed the flow of Qi and blood in the local channels, creating severe acute symptoms.
Around two to three weeks after the injury, swelling and pain are milder but still present, and the joint feels unsteady rather than locked. This is Blood Deficiency and Stagnation. The tongue appears pale-red with a thin white coat, and the pulse is wiry and thin. The body has begun to clear the stagnation, but its ability to nourish the sinews is already running low, leaving incomplete healing.
Three weeks or more after a dislocation, or when the same joint slips out repeatedly, the pain turns into a dull ache and the joint feels loose and weak. Two deficiency patterns often appear here.
Liver Blood and Kidney Essence Deficiency shows a pale, thin tongue with possible fine cracks and a deep, fine, weak pulse, pointing to a deep loss of the reserves that anchor the joints.
A broader Qi and Blood Deficiency shares similar signs but emphasizes whole-body fatigue, pale complexion, and poor tissue repair. Both patterns signal that the foundation is weak and needs rebuilding.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi And Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain that worsens with pressure, Marked swelling and bruising around the joint, Joint locked and completely immobile, Dark purplish tongue with stasis spots, Irritability or emotional tension
- **Worse with**: Moving or bearing weight on the joint, Anger and emotional stress, Greasy, spicy, or icy-cold food, Cold, damp weather
- **Better with**: Rest and immobilization, Cold compress in the first 48 hours, Elevating the injured part, Gentle movement after the acute phase
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a joint is suddenly wrenched or struck, the local flow of Qi and Blood is violently disrupted. This creates a bottleneck where Blood pools and Qi becomes trapped, leading to the intense swelling, fixed stabbing pain, and inability to move that define the first week or two after a dislocation.
Because Qi is the commander of Blood, the stagnation of Qi amplifies the Blood stasis, and the accumulated Blood further blocks the free movement of Qi - a vicious cycle that keeps the joint locked and painful until the stasis is moved.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with scattered stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, and a thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and choppy (涩 sè), like a taut guitar string scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: In Qi and Blood Stagnation, the injured channels are already choked with congealed Blood and trapped Qi. Rest and elevation take mechanical pressure off the vessels, while cold constricts them to prevent further leakage. Gentle movement after the acute phase coaxes Qi and Blood back into circulation. Stress and anger, on the other hand, fan the Liver Qi that is already knotted, tightening the stagnation. Cold and greasy foods congeal Blood, making the stasis harder to resolve, while heat in the early stage would dilate vessels and increase swelling.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Rest the injured joint completely and keep it elevated above heart level to drain swelling. In the first 24-48 hours, apply cold compresses - never heat - to constrict vessels and slow further bleeding into the tissues. Eat lightly: congee, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of blood-moving foods like hawthorn berry tea or a little black wood-ear mushroom. Avoid alcohol, greasy food, and icy drinks, which congeal Blood and worsen stasis.

### Blood Deficiency and Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-deficiency-and-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed dull or stabbing pain at the joint, Pain less than acute phase but still present after 2-3 weeks, Pale or sallow face with a dusky tinge, Fatigue and low energy, Dizziness or palpitations, Dry skin or brittle nails
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Cold weather or drafts, Prolonged standing or sitting, Emotional stress, Raw or cold foods
- **Better with**: Gentle movement, Warm compresses, Rest and sleep, Nourishing, warm meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a joint is dislocated, the trauma initially causes severe blood stasis and swelling. As the acute phase passes, the body uses a great deal of blood to repair the injury, which can leave you with a deficiency of nourishing blood. The remaining stagnation lingers in the channels, blocking the flow of Qi and blood to the joint. This combination of not enough blood to nourish the sinews and bones, plus the obstruction from old stasis, results in a dull, fixed pain that persists even after the worst swelling has gone down, along with stiffness and weakness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-purple body, thin, with possible stasis spots and distended sublingual veins; thin white coat. Pulse: fine (Xi) and choppy (Se), often wiry (Xian) on the left Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Gentle movement and warmth promote circulation of Qi and blood, helping to dissolve stasis and deliver nourishment to the injured joint. Rest and sleep allow the body to rebuild blood and repair sinews. Overexertion depletes Qi and blood, weakening the joint further, while cold congeals blood and makes stasis worse. Emotional stress stagnates Liver Qi, which in turn worsens blood stasis, so calmness is essential for healing.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, blood-nourishing foods like bone broth, dark leafy greens, beets, small amounts of red meat, and goji berries. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which congeal blood and worsen stasis. Gentle movement like walking or tai chi encourages circulation without straining the joint, but rest when you feel fatigued. Keep the area warm with a scarf or wrap.

### Liver Blood and Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-blood-and-kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Lingering dull joint pain, Joints feel loose or unstable, Lower back soreness and weak knees, Dizziness and blurred vision, Premature greying or hair loss, Fatigue and low energy
- **Worse with**: Overwork and physical strain, Cold, damp weather, Skipping meals or poor diet, Chronic stress and worry
- **Better with**: Warm compresses on the joint, Rest with gentle movement, Nutrient-dense, warm meals, Consistent sleep routine
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver governs the sinews and the Kidneys govern the bones. When Liver Blood and Kidney Essence are both depleted, sinews lose their nourishment and bones become brittle. The joints then lack the firm anchoring they need to stay in place, making them prone to dislocation even with mild strain, and slow to stabilize after reduction.
This pattern typically appears weeks after an injury or in people who suffer repeated dislocations. The underlying emptiness means the body cannot generate enough new tissue to repair the joint capsule, so a dull ache and a sense of looseness persist long after the swelling has gone down.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with possible fine cracks; thin white or scanty coating, slightly dry. Pulse: deep, fine, and weak; especially fine at the left Guan (Liver) position and deep-weak at both Chi (Kidney) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Liver Blood and Kidney Essence are deep reserves that rebuild slowly. Overwork, poor nutrition, and cold all deplete these reserves further, leaving sinews and bones undernourished and the joint insecure. Warmth, rest, and nutrient-rich foods gently replenish the Blood and Essence, while consistent sleep allows the body to repair. Gentle movement keeps Qi and Blood circulating without straining the weakened joint, helping to restore stability over time.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, nourishing foods that build Blood and Essence: black sesame, walnuts, goji berries, bone broths, and dark leafy greens. Avoid raw, cold foods that weaken digestion. Rest the joint, but do gentle, controlled strengthening exercises once the acute phase has passed - complete immobility can lead to further weakness. Keep the area warm, and avoid overwork and excessive sexual activity, both of which drain Kidney Essence.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, lingering joint ache, Recurrent dislocation after minor strain, Pale or sallow complexion, Generalized fatigue and weakness, Poor appetite and loose stools
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Cold, raw foods, Worry and pensiveness, Cold, damp weather
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle stretching, Warmth on the joint
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi and Blood are deficient, the sinews and bones lose their nourishment and strength. The Spleen fails to produce enough Qi and Blood, so muscles and ligaments become weak and unable to hold the joint securely in place. This makes the joint prone to slipping out again, even after minor strain or ordinary movement.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine, weak, and thready, especially weak at the right Guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are the foundation for strong sinews and stable joints. Rest and nourishing foods support the Spleen in producing Qi and Blood, while warmth helps circulation. Overexertion and cold foods deplete Qi and Blood, and emotional worry further weakens the Spleen, making joints more vulnerable to dislocation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and bone broths with gentle spices to build Qi and Blood. Avoid raw, cold, or greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Gentle exercise such as Qi Gong or walking supports circulation without straining the joints. Prioritize adequate rest and avoid overexertion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM help after I've already had my joint put back in place?
Yes, and this is actually the ideal moment to start. Once the joint is reduced and stabilized, TCM can accelerate the healing of damaged soft tissues, reduce residual swelling and pain, and prevent the stagnation from becoming chronic. Herbal formulas can be taken immediately, and acupuncture can be applied to distal points even while the joint is still immobilized.

### Will acupuncture and herbs prevent my shoulder from dislocating again?
If your dislocations are recurrent and happen with minimal force, TCM aims to correct the underlying weakness that allows the joint to slip. This usually involves nourishing Liver Blood and Kidney Essence to strengthen the sinews and bones. With consistent treatment over several months, many people experience a significant reduction in the frequency of dislocations and a greater sense of joint stability. It is not a quick fix, but it addresses the root cause that surgery and physical therapy alone may not reach.

### How soon after a dislocation can I start acupuncture?
Acupuncture can begin as soon as the joint has been reduced and any fracture has been ruled out. In the early stage, needles are generally placed away from the injured area-on the opposite limb or on points along the affected channel-to avoid aggravating the local swelling. This approach can relieve pain, calm muscle spasm, and promote the circulation of Qi and Blood to support healing.

### Do I need to stop taking my pain medication if I use Chinese herbs?
No, you should not stop prescribed medication without consulting your doctor. TCM can usually be used alongside NSAIDs or other pain relievers. However, some herbs that move Blood, such as Dang Gui or Tao Ren, may have a mild blood-thinning effect, so it is important to tell both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor about everything you are taking. They can then monitor for any potential interactions.

### What foods should I eat to help my joint heal?
Focus on warm, nourishing foods that build Qi and Blood. Bone broths, slow-cooked stews with dark leafy greens, moderate amounts of lean protein, and the gentle use of warming spices like ginger and turmeric are all excellent. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which can constrict the channels and slow healing. Greasy and overly spicy foods should also be limited, as they can create Dampness or Heat that interferes with recovery.

### Can children or older adults use TCM for a dislocation?
Yes, with appropriate adjustments. Children, whose bodies are still developing, often heal quickly and may need only a short course of herbs to resolve stagnation. Older adults, who are more likely to have underlying Kidney and Liver deficiency, may benefit from a longer, more nourishing approach. In both cases, a qualified TCM practitioner will tailor the formula and acupuncture points to the person's age and constitution. Always ensure the practitioner knows the full medical history.
