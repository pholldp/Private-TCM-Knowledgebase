## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Western medicine defines Functional Gastrointestinal Disorders as a group of conditions where chronic digestive symptoms - such as epigastric pain, bloating, early satiety, nausea, and altered bowel habits - occur without any identifiable structural, inflammatory, or biochemical abnormality. The most common are functional dyspepsia and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Diagnosis is made using the Rome criteria, which rely on symptom patterns and duration, after excluding organic disease through endoscopy, imaging, and lab tests. The underlying mechanisms are thought to involve altered gut-brain communication, visceral hypersensitivity, abnormal motility, and psychosocial factors.

### Treatments
Conventional management typically includes dietary modifications (low-FODMAP, avoiding triggers), medications such as proton pump inhibitors for dyspepsia, antispasmodics, prokinetics, and neuromodulators (low-dose antidepressants), as well as psychological therapies like cognitive behavioral therapy and gut-directed hypnotherapy. Treatment aims to reduce symptoms and improve quality of life, but responses vary widely.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands functional gastrointestinal disorders primarily through the dynamic relationship between the Liver and the Spleen-Stomach network. The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body, including the digestive tract. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger cause the Liver Qi to stagnate, it can 'invade' the Stomach, disrupting its natural downward movement and leading to bloating, belching, and pain that often shifts with mood. This is why so many people notice their symptoms flare during stressful periods - the Liver's emotional burden directly impacts digestion.
The Spleen, in TCM, is the central organ of digestion, transforming food into Qi and blood. When the Spleen's Qi is weak - often from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness - it cannot properly process food, leading to fatigue, loose stools, and bloating after even small meals. This deficiency can also generate internal dampness, a heavy, sticky pathogenic factor that further clogs the digestive system, causing nausea, a feeling of fullness, and a thick tongue coating.
Other patterns include damp-heat, where accumulated dampness combines with heat from rich foods or alcohol, creating a bitter taste and burning sensation; phlegm-dampness, which produces a heavy, foggy-headed sluggishness; and Spleen Yang deficiency, where the digestive fire is too weak to warm the gut, causing cold-type pain that improves with heat.
Because the same set of Western symptoms can arise from any of these imbalances, TCM doesn't treat 'functional GI disorder' as a single entity - it treats the specific pattern behind your symptoms. The tongue and pulse are the key diagnostic tools that reveal which pattern is active.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner differentiates these patterns by asking what the discomfort feels like, when it strikes, and what makes it better or worse. The quality of the pain-sharp or dull, moving or fixed-along with the tongue and pulse, points toward one pattern rather than another. Emotional triggers and digestive symptoms like belching or stool consistency are especially revealing.
If the symptoms flare with stress and include epigastric distension, frequent belching, and a sensation of pain that moves, the picture suggests Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. The tongue is often pale red with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels wiry. This pattern is very common in functional GI disorders where mood plays a strong role.
When the main complaint is chronic bloating after meals, fatigue, and loose stools with poor appetite, Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency is more likely. The tongue appears pale and slightly swollen with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak. Unlike the Liver pattern, discomfort here is dull and improves with rest and warmth rather than emotional release.
Damp-Heat and Phlegm-Dampness both cause epigastric fullness and nausea, but they differ in heat signs. Damp-Heat brings a bitter taste, a sticky yellow tongue coating, and a slippery rapid pulse. Phlegm-Dampness presents with chest stuffiness, a greasy white coating, and a slippery pulse without heat.
Spleen Yang Deficiency adds cold signs-dull pain that eases with warmth, cold limbs, and watery stools-with a pale tongue and a deep, weak pulse.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain that radiates to the rib area, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent belching and acid reflux, Irritability, moodiness, and frequent sighing, Sensation of a lump or blockage in the throat
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Anger or frustration, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Overeating or large meals, Irregular meal times
- **Better with**: Reducing stress, Gentle walking, Deep breathing exercises, Warm, cooked meals, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for keeping Qi flowing smoothly throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger build up, that flow stalls and the stuck Liver Qi can surge sideways to "invade" the Stomach, disrupting its natural downward movement. This leads to bloating, distending pain in the upper abdomen that often radiates to the ribs, frequent belching, and acid reflux. Because the trigger is emotional, symptoms typically flare during times of tension and ease when you feel calm.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, may have redder sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, most pronounced at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and anger cause Liver Qi to stagnate and surge into the Stomach, disrupting digestion and creating bloating, belching, and reflux. Calming the mind and moving the body gently helps smooth the flow of Liver Qi, while warm, simple meals reduce the burden on the Stomach, allowing it to function properly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, regular meals and avoid eating when emotionally upset. Gentle daily movement like walking, yoga, or tai chi helps keep Qi flowing. Practice stress management such as deep breathing or meditation to prevent Liver Qi from stagnating. Avoid spicy, greasy, and acidic foods that can aggravate reflux.

### Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-stomach-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and fullness after meals, Poor appetite or reduced desire to eat, Loose or poorly formed stools, Fatigue and physical weakness, Sallow or pale complexion
- **Worse with**: Overeating or large meals, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Fatigue and overwork, Emotional stress, Cold, damp environments
- **Better with**: Small, frequent meals, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle walking, Rest and adequate sleep, Warmth on the abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is the organ that transforms food into Qi and blood. When its Qi is deficient, it can't properly digest food or absorb nutrients. The undigested food sits in the stomach and intestines, causing bloating and loose stools, while the whole body feels tired because it isn't getting the energy it needs.
Without enough Spleen Qi to hold things up, the upper abdomen feels heavy and full after even small meals, and the complexion turns pale or sallow. This pattern is a core deficiency that makes functional GI symptoms chronic and slow to resolve.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, tooth-marked body with a thin white coating. Pulse: Weak, soft, and lacking force, especially at the right Guan (Spleen-Stomach) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen thrives on warmth and struggles with cold. Raw and cold foods further weaken its transforming ability, leading to more bloating and loose stools. Small, warm meals ease the digestive burden, while rest and gentle movement support Qi production and flow. Overwork and stress drain Spleen Qi, so avoiding them helps the digestive fire recover.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and avoid raw, cold, or iced items. Take small, frequent meals rather than large ones to avoid overwhelming a weak Spleen. Gentle walking after meals aids digestion. Prioritize adequate rest and avoid overwork, as fatigue depletes Qi further.

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and fullness in the upper abdomen, Bitter or sticky taste in the mouth, Sticky, incomplete bowel movements, Heaviness in the body and limbs, Thirst with little desire to drink, Nausea or queasiness
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overeating or large meals, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Coix seed (Job&#x27;s tears) tea, Bitter greens (dandelion, chicory), Gentle daily movement, Cool, dry environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's ability to transform food and fluids weakens, dampness accumulates. Over time, this dampness can combine with heat from a rich diet, alcohol, or external humidity, creating a sticky, turbid mixture that lodges in the stomach and spleen. This damp-heat blocks the normal descending and ascending flow of Qi, causing bloating, nausea, a bitter sticky taste, and sluggish, incomplete bowel movements.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, thick yellow greasy coating especially at centre and root. Pulse: Soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), often slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, sweet, and spicy foods, along with alcohol, directly fuel dampness and heat inside the body, while hot, humid environments add external dampness that the Spleen struggles to manage. Overeating overwhelms the Spleen's processing capacity, causing more stagnation. In contrast, light, bland foods and bitter herbs help drain dampness and clear heat. Gentle movement activates the Spleen's transforming function, while a cool, dry setting prevents external dampness from adding to the internal burden.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and heavily spiced foods, as well as alcohol, dairy, and excessive sweets, which all create more dampness and heat. Eat light, easily digestible meals centered on cooked vegetables, moderate whole grains, and small amounts of lean protein. Include naturally cooling and dampness-draining foods such as bitter greens (dandelion, chicory), celery, cucumber, and coix seed (Job's tears) tea. Gentle daily movement like walking or yoga helps the Spleen Qi transform dampness; avoid sitting for long periods, especially in hot, humid conditions.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Feeling of fullness and stuffiness in the upper abdomen, Bloating and distension after eating, Loose sticky stools, Heavy sensation in body and limbs, Drowsiness and excessive sleepiness, Bland taste in mouth, no desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Damp, humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle, Overeating or large meals
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Warmth on the abdomen, Gentle walking, Dry, warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids. When it becomes weak, it fails to process fluids properly, leading to the accumulation of dampness and phlegm in the middle burner. This sticky phlegm-dampness obstructs the flow of Qi, causing epigastric fullness, nausea, and a heavy sensation. The lack of proper fluid transformation also results in a greasy tongue coating and a lack of thirst.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and often soggy (濡 rú).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, cold, and raw foods directly increase dampness and phlegm, the very substances clogging the middle burner. Warm, cooked foods and abdominal warmth support the Spleen's transformation function, helping it process fluids correctly. Gentle movement activates Qi, which is needed to move and dry the accumulated dampness, while a dry environment prevents external dampness from invading and worsening the condition.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-heavy foods that create more dampness. Include barley, adzuki beans, and lightly cooked ginger to help drain dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking supports Qi flow and helps the body clear heaviness.

### Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull abdominal pain that improves with warmth and pressure, Cold hands and feet, Loose watery stools with undigested food, Abdominal bloating worse after eating
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overeating or large meals, Cold, damp environments, Fatigue and overwork, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Warmth on the abdomen, Warm drinks and soups, Rest and adequate sleep, Gentle pressure or massage, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen's Yang is responsible for warming and transforming food into Qi and blood. When it becomes deficient, the digestive fire weakens, leading to internal cold. This cold congeals Qi, causing dull, persistent pain that feels better with warmth and pressure. Without enough Yang, the Spleen cannot separate pure from impure, so water and food pass through poorly, resulting in watery stools with undigested food.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, tender body with scalloped edges (teeth marks) and a white slippery coating. Pulse: Deep, slow, and weak, especially at the right guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Yang is like a digestive fire that needs warmth to function. Warmth from food, drinks, and external heat helps restore that fire, while cold dampens it. Overeating overwhelms the already weak Spleen, leading to bloating and pain. Rest conserves Qi, and gentle pressure helps move stagnant Qi. Stress disrupts the flow of Liver Qi, which can further weaken the Spleen.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, well-cooked foods like soups and stews; avoid raw, cold, and iced items. Use warming spices such as ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper. Keep your abdomen warm with a scarf or hot water bottle. Gentle daily movement like walking supports Spleen Qi without overtaxing it. Chew food thoroughly and stop eating before you feel full.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can stress really cause my stomach problems?
Yes, in TCM, emotional stress is one of the most common triggers for functional GI disorders. The Liver is easily affected by frustration, anger, and prolonged tension. When Liver Qi stagnates, it can directly disrupt the Stomach's ability to move food downward, leading to bloating, belching, and pain. This is why symptoms often flare during stressful periods and improve when you relax. Treatment focuses on smoothing the Liver Qi alongside digestive support.

### How long does it take to see results with TCM?
Many people notice a reduction in bloating and discomfort within the first 2-4 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs. Excess patterns like Liver Qi Stagnation or Damp-Heat often respond more quickly. Deficiency patterns, such as Spleen Qi Deficiency, may take 3-6 months to fully rebuild digestive strength. Progress is usually gradual, with symptoms becoming less frequent and less intense over time.

### Will I need to change my diet?
Yes, dietary adjustments are a key part of treatment. The universal recommendation is to eat warm, cooked foods and avoid raw, cold, and icy items, which weaken the Spleen's digestive fire. You'll also receive pattern-specific guidance - for example, avoiding greasy, spicy foods if you have damp-heat, or incorporating warming spices like ginger if you have Spleen Yang deficiency. These changes support the herbal and acupuncture work, speeding your recovery.

### Can I take TCM herbs with my current medications?
In most cases, TCM herbs can be safely combined with conventional medications for functional GI disorders, such as proton pump inhibitors or antispasmodics. However, you should never stop prescribed medications abruptly. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about all treatments you are using. If you take blood thinners or have other complex health conditions, a careful review of your herbal formula is essential to avoid any rare interactions.

### Is acupuncture painful for digestive issues?
Acupuncture uses very fine, single-use needles and is generally not painful. You may feel a brief pinch upon insertion, followed by a dull ache or tingling sensation, which is a sign of Qi activation. Points on the abdomen, legs, and arms are commonly used for digestive disorders, and most patients find the experience deeply relaxing. Many even fall asleep during the 20-30 minute session.

### What if I've had these symptoms for years?
Long-standing functional GI disorders can still respond well to TCM, though they often require a longer treatment course. Chronic symptoms typically involve deeper deficiency or a mixture of patterns, so rebuilding the Spleen and clearing accumulated dampness or stagnation takes time. With consistent herbs, acupuncture, and dietary changes, many patients experience significant and lasting improvement, even after years of discomfort.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a multicentre randomised controlled trial** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m1319))
   *Journal/Publication: Ma T, et al. BMJ. 2020;368:m1319.*
   This large RCT involving 564 participants found that acupuncture significantly reduced postprandial distress syndrome scores and improved quality of life compared to sham acupuncture. The benefits persisted for 24 weeks after treatment, demonstrating acupuncture's potential as an effective therapy for functional dyspepsia.

2. **Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008487.pub2))
   *Journal/Publication: Lan L, et al. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2014;(10):CD008487.*
   This Cochrane systematic review assessed seven trials and found that acupuncture may improve symptoms of functional dyspepsia compared to sham acupuncture, though the quality of evidence was moderate due to risk of bias. It highlighted the need for larger, well-designed studies.

3. **Chinese herbal medicine for functional dyspepsia: systematic review of randomized controlled trials** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgh.12978))
   *Journal/Publication: Xiao Y, et al. J Gastroenterol Hepatol. 2015;30(9):1326-36.*
   This review analyzed 49 RCTs and found that Chinese herbal medicine, particularly formulas based on pattern differentiation, significantly improved global symptom scores compared to placebo or prokinetics. The most commonly studied formulas included Chaihu Shugan San and Xiang Sha Liu Jun Zi Tang.
