## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Cough-Variant Asthma is a subtype of asthma where a chronic dry cough is the only or predominant symptom, without the classic wheezing or shortness of breath. The cough is often triggered by allergens, cold air, exercise, or respiratory infections, and tends to be worse at night. Diagnosis usually involves spirometry with a bronchial provocation test to confirm airway hyper-responsiveness.

### Treatments
Standard treatment focuses on reducing airway inflammation and hyper-reactivity. Inhaled corticosteroids are the mainstay, often combined with long-acting bronchodilators. Leukotriene receptor antagonists may be added, and short-acting bronchodilators are used for acute episodes. Trigger avoidance and environmental control are also recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Lungs are the organ most directly connected to the outside world through the nose and throat, and their primary job is to send Qi downward. A cough - any cough - is a sign that Lung Qi is rebelling upward instead of descending. In Cough-Variant Asthma, what forces the Qi upward varies greatly from person to person.
External Wind is a common trigger. Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat can invade the body’s surface and block the Lung’s ability to diffuse and descend, causing a sudden, paroxysmal dry cough. This is why CVA often begins after a cold or flu and worsens with exposure to cold air or wind. The pattern is acute and relatively straightforward to resolve if treated early.
When the cough becomes chronic, the root often shifts inward. A weak Spleen fails to transform fluids properly, leading to Dampness that congeals into Phlegm. This Phlegm settles in the Lungs, creating a persistent, productive cough - the body’s attempt to expel the obstruction. If the Phlegm sits long enough, it can generate Heat, turning the sputum yellow and sticky. In other cases, emotional stress causes Liver Qi to stagnate and transform into Fire, which then flames upward to assault the Lungs, producing a forceful, spasmodic cough with rib pain and a bitter taste.
After months or years of coughing, the Lung’s own resources become depleted. Qi and Yin - the energy and cooling moisture that keep the airways calm and lubricated - run low. The result is a stubborn dry cough that worsens with exertion, accompanied by fatigue, night sweats, and a dry throat. This is why one Western diagnosis can have so many different faces in TCM: the cough is the same symptom, but the underlying pattern is completely different.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by listening carefully to the cough itself - is it dry and hacking, or wet and rattling? They ask about sputum color and consistency, what triggers the episodes, and whether there is throat itch, chest tightness, or rib pain. The tongue and pulse then act as a map, confirming whether the root is an external invasion, internal phlegm, or a deeper deficiency.
When the cough is paroxysmal, dry, and worse with cold air, with an itchy throat and a thin white tongue coating, the picture points to Wind-Cold invading the Lungs. In contrast, a harsh cough with yellow sputum, a sore throat, thirst, a red tongue with a thin yellow coating, and a floating rapid pulse signals Wind-Heat. The presence of fever or chills and the sputum color are the key differentiators.
If the cough is productive and the sputum is sticky and white, with a sensation of chest fullness, a white greasy tongue coating, and a slippery pulse, Damp-Phlegm has lodged in the Lungs. When that phlegm turns yellow and thick, the tongue becomes red with a yellow greasy coating, and the pulse feels rapid and slippery, it indicates Phlegm-Heat. The shift from white to yellow sputum and tongue coating marks the transformation.
A persistent dry cough that worsens with exertion, accompanied by shortness of breath, a red tongue with little coating, and a thready rapid pulse suggests Qi and Yin Deficiency from prolonged illness. If the cough comes in violent bouts triggered by stress or anger, with rib-side pain and a wiry rapid pulse, the diagnosis leans toward Liver Fire insulting the Lungs. The emotional trigger and the location of discomfort help separate these two.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chills more than fever, Clear watery nasal discharge, Cough worse with cold air or at night, Itchy throat, No sweating, Thin white watery phlegm or dry cough
- **Worse with**: Cold air, Drafts, Cold drinks, Raw foods, Night time
- **Better with**: Warm drinks, Ginger tea, Keeping warm, Rest, Steam inhalation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Cold invades the body's surface and blocks the Lung's ability to diffuse and descend Qi. This forces Qi to rebel upward as a paroxysmal cough, often dry or with scanty thin white phlegm. The cold constricts the airways, making the cough worse at night or when exposed to cold air.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (fú) and tight (jǐn).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Cold tightens and obstructs the Lung's dispersing function, causing Qi to rebel upward as a cough. Warmth and rest help release the exterior and restore the normal flow of Lung Qi, while cold air, cold drinks, and raw foods introduce more Cold, perpetuating the pattern. Night time is Yin and cold, which exacerbates the constriction, making the cough more frequent.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Stay warm, especially cover the neck and chest to shield from drafts. Drink warm fluids like ginger tea with a little brown sugar to gently disperse Cold. Avoid cold drinks, raw salads, and dairy products, which can create phlegm and worsen the cough. Rest and allow the body to recover; gentle steam inhalation can help soothe the throat and loosen any mucus.

### Wind-Heat invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thick yellow phlegm, Sore or painful throat, Fever stronger than chills, Nasal congestion with yellow mucus, Red tongue tip with thin yellow coating, Floating and rapid pulse
- **Worse with**: Spicy, fried, or greasy foods, Smoky or polluted air, Overwork or lack of rest, Exposure to wind and drafts, Alcohol
- **Better with**: Cool, well-ventilated room, Warm herbal tea (chrysanthemum, peppermint), Rest and sleep, Light, non-greasy meals, Steam inhalation with mint
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Wind-Heat invades the body, it first attacks the Lungs because the Lungs are the most delicate organ and open directly to the outside through the nose and throat. The Heat disrupts the Lung's ability to spread and descend Qi, causing it to rebel upward as a harsh cough. The Heat also dries up body fluids, turning normal mucus into thick, yellow phlegm, and scorches the throat, making it sore and dry.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red tip, thin yellow coating, body slightly red. Pulse: floating and rapid (浮数 fú shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Heat is an exterior pathogen that brings Heat into the Lungs. Spicy foods, alcohol, and smoky air add more Heat and irritate the Lungs, while overwork weakens the body's defensive Qi, making it harder to push the pathogen out. Cooling herbal teas and light foods help clear Heat without burdening digestion, and rest allows the body to focus its Qi on expelling the invader. A cool, fresh environment and gentle steam inhalation soothe the throat and help the Lungs recover their natural downward rhythm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Drink plenty of warm fluids like chrysanthemum tea, peppermint tea, or fresh pear juice to cool and moisten the Lungs. Avoid spicy, fried, and greasy foods, as well as alcohol, which add internal Heat. Rest in a cool, well-ventilated room and protect yourself from drafts without overheating.

### Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with copious white sticky phlegm that is easy to expectorate, Feeling of fullness or heaviness in the chest, Poor appetite and abdominal bloating, Fatigue and heaviness of the body, Loose stools
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid weather, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Overeating sweets, Sedentary lifestyle, Milk and cheese
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Lightly spiced, cooked meals, Gentle walking, Avoiding dairy and sweets
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly, and those fluids turn into Dampness that congeals into Phlegm. This Phlegm then rises and settles in the Lungs, blocking the normal downward flow of Lung Qi. The result is a chronic, productive cough - the body's effort to expel the sticky obstruction - with white, easy-to-expectorate phlegm that is typical of cough-variant asthma in its remission or chronic phase.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale swollen body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), like beads rolling, possibly soggy (濡 rú).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Phlegm is a heavy, sticky pathogen that thrives on moisture and stagnation. Cold and raw foods, dairy, and sweets all burden the Spleen, generating more Dampness that turns into Phlegm. A humid environment and lack of movement allow Dampness to settle. In contrast, warmth, light spices, and gentle exercise help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, while dryness and movement disperse the Phlegm that is clogging the Lungs.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and sweet foods, especially dairy, which creates Dampness and Phlegm. Favor warm, cooked meals with gentle spices like ginger, cardamom, and a little black pepper. Light daily movement, such as walking, helps move Qi and transform Dampness; avoid lying down immediately after eating.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Thick, sticky yellow or green sputum, Chest oppression and fullness, Feeling of body heat or fever, Wheezing or gurgling sounds in the throat, Thirst with desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy food, Hot, humid weather, Emotional stress, Smoke or pollution, Overexertion
- **Better with**: Cool fresh air, Light, cooling diet, Rest and relaxation, Deep breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In cough-variant asthma, Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs means that sticky, inflammatory mucus has accumulated in the airways and become heated. This creates a persistent obstruction that triggers the cough reflex, making the airway hypersensitive. The body tries to expel the thick, yellow phlegm, but it is often difficult to bring up, leading to bouts of coughing, chest tightness, and a sensation of heat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating, possibly swollen at the front; may have red prickles on the front third. Pulse: slippery and rapid (滑数, huá shuò), strongest at the right cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, greasy, and heating foods create more Phlegm-Heat in the body, directly worsening the Lung obstruction. Hot, humid weather similarly adds external Heat and Dampness. Emotional stress can stir up internal Heat. Cooling, light foods and fresh air help clear Heat and resolve Phlegm, while rest allows the body to recover without further taxing the Lungs.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods that generate Heat and Phlegm. Dairy and cold drinks can worsen Phlegm. Eat cooling, moistening foods like Asian pears, radish, and bamboo shoots. Keep the air moist and avoid smoke and strong odors.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry, tickling cough with little or no sputum, Worse with exertion or talking, Night sweats, Fatigue and weakness, Dry mouth and throat, Warm palms and soles
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or long talking, Dry, spicy foods, Late nights, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Rest and sleep, Warm, moist foods, Humidifier, Gentle breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Prolonged coughing exhausts the Lung's Qi and Yin - the energy and cooling moisture that keep the airways calm and lubricated. Without enough Qi to descend and enough Yin to moisten, the throat becomes dry and irritable, triggering a stubborn dry cough that worsens with activity. This pattern often appears in the stable phase when the acute flare has passed but the body remains depleted.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with little or no coating, possibly cracked and dry. Pulse: thready (fine) and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin are deeply depleted after a long illness. Overexertion, talking, and late nights consume Qi, while spicy and dry foods consume Yin fluids, making the cough worse. Rest, nourishing foods, and moist air replenish both Qi and Yin, calming the dry, irritable cough.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion. Eat small, warm meals with moistening foods like pear, honey, almond, and congee. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly drying foods. Use a humidifier if the air is dry.

### Liver Fire insulting the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-insulting-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden, forceful coughing fits, Rib pain or distension that worsens with coughing, Irritability and quick temper, Bitter taste in the mouth, Feeling of Qi rushing upward during cough, Scant sticky phlegm or stringy clumps, Red eyes
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or stress, Spicy, hot, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, stuffy environments
- **Better with**: Calming down after an emotional upset, Cool drinks like chrysanthemum tea, Deep, slow breathing, Avoiding spicy and greasy food
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional frustration, repressed anger, or prolonged stress cause the Liver Qi to stagnate. Over time that stagnation generates Fire, which then flames upward along the Liver channel to attack the Lungs. This upward assault disrupts the Lung's ability to send Qi downward, triggering sudden, forceful coughing fits that often come with rib pain and a bitter taste in the mouth.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with distinctly redder sides, thin dry yellow coating. Pulse: wiry and rapid, especially wiry at the left middle position (Liver); the right front position (Lung) may feel full or slightly overflowing.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Anger and frustration cause Liver Qi to stagnate and generate Fire; that Fire travels upward to assault the Lungs, triggering the cough. Calming the mind and soothing the emotions allow Liver Qi to flow freely again, removing the root of the attack. Cooling foods and drinks help drain the excess Heat, while deep breathing directly assists the Lungs in restoring their normal downward movement of Qi.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize cooling, calming foods like chrysanthemum tea, pear, water chestnut, and celery. Avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy dishes, and greasy fried foods, which add Heat. Gentle stress management - such as walking, journaling, or breath work - helps keep Liver Qi moving smoothly and prevents Fire from building up.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does a TCM practitioner diagnose cough-variant asthma without a spirometry test?
The practitioner listens carefully to the quality of your cough, asks about sputum color and consistency, and identifies what triggers or relieves it. They then examine your tongue and feel your pulse - these reveal whether the root is an external invasion, internal phlegm, or a deeper deficiency. This pattern diagnosis guides the entire treatment.

### Can I use TCM if I’m already taking an inhaler?
Yes, TCM can safely complement your current medication. Herbal formulas and acupuncture work on a different level - they aim to correct the internal imbalances that make the airways reactive. Do not stop or reduce your prescribed inhaler without consulting your doctor. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing physician about all treatments you are using.

### What foods should I avoid to help my cough?
In general, avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, as well as dairy products, which TCM considers phlegm-producing. Spicy and fried foods can worsen Heat patterns. Instead, favor warm, cooked meals like soups and congees. Ginger tea can help with Wind-Cold, while chrysanthemum tea is better for Wind-Heat. Your practitioner will give you specific advice based on your pattern.

### How long until I see results?
Many patients notice a reduction in cough frequency within 2-4 weeks of starting herbs and acupuncture. Acute patterns clear faster, while chronic phlegm or deficiency patterns take longer. Consistency is key - daily herbs and weekly acupuncture sessions produce the best outcomes.

### Is acupuncture effective for cough-variant asthma?
Yes. Acupuncture helps regulate Lung Qi, reduce inflammation, and calm airway hyper-reactivity. Points like Lieque (LU-7) and Feishu (BL-13) are commonly used to descend rebellious Qi and stop coughing. Many patients find that regular acupuncture reduces their reliance on rescue inhalers.

### Can children be treated with TCM for this condition?
Absolutely. TCM offers gentle, non-invasive options for children, including pediatric herbal formulas, acupressure, and dietary adjustments. Always work with a practitioner experienced in pediatric care to ensure safe dosing.

### Will the cough come back after TCM treatment?
TCM aims to resolve the underlying pattern, not just suppress the cough. When the root imbalance - whether it’s Phlegm, Liver Fire, or Qi Deficiency - is corrected, the airways become less reactive and the cough is less likely to recur. Some patients may need occasional maintenance sessions during allergy season or after a cold.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for cough variant asthma: A systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang J, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for cough variant asthma: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2019;2019:6412467.*
   A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating Chinese herbal medicine for cough-variant asthma. Herbal treatment significantly improved total effective rate and reduced cough symptom scores compared to conventional therapy, with a lower relapse rate during follow-up.

2. **Clinical observation on acupuncture for cough-variant asthma**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang L, et al. Clinical observation on acupuncture for cough-variant asthma. Shanghai J Acupunct Moxibustion. 2023;42(2):112-117.*
   An RCT of 90 patients comparing acupuncture plus conventional medication versus medication alone. The acupuncture group showed significantly greater reduction in cough frequency and improved quality of life scores after 4 weeks of treatment.

3. **Guideline for TCM diagnosis and treatment of cough-variant asthma**
   *Journal/Publication: China Association of Chinese Medicine. Guideline for TCM diagnosis and treatment of cough-variant asthma. Chin J Exp Tradit Med Formulae. 2021;27(15):1-10.*
   A national expert consensus guideline providing standardized TCM pattern differentiation, herbal formulas, and acupuncture protocols for cough-variant asthma. It outlines six core patterns and recommends integrated treatment strategies for acute and remission stages.

4. **Research progress on integrated Chinese and Western medicine treatment of cough-variant asthma**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, et al. Research progress on integrated Chinese and Western medicine treatment of cough-variant asthma. Adv Clin Med. 2024;14(1):154-160.*
   A narrative review summarizing recent clinical and experimental studies on TCM therapies for cough-variant asthma, including herbal medicine, acupuncture, and acupoint application. It highlights the mechanisms of action and the advantages of integrated approaches in reducing recurrence.
