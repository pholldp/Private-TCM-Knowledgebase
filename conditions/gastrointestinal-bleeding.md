## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding refers to any bleeding that occurs in the digestive tract, from the esophagus to the rectum. It is typically categorized as upper GI bleeding (from the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum) or lower GI bleeding (from the small intestine, colon, or rectum).
Common symptoms include vomiting blood (hematemesis), passing black, tarry stools (melena), or bright red blood from the rectum (hematochezia). Diagnosis is usually made through endoscopy, colonoscopy, or imaging studies to locate the source and determine severity.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment depends on the severity and source of the bleed. Acute, severe bleeding may require endoscopic procedures to cauterize or clip the bleeding vessel, along with intravenous fluids and blood transfusions. Medications such as proton pump inhibitors are used to reduce stomach acid and promote healing of ulcers. In some cases, surgery is necessary. For chronic, slow bleeding, iron supplements may be prescribed to address anemia.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Stomach and Spleen are the primary organs responsible for receiving, digesting, and transforming food, but they also play a crucial role in managing blood. The Spleen, in particular, has the job of holding blood within the vessels. When the Spleen's Qi is strong, blood stays where it belongs. When it is weakened by chronic illness, overwork, or poor diet, it loses its grip, and blood can seep out slowly, leading to dark, tarry stools.
The Stomach, on the other hand, is susceptible to heat. Excessive heat - from fiery foods, alcohol, or emotional stress that transforms into fire - can agitate the blood, forcing it to move recklessly and burst out of the vessels. This results in brighter, more active bleeding, often with a burning sensation.
The Liver also plays a key role. Emotional stress, frustration, or anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate and eventually turn into fire, which then attacks the Stomach. This rebellious Qi surges upward, causing vomiting of blood that is often dark red and accompanied by rib-side pain and irritability.
Finally, long-standing illness can lead to blood stasis - a fixed, stabbing pain and bleeding of dark, clotted blood. And when bleeding has been prolonged, both Qi and Blood become deficient, creating a cycle of weakness that makes even minor bleeds hard to stop. TCM identifies the pattern by looking at the color and volume of blood, the nature of pain, and the overall energy of the person, then treats the root imbalance to stop the bleeding and prevent recurrence.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the color and volume of the blood, the location and nature of any pain, and how you feel overall. The answers quickly narrow down whether the bleeding is driven by heat, deficiency, or stagnation.
When the blood is bright red and there is a burning sensation in the stomach with thirst, Stomach Fire is likely. The tongue is red with a yellow coating and the pulse is rapid.
If instead the blood is dark red and accompanied by rib-side pain, irritability, and a bitter taste, the pattern shifts to Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach, with a red tongue and a greasy yellow coating.
Chronic, slow oozing that produces dark, tarry stools points toward a deficiency pattern. If fatigue and poor appetite are prominent and the tongue is pale with a weak pulse, Spleen Qi is too weak to hold blood (Qi not controlling Blood).
When blood loss has been prolonged, extreme exhaustion, dizziness, and pale, thin blood signal Qi and Blood Deficiency, with a pale tongue and a thready weak pulse.
A long history of fixed, stabbing epigastric pain and vomited blood that is dark purple with clots suggests Blood Stagnation. The tongue shows purplish spots and the pulse feels choppy. This pattern often develops from earlier conditions that were not resolved.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Stomach Fire (Stomach Heat)
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-fire-stomach-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting bright red (not dark) blood, Intense burning pain in the stomach area, Constant thirst for very cold drinks, Foul breath and swollen, bleeding gums, Constipation with dry, hard stools
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Overeating or large meals
- **Better with**: Cold drinks and cooling foods, Rest and lying down, Cool environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When excessive heat builds up in the Stomach, it acts like a fire that scorches the delicate blood vessels in the stomach lining. This heat forces the blood to move recklessly and burst out of the vessels, leading to the vomiting of bright red blood or the passage of dark, tarry stools. The burning sensation, intense thirst, and foul breath are all direct signs of this internal fire raging in the digestive tract.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly with red prickles in the centre, and a thick, dry, yellow coating. Pulse: rapid, slippery, and forceful, especially strong at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, greasy foods and alcohol are heating in nature and directly add to Stomach Fire, making the heat more intense and further damaging the blood vessels. Emotional stress, especially anger, can generate Liver Fire that invades the Stomach, fanning the flames. Conversely, cold drinks and cooling foods help counteract the excessive heat, while physical rest prevents the agitation of Qi and blood, allowing the body to redirect its energy toward healing and containing the blood.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid all spicy, greasy, fried, and roasted foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add fuel to the fire. Focus on cooling, moistening foods like watermelon, cucumber, pears, and mung bean soup. Eat small, light meals and never overeat to avoid overworking the already overheated Stomach.

### Qi not controlling Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-not-controlling-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark, tarry stools (melena) rather than bright red blood, Chronic, slow oozing bleeding, not sudden or profuse, Deep fatigue and shortness of breath on mild exertion, Pale, sallow face lacking lustre, Poor appetite and loose stools
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, Raw, cold, or greasy foods, Stress and worry, Prolonged standing or physical strain
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warm, easy-to-digest foods (like congee), Gentle abdominal massage, Moxibustion on the lower abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for holding blood inside the vessels. When Spleen Qi is weakened by chronic illness, overwork, or a poor diet, it loses this gripping power. Blood then seeps out slowly, often appearing as dark tarry stools rather than sudden bright red bleeding.
Because Qi is also the body's main source of energy, the same Spleen deficiency leads to deep fatigue, pale complexion, and poor appetite, creating a cycle where blood loss further drains Qi.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine and weak, especially at the right guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Qi is easily drained by overwork, poor diet, and mental strain. Rest and warm, simple foods allow the Spleen to rebuild its strength so it can once again hold blood inside the vessels. Moxibustion and gentle massage directly warm and tonify the Spleen and Stomach, reinforcing the body's grip on blood. In contrast, raw and cold foods, exhaustion, and worry further weaken the Spleen's holding function, making bleeding more likely.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods that are easy to digest, such as congee, soups, and well-cooked grains. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Small, frequent meals are better than large ones. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork; gentle movement like walking or tai chi can help circulate Qi without exhausting it.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of dark red blood, Distending pain in upper abdomen that radiates to ribs, Frequent belching and acid reflux, Symptoms worsen with stress or anger, Irritability and moodiness, Bitter taste in the mouth
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Skipping meals or irregular eating
- **Better with**: Stress reduction and relaxation, Bland, easily digestible foods, Gentle walking or stretching, Regular meal times
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress and frustration cause the Liver Qi to stagnate. Over time, this constrained Qi can transform into fire, which then attacks the Stomach. The resulting rebellious Qi surges upward, forcing blood out of the vessels and leading to vomiting of dark red blood. This is why the bleeding often comes with belching, acid reflux, and pain that radiates to the ribs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially at the sides, with a thin white or yellow greasy coating if heat is present. Pulse: wiry (xian), especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress is the primary driver of Liver Qi stagnation, so calming the mind and reducing frustration directly address the root cause. Spicy and greasy foods generate heat and further irritate the Stomach, while a bland diet helps cool and soothe it. Regular, gentle eating habits support the Stomach's normal downward flow, preventing rebellious Qi that forces blood upward.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which can further irritate the stomach. Eat smaller, bland meals at regular times. Incorporate stress-reducing practices like meditation, gentle yoga, or walking. Getting enough sleep and avoiding emotional upset are crucial for recovery.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting dark purplish blood with clots, Fixed, stabbing epigastric pain, Pain worsens at night and with pressure, Dark or dusky facial complexion
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Overexertion or overwork, Cold environment, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods
- **Better with**: Warm, easy-to-digest foods (like congee), Gentle walking or stretching, Applying a warm compress to the stomach, Rest and lying down, Stress reduction and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Long-standing illness or injury obstructs the flow of Qi and blood in the stomach, causing blood to congeal and stagnate in the vessels. This stagnant blood creates fixed, stabbing pain and eventually ruptures the vessels, leading to bleeding. Because the blood is old and trapped, it appears dark purple or clotted.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purplish body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè), rough like a knife scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold congeals blood, making stasis worse, while warmth and gentle movement encourage Qi and blood to flow smoothly again. Emotional stress tightens the Liver Qi, which further obstructs blood flow, so calming the mind helps release that stagnation. Rest reduces the body's demand on Qi, allowing it to focus on healing and containing blood.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easy-to-digest foods like congee and soups. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that congeal blood. Gentle daily walking or stretching promotes circulation, but avoid heavy exertion. Manage stress with calm activities, as emotional tension can worsen Qi stagnation and blood stasis.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, thin blood (vomited or in stool), Extreme fatigue and weakness, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Heart palpitations, Pale lips and nail beds, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, Raw, cold, or greasy foods, Stress and worry, Cold environment, Prolonged bleeding or illness
- **Better with**: Rest and sleep, Warm, easy-to-digest foods (like congee), Gentle walking or stretching, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Prolonged or heavy gastrointestinal bleeding drains both Qi and Blood. Without enough Qi to hold blood in the vessels and enough Blood to nourish the body, bleeding becomes pale and thin, and profound weakness sets in. The cycle of loss and depletion leaves the body too weak to heal, making even minor bleeding difficult to stop.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are already severely depleted, so rest and easily assimilated nourishment are essential to rebuild them. Overexertion, cold, and stress further drain Qi and Blood, making it even harder for the body to hold blood within the vessels. Warm, gentle foods and adequate sleep support the Spleen's ability to produce and manage Blood, which is the foundation for stopping this type of bleeding.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals of warm, easily digestible foods like rice congee, bone broths, and well-cooked vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and sleep to allow Qi and Blood to rebuild, and avoid heavy exertion until strength returns.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM stop active gastrointestinal bleeding?
Yes, TCM can help stop bleeding, especially when it is mild to moderate and chronic. Herbs like San Qi (notoginseng) and Bai Ji (hyacinth bletilla) are well-known for their hemostatic properties and are often included in formulas to arrest bleeding. However, severe, active bleeding with large volumes of bright red blood or signs of shock is a medical emergency and requires immediate hospital care. TCM can be used alongside conventional treatment once the patient is stable to promote healing and prevent recurrence.

### How long does it take to see results with TCM?
Improvement in bleeding symptoms can often be seen within a few days to a week for acute, heat-related patterns. For chronic bleeding due to deficiency, it may take 2-4 weeks to notice a change in stool color and energy levels. Full resolution of the underlying pattern and rebuilding of Qi and Blood typically takes 3-6 months of consistent herbal and acupuncture treatment.

### Is it safe to combine TCM herbs with my proton pump inhibitors or other medications?
In many cases, yes, but it is essential to coordinate with both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor. Some herbs, such as Da Huang (rhubarb), may interact with anticoagulant medications. Always provide a full list of your medications to your TCM practitioner, and do not stop any prescribed medication without consulting your doctor. TCM can be a valuable complement to conventional care for chronic GI issues.

### What should I eat to help with gastrointestinal bleeding?
In general, avoid spicy, greasy, and very hot or cold foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which can irritate the digestive tract and worsen heat. Favour warm, easy-to-digest foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and soups. For deficiency patterns, nourishing foods such as bone broth, cooked root vegetables, and small amounts of lean protein can help rebuild Qi and Blood. Your TCM practitioner will give you specific dietary advice based on your pattern.

### Can stress really cause gastrointestinal bleeding?
In TCM, yes. Emotional stress, particularly frustration and anger, can cause Liver Qi to stagnate and transform into fire, which then attacks the Stomach. This can lead to the vomiting of blood or the development of ulcers that bleed. This is why many people notice their stomach symptoms worsen during stressful periods. TCM treatment for this pattern includes calming the Liver and harmonizing the Stomach, often with herbs and acupuncture, and stress management is a key part of recovery.

### Will the bleeding come back after TCM treatment?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance that caused the bleeding, not just stop the current episode. When treatment is completed and the pattern is resolved, the risk of recurrence is greatly reduced. However, maintaining a healthy diet, managing stress, and avoiding known triggers are important for long-term prevention. Some patients may benefit from periodic 'maintenance' treatments.
