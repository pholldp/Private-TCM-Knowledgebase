# Fast Heartbeat (心悸)
**Pinyin**: xīn jì | **Kind**: Symptom | **Category**: Heart Rhythm & Palpitations, Chest Discomfort  
**Synonyms**: Increased Heart Rate, Rapid Pulse, Tachycardia, Fast Heart Rate, Rapid Heart Rate, Rapid Heartbeat, Racing Heart | **Body Regions**: Chest

---

## Preview Summary
A racing heart that worsens with stress and leaves you exhausted is a completely different condition in TCM than one that flares at night with a red tongue tip-and each has its own...

---

## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Heart palpitations are the sensation of a rapid, fluttering, or pounding heart. They can be triggered by stress, caffeine, certain medications, or underlying conditions like arrhythmias, thyroid disorders, or anemia. Doctors typically diagnose palpitations through an electrocardiogram (ECG), a Holter monitor to track heart rhythm over 24-48 hours, and blood tests to rule out metabolic causes. In many cases, palpitations are benign, but they can still be deeply unsettling.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment depends on the cause. When no serious arrhythmia is found, reassurance and lifestyle changes-reducing caffeine, managing stress-are often recommended. For more persistent or symptomatic palpitations, doctors may prescribe beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, or antiarrhythmic drugs to slow the heart rate. If a specific arrhythmia is identified, procedures like cardioversion or ablation may be considered.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees palpitations as a disturbance of the Shen-the mind-spirit that resides in the Heart. The Heart needs a steady supply of Qi and Blood to anchor the Shen calmly. When that nourishment runs low, the Shen becomes unsettled, and the heart races. This is why deficiency is the most common root: if the Heart lacks Qi, Blood, Yin, or Yang, it cannot keep a steady beat.
But not all palpitations come from emptiness. Sometimes a disruptive force agitates the Heart directly. Phlegm-Fire, born from poor digestion and emotional stress, can surge upward and stir the Shen into a frantic rhythm. In these cases the heartbeat feels forceful and oppressive, not weak and hollow. The triggers also differ-deficiency palpitations tend to worsen with fatigue or fright, while excess palpitations are more likely to flare after heavy meals or during periods of intense frustration.
The Spleen plays a hidden but vital role. It transforms food into Qi and Blood, so weak digestion often leads to Heart Blood or Qi deficiency. The Liver, which governs the smooth flow of emotions, can also be involved: when stress knots Liver Qi, it can generate Heat that disturbs the Heart. And the Kidneys, the root of Yin and Yang for the whole body, can fail to support the Heart if they are depleted. This web of connections is why one Western diagnosis of “palpitations” can unfold into six different TCM patterns, each with its own treatment strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what the heartbeat actually feels like and what brings it on. Is it a forceful thumping, a fluttering, or a hollow racing sensation? The quality, timing, and triggers of the palpitations are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the heartbeat is weak and easily triggered by a sudden noise or a busy day, and you feel timid or anxious, this suggests Heart Qi Deficiency. The tongue is pale and the pulse is thin and weak, reflecting the lack of Qi to steady the rhythm.
When palpitations come with dizziness, a pale face, and trouble falling asleep, the picture shifts to Heart Blood Deficiency. Here the tongue is also pale but the pulse is thready, as the blood is too scanty to anchor the mind.
If the heartbeat is rapid and accompanied by irritability, night sweats, and a warm sensation in the chest or palms, that points to Heart Yin Deficiency. The tongue looks red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid-signs of empty-heat agitating the Heart.
Palpitations that are accompanied by fatigue, poor appetite, and loose stools, along with insomnia and forgetfulness, indicate Heart and Spleen Qi and Blood Deficiency. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak and thready. This pattern combines the weakness of both organs.
A forceful, rapid heartbeat with a feeling of chest oppression, restlessness, and a bitter taste in the mouth suggests Phlegm-Fire harassing the Heart. The tongue is red with a thick, yellow, greasy coating, and the pulse is slippery and rapid-classic signs of heat and phlegm.
When the heartbeat is rapid yet feels weak, and the person has cold hands and feet, a pale puffy face, and fatigue, Heart Yang Deficiency is likely. The tongue is pale and swollen, and the pulse is deep, weak, and rapid, indicating the heart’s warming power has declined.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Heart and Spleen Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-and-spleen-qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fluttering or pounding sensation in the chest, Worse after exertion or eating, Poor appetite and bloating, Pale face and lips, Forgetfulness and poor concentration
- **Worse with**: Excessive worry and overthinking, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Overexertion and heavy exercise, Irregular meal times, Late nights and insufficient sleep
- **Better with**: Rest and calm activities, Warm, easily digested meals, Gentle walking after meals, Consistent sleep schedule, Nourishing soups with dates and longan
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is the engine that makes Qi and Blood from food. When Spleen Qi is weak, the body produces less Blood, and the Heart lacks the nourishment it needs to house the mind (Shen) calmly. The Heart then races, trying to keep up, causing a fast heartbeat that is especially noticeable at rest or after exertion, alongside fatigue and poor appetite.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: fine, weak, especially empty at the left Cun (Heart) position and soft at the right Guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen transforms food into Qi and Blood. Worry, irregular eating, and cold or greasy foods weaken this process, leaving the Heart without enough Blood to anchor the mind, so the heartbeat becomes fast and unsteady. Rest, warm meals, and nourishing foods like dates and longan help the Spleen rebuild Qi and Blood, steadying the Heart. Overexertion and lack of sleep further drain energy, so gentle movement and good sleep hygiene are protective.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals at regular times to support Spleen Qi. Include blood-nourishing foods like red dates, longan, and dark leafy greens. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that tax digestion. Gentle walking and calming activities help Qi flow without depleting it.

### Heart Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations that feel rapid but weak, often with a thready pulse, Difficulty falling asleep, with vivid dreams or restlessness, Pale face, lips, and nail beds, Dizziness or light-headedness, especially on standing, Poor memory and being easily startled
- **Worse with**: Overwork and prolonged fatigue, Excessive thinking or worry, Blood loss, e.g., heavy menstruation, Skipping meals or poor nutrition
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Eating warm, nourishing meals, Calming activities like meditation, Gentle, regular exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Heart depends on a steady supply of Blood to anchor the Shen (spirit) and keep its rhythm steady. When Heart Blood runs low, the Shen loses its stable home, becoming restless and easily startled. The heart then beats faster, almost as if trying to pump more nourishment to itself, producing a rapid, thready heartbeat that worsens with fatigue or after mental strain.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, possibly thin, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine (细 xì), weak (弱 ruò), and possibly choppy (涩 sè).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Blood is directly consumed by overthinking, stress, and blood loss. Rest and blood-nourishing foods provide the raw material the Heart needs to anchor the Shen, while calming activities quiet the mind and prevent further depletion. Gentle movement supports circulation without draining the already limited Blood reserves.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat blood-building foods like dark leafy greens, beets, red meat, dates, and goji berries. Avoid overwork and mental strain; prioritize restful sleep. Gentle exercise like walking or tai chi helps circulate blood without exhausting it.

### Heart Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations worse at night or at rest, Night sweats, Feeling of heat in palms, soles, and chest, Restless, anxious feeling with vivid disturbing dreams
- **Worse with**: Late nights and overwork, Spicy, fried, and drying foods, Stress and anxiety, Hot weather, Excessive sweating
- **Better with**: Rest and quiet, Cool drinks and moistening foods, Cool environment, Gentle breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Yin is the cooling, nourishing substance that keeps the Heart steady and the mind calm. When it runs low, a relative excess of Yang energy rises as empty-heat, agitating the Heart Shen (spirit). This inner restlessness makes the heart beat fast and thin, often flaring up at night when Yin should be dominant.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a redder tip, little or no coating, possible midline crack, dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin) and rapid, empty on deep pressure, especially weak at the left cun (Heart) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yin is like the water that cools and steadies the Heart's fire. Late nights, overwork, and emotional strain further deplete that water, allowing empty-heat to flare and speed up the heartbeat. Spicy, fried, and drying foods add heat, while excessive sweating drains fluids. Rest, quiet, cooling foods, and a calm environment help replenish Yin and settle the agitated Shen, allowing the heart rate to slow naturally.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the body cool and well-hydrated. Favor moistening foods like pears, lily bulb, black sesame, and congee; avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods. Wind down early and protect sleep-late nights directly consume Yin. Gentle, calming practices such as meditation or slow walking help anchor the mind.

### Heart Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations that worsen with even mild exertion, Shortness of breath after physical activity, Spontaneous sweating without heat, Fatigue and lack of stamina, Dizziness on exertion, Pale complexion, Mild anxiety or unease
- **Worse with**: Physical overexertion, Emotional stress or sudden fright, Cold, raw foods, Lack of sleep
- **Better with**: Rest and avoiding overexertion, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle breathing exercises, Quiet, calm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Qi is the force that keeps the heartbeat steady and strong. When that Qi is weak, the heart struggles to pump blood effectively, so it races to compensate - especially after even mild exertion or a sudden fright. The Shen (mind-spirit) becomes unsettled without enough Qi to anchor it, creating a nervous, fluttery awareness of the heartbeat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, with a thin white coat; a midline crack toward the tip may appear in chronic cases. Pulse: Empty (Xu) - soft and large superficially but disappears under pressure, weakest at the left Cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Qi is already deficient, so any demand on it - physical effort, emotional shock, even digestion of cold foods - depletes it further, causing the heart to race. Rest and calm conserve Qi, while warm, cooked foods are easily transformed into the Qi and Blood the heart needs. Gentle movement builds Qi gradually without straining it, and a quiet environment prevents the Shen from being startled, which would otherwise scatter Heart Qi.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize gentle, consistent activity like walking or tai chi rather than bursts of intense exercise. Eat warm, cooked meals with Qi-building foods such as rice, oats, chicken, dates, and root vegetables. Avoid cold, raw foods and iced drinks, which tax the Spleen Qi needed to produce Heart Qi. Rest as soon as you feel tired - pushing through drains the very energy your heart is asking for.

### Phlegm-Fire harassing the Heart
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fire-harassing-the-heart)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Forceful, rapid heartbeat, Chest oppression with thick yellow phlegm, Bitter taste in the mouth, Mental restlessness and agitation, Flushed red face
- **Worse with**: Spicy or greasy food, Alcohol, Emotional stress, Hot humid weather
- **Better with**: Light, cooling foods, Stress management, Gentle exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the digestive system fails to process fluids properly, it creates a sticky, heavy substance called phlegm. Emotional stress and dietary heat can then ignite that phlegm, turning it into phlegm-fire that rushes upward and disturbs the Heart. This agitation produces a forceful, rapid heartbeat, chest tightness, and a restless, irritable state of mind.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, swollen with red prickly tip, thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: slippery and rapid, forceful especially at the left Cun (Heart) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, spicy foods and alcohol create dampness and heat in the body, which combine into phlegm-fire - the very root of this pattern. Emotional stress stirs up fire and disrupts Qi flow, further agitating the Heart. Cooling, light foods and calming practices help clear heat and dissolve phlegm, while gentle movement prevents stagnation that feeds the fire.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and heavily sweetened foods that generate phlegm and heat. Emphasize light, cooling vegetables like celery, cucumber, and mung beans. Reduce alcohol and coffee. Gentle daily movement like walking or qigong helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation.

### Heart Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations that worsen with exertion, Cold hands and feet, feeling of cold, Bright pale or puffy face, Chest stuffiness or mild chest pain, Fatigue and low energy, Spontaneous sweating without heat
- **Worse with**: Fear and shock, Cold foods and iced drinks, Exposure to cold weather, Strenuous exercise or overwork
- **Better with**: Rest and staying warm, Warm environment, Warm ginger or cinnamon tea, Gentle walking, Moxibustion on chest and back
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Yang is the warm, driving force that keeps blood moving steadily through the body. When this Yang is weakened, the heart lacks the fire needed to pump with strength and rhythm. The heartbeat becomes rapid but weak, like a small engine revving too fast because it can't generate enough power. Because warmth is also lost, cold hands and feet, a pale puffy face, and chest stuffiness appear as blood and Qi stagnate in the chest.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Pale, puffy, tender tongue body with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse is deep and weak, often weakest at the left Cun (Heart) position; may be knotted (slow with irregular pauses).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yang is like a small fire inside the chest; cold extinguishes it, while warmth and rest stoke it. Cold foods, cold weather, and exhaustion drain Yang further, making the heartbeat more frantic and weak. Fear and shock scatter the Qi, directly weakening Heart Yang. In contrast, warm foods, moxibustion, and gentle movement gently feed the fire, helping the heart pump with steady strength.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the chest, back, and feet warm at all times. Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and bone broths; add a little ginger, cinnamon, or a pinch of black pepper to meals. Avoid raw salads, cold drinks, and iced foods. Gentle daily walking helps move Qi without exhausting Yang. Moxibustion at home on the lower abdomen (Qihai REN-6) and lower leg (Zusanli ST-36) can be very supportive.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### What does TCM consider the root cause of a fast heartbeat?
TCM sees palpitations as a sign that the Heart’s Shen (spirit) is unsettled. This usually happens because the Heart isn’t receiving enough Qi, Blood, Yin, or Yang to stay calm, or because a disruptive force like Phlegm-Fire is agitating it. The root cause often lies in another organ-a weak Spleen that can’t produce enough Blood, for example, or emotional stress that has knotted the Liver Qi.

### Can acupuncture help with heart palpitations?
Yes. Acupuncture can calm the nervous system and regulate heart rhythm by balancing the flow of Qi. Points like Shenmen (HT-7) and Neiguan (PC-6) are particularly effective for settling the Shen and steadying the heartbeat. Many people feel a sense of calm during the treatment itself, and regular sessions can reduce both the frequency and intensity of palpitations over time.

### How long does TCM treatment for palpitations take?
That depends on the pattern. Excess patterns like Phlegm-Fire may respond in a few weeks. Deficiency patterns, which are more common, often require 3-6 months of consistent herbs and acupuncture to rebuild the body’s reserves. Most patients notice some improvement within the first month-better sleep, less anxiety, or fewer episodes.

### Can I take TCM herbs alongside my beta-blockers?
In most cases, yes, but it’s essential to coordinate with both your cardiologist and your TCM practitioner. Some herbs, like Dang Gui (Angelica sinensis), may interact with blood-thinning medications. Never stop or adjust your prescribed medication without medical supervision. Always bring a complete list of your medications to your TCM consultation.

### Is it safe to treat palpitations with TCM during pregnancy?
Many acupuncture points and gentle herbal formulas are safe during pregnancy, but some herbs and points are contraindicated because they can stimulate uterine contractions. Always tell your practitioner if you are pregnant or trying to conceive. With proper precautions, TCM can be a supportive option for pregnancy-related palpitations, which often stem from Blood deficiency.

### What lifestyle changes does TCM recommend for palpitations?
Eat warm, cooked meals at regular times to support Spleen Qi. Avoid excessive cold or raw foods, caffeine, alcohol, and greasy, spicy dishes that generate Phlegm-Heat. Gentle exercise like walking or tai chi helps move Qi without overtaxing the Heart. Prioritize rest and a consistent sleep schedule, and consider calming practices like meditation to settle the Shen.

### Will my palpitations come back after TCM treatment?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance, not just mask the symptom. When the root pattern is fully resolved, palpitations often do not return. However, if the lifestyle or emotional triggers that caused the imbalance reappear, the pattern can recur. Your practitioner will usually recommend periodic “tune-up” treatments and self-care strategies to maintain balance.
