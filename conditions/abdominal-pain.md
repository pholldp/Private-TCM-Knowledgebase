## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, abdominal pain is a symptom rather than a diagnosis. It can arise from any structure in the digestive tract - stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, gallbladder, pancreas - as well as from the urinary or reproductive systems. Doctors classify it by location (upper, lower, right, left), onset (acute or chronic), and character (cramping, burning, stabbing), then use blood tests, imaging, and endoscopy to rule out serious causes like appendicitis, ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, or gallstones.
When no structural or infectious cause is found, the pain is often labelled functional - as in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) or functional dyspepsia. These conditions are real and distressing, but they don't show up on a scan. The conventional approach then shifts to managing symptoms rather than addressing a specific disease process.

### Treatments
Mild abdominal pain is often treated with antacids, acid-suppressing medications (PPIs), or antispasmodics. If an infection is present, antibiotics may be prescribed. For chronic functional pain, low-dose antidepressants, cognitive behavioral therapy, and dietary modifications (low-FODMAP, gluten-free) are common strategies. Pain relievers like NSAIDs are used cautiously because they can irritate the stomach lining. The goal is usually to reduce discomfort and improve quality of life while monitoring for any change that might signal a more serious underlying condition.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM views the abdomen as the central hub where the Spleen, Stomach, Liver, and Intestines work together to transform food into energy and move everything downward in a smooth, orderly way. Pain arises whenever this smooth flow - what we call Qi - gets stuck. The character of the pain tells us exactly what is causing the stuckness. A distending, wandering pain that moves around the belly and eases after belching or passing gas is Qi stagnation, usually driven by emotional stress that tightens the Liver's influence over digestion.
A sudden, severe cramping pain that feels icy inside and loves a hot water bottle points to Cold invading the Interior. This often happens after exposure to cold wind or eating too much cold food. The Cold literally congeals the Qi and blood, causing an abrupt blockage. On the other hand, a heavy, bloated pain that worsens with pressure and comes with sticky, urgent bowel movements is Damp-Heat - a pattern of inflammatory stagnation that thrives on greasy, rich foods and hot, humid environments.
Pain that hits shortly after a heavy meal, with severe bloating, nausea, and sour regurgitation, is Food Stagnation. Here, the Stomach simply cannot process the load, and undigested food sits and blocks the normal downward movement. A dull, lingering ache that feels better with warmth and gentle pressure is Spleen Yang Deficiency - the digestive fire has weakened, and internal cold slows everything down. Finally, a fixed, stabbing pain that refuses pressure and worsens at night is Blood Stagnation, a deeper, more stubborn blockage that often develops after long-standing Qi stagnation or injury.
Because these six patterns have such different roots, the same Western diagnosis - say, IBS - could be treated as Liver Qi Stagnation in one person, Spleen Yang Deficiency in another, and Damp-Heat in a third. TCM doesn't just ask where it hurts; it asks how it hurts, when it hurts, and what makes it better. That is the key to a treatment that actually matches your body's story.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first asks about the character of the pain. A distending, wandering ache that moves around the belly and eases after belching or passing gas strongly suggests Liver Qi Stagnation (肝气郁结). The tongue is often pale-red with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels wiry. Stress or emotional upset usually makes it worse, which is a key clue.
If the pain came on suddenly after exposure to cold and feels like a tight cramp that improves with a hot water bottle, Exterior Cold invading the Interior (寒邪内阻) is likely. The tongue appears pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse is deep and tight. The person may also feel chilled and prefer to curl up.
When the belly feels heavy and bloated, with pain that worsens when pressed, and there are sticky bowel movements or constipation with a sensation of incomplete emptying, Damp-Heat in the Large Intestine (大肠湿热) is suspected. The tongue is red with a yellow, greasy coat, and the pulse is slippery and rapid. A feeling of thirst or a stuffy sensation in the chest often accompanies it.
Pain that appears shortly after overeating, with severe bloating, nausea, sour regurgitation, and a thick, greasy tongue coating points to Food Stagnation in the Stomach (食积胃肠). The pain refuses pressure, and the pulse feels slippery and full. A recent history of heavy or rich meals is the giveaway.
A dull, lingering ache that feels better when you press on it or apply warmth, and worsens when you are hungry or tired, indicates Spleen Yang Deficiency (脾阳虚). The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is deep and thin. The person often feels generally cold, especially in the hands and feet, and may have loose stools.
When the pain is fixed in one spot, feels stabbing or boring, and is definitely worse with pressure, Blood Stagnation (瘀血阻滞) is the likely diagnosis. The tongue may show purple spots or a dusky color, and the pulse is choppy. This pattern often develops after a long history of Qi stagnation or an old injury.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending or bloating pain that moves around, Pain radiates to the rib sides or flanks, Better after belching or passing gas, Worse with emotional stress or anger, Frequent sighing, Irritability or low mood
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Overthinking or worry
- **Better with**: Belching or passing gas, Gentle movement or walking, Applying warmth to the abdomen, Deep breathing and relaxation, Peppermint or chamomile tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress builds up, that flow stalls and Qi backs up in the abdomen, creating a distending, bloated sensation rather than a sharp pain. The pain often migrates because the trapped Qi moves around, and it characteristically eases after belching or passing gas, which releases some of the pent-up pressure.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body with red or slightly purple sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, most prominent at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Emotional stress directly knots the Liver Qi, so anger and frustration make the distension worse. Releasing trapped Qi through belching or passing gas brings almost instant relief because it clears the stagnation. Gentle movement and relaxation smooth the flow of Qi, while greasy or heavy food clogs the system and adds to the blockage.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals and avoid greasy, spicy, or deep-fried foods that burden the Liver. Sip peppermint or chamomile tea to gently move Qi. Daily gentle movement like walking, yoga, or tai chi helps keep Qi flowing. Make time for relaxation and deep breathing, especially when you feel tense.

### Exterior Cold invading the Interior
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/exterior-cold-invading-the-interior)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Severe abdominal pain that feels cold and improves with warmth, Watery diarrhoea with undigested food fragments, Cold hands and feet, Intense chilliness and aversion to cold, No thirst or preference for warm drinks, Nausea or vomiting of clear fluid
- **Worse with**: Exposure to cold (wind, drafts, cold surfaces), Cold or raw foods and iced beverages
- **Better with**: Applying warmth to the abdomen, Warm ginger tea, Rest (lying down, curled-up position, adequate sleep), Warm, easily digestible foods (soups, congee), Moxibustion on the navel
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a sudden, intense chill invades the body, the cold energy penetrates deep into the abdomen. It congeals there, freezing the normal flow of Qi and blood. This abrupt blockage triggers an acute, cramping pain that feels icy inside and immediately eases when warmth is applied.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, teeth-marked body with a white, moist, slippery coating. Pulse: deep (Chen), slow (Chi) or tight (Jin).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold has a contracting, congealing nature that freezes the flow of Qi and blood in the abdomen, causing acute pain. Warmth relaxes the constricted channels, disperses the cold, and restores normal circulation, which immediately eases the cramping. Cold foods and environments introduce more cold, worsening the blockage and pain.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the abdomen warm with a hot water bottle or heating pad. Eat warm, cooked foods like rice congee and broths; strictly avoid raw, cold, and iced items. Sip fresh ginger tea with a little brown sugar to warm the interior. Dress warmly, especially covering the belly, and avoid sitting on cold surfaces.

### Damp-Heat in the Large Intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-large-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Abdominal distention and pain that worsens with pressure, Urgent, frequent bowel movements with a burning sensation at the anus, Foul-smelling, yellow-brown stools that may contain mucus or blood, Feeling of incomplete evacuation after bowel movements, Thirst, scanty dark urine, and a heavy sensation in the body
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, humid weather, Overeating or heavy meals, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration
- **Better with**: Light, bland foods (congee, steamed vegetables), Cooling drinks (mung bean soup, chrysanthemum tea), Cool, dry environment, Gentle movement or walking, Rest (lying down, curled-up position, adequate sleep)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Damp-Heat settles in the Large Intestine when rich, greasy foods or hot, humid environments overload the digestive system. This creates a sticky, inflammatory obstruction that blocks the smooth flow of Qi and blood, causing the abdomen to feel distended and painful, especially when pressed. The Heat and Dampness irritate the bowel lining, triggering urgent, burning bowel movements that often feel incomplete.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating, thickest at centre and root. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat thrives on rich, heating foods and humid environments, which add more Dampness and Heat to the body, worsening the intestinal blockage and irritation. Cooling, light foods and a dry environment help drain Dampness and clear Heat, easing the pressure and burning sensation. Stress disrupts the Liver's ability to keep Qi flowing smoothly, which can further stagnate Qi in the abdomen and aggravate the pain.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, or sugary foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add Heat and Dampness. Focus on light, easy-to-digest meals like congee, steamed vegetables, and mung bean soup. Stay hydrated with room-temperature water or cooling herbal teas such as chrysanthemum. Gentle walking can help Qi flow, but rest when fatigue is present.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and distending pain worse after eating, Pain that refuses pressure, Sour, rotten-smelling belching and acid regurgitation, Relief after vomiting, Thick, greasy, curd-like tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Overeating or heavy meals, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Pressure on the abdomen or painful area, Eating late at night
- **Better with**: Vomiting, Warm, easily digestible foods (soups, congee), Gentle movement or walking, Rest (lying down, curled-up position, adequate sleep)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you overeat or eat heavy, greasy foods that are hard to digest, the stomach cannot process the load. The undigested food sits and stagnates, physically blocking the normal downward movement of Stomach Qi. This creates a sensation of bloating, distention, and pain that feels worse with pressure. As the food ferments, it produces sour belching, acid reflux, and a rotten smell. Vomiting brings relief because it clears the blockage and allows Qi to descend again.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy, curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and full (实 shí), strongest at the right middle position (Stomach).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating and greasy foods overload the digestive system, creating a physical blockage that prevents Qi from descending. This stagnation is what causes the painful distension and sour reflux. Vomiting is the body's way of forcibly clearing the blockage, which is why pain is immediately relieved afterward. Light, warm foods and gentle movement help Qi descend and aid digestion, while pressure on the abdomen or lying down right after a meal worsens the obstruction.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, lighter meals and avoid greasy, fried, or heavily processed foods. Chew thoroughly and stop eating before you feel completely full. A short period of eating only light congee or soup can help the stomach recover. Gentle walking after meals encourages the downward movement of Qi.

### Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, lingering pain that is not severe, Pain eases with warmth and gentle pressure, Loose, watery stools with undigested food, Cold hands and feet, dislikes cold, Fatigue and heavy limbs
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and iced beverages, Skipping meals or hunger, Exposure to cold (wind, drafts, cold surfaces), Overwork and fatigue, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration
- **Better with**: Applying warmth to the abdomen, Gentle pressure or massage, Rest (lying down, curled-up position, adequate sleep), Warm, easily digestible foods (soups, congee), Warm ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen’s Yang is the body’s digestive fire. When it weakens, internal cold develops because there isn’t enough warmth to power digestion and keep Qi moving. This cold causes the abdominal muscles and channels to contract and the flow of Qi to slow down, producing a dull, lingering ache. Because the root is a lack of warmth, the pain feels better with heat and gentle pressure, which temporarily boost circulation and ease the stagnation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, and tender with teeth marks and a white, slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Warmth and rest act like adding fuel to a low fire: they temporarily boost deficient Spleen Yang, helping Qi to flow and easing the dull ache. Cold, raw foods, and exhaustion do the opposite-they snuff out that weak flame, allowing internal cold to build and pain to return. Gentle pressure also helps by encouraging local Qi movement through the sluggish abdomen.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Make warm, cooked meals the foundation of your diet-think soups, stews, and congees. Add a little fresh ginger, cinnamon, or a pinch of black pepper to your cooking to stoke the digestive fire. Avoid raw salads, iced drinks, and dairy, which dampen and cool the Spleen. Keep your abdomen covered and warm, especially in cold weather, and prioritize rest when you feel fatigued.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain that does not move, Pain that worsens with pressure, Worsens at night, Dark purplish complexion, lips, or nails, Dark menstrual blood with clots
- **Worse with**: Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Cold or raw foods and iced beverages, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Pressure on the abdomen or painful area
- **Better with**: Gentle movement or walking, Applying warmth to the abdomen, Turmeric or ginger tea, Deep breathing and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the smooth flow of Qi in the abdomen is blocked over time, blood can become sluggish and form stasis. This stagnant blood acts like a bruise inside, obstructing the channels and causing a fixed, knifelike pain that does not move. Because it is a deep, physical blockage, pressure on the area makes it worse, and the pain often intensifies at night when the body's energy turns inward and blood flow naturally slows.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè), rough and uneven; may be wiry (弦 xián) if Qi stagnation is involved.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Blood stasis is a physical blockage that needs movement and warmth to dissolve. Gentle exercise and heat encourage blood to flow, reducing stagnation, while cold and prolonged sitting cause blood to congeal, worsening the pain. Emotional stress tightens the Liver Qi, which further obstructs blood flow, making the stabbing sensation more intense. Pressure directly compresses the blocked channels, which is why the pain refuses touch and feels better when the area is left alone.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the abdomen warm with a heating pad or warm compress, and avoid cold drinks and raw foods that can congeal blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or stretching helps keep blood flowing smoothly; avoid long periods of sitting still. Include blood-moving spices in your cooking, such as turmeric, ginger, and garlic.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help with stomach pain?
Yes. Acupuncture works by unblocking the flow of Qi in the channels that run through the abdomen. Specific points on the abdomen, legs, and back are chosen to either move stagnation, warm cold, clear heat, or strengthen deficiency. Many patients feel a soothing, relaxed sensation during the session, and pain often eases within the first few treatments.

### How quickly will I feel better?
It depends on the pattern. Sudden pain from cold or overeating can resolve in as little as one or two sessions. Chronic, stress-related pain or dull aches from a weak digestion usually require several weeks of regular treatment - weekly acupuncture combined with daily herbal formulas is the most common approach. Your practitioner will give you a realistic timeline after your first consultation.

### What if my pain is from stress?
Stress is a major trigger for the Liver Qi Stagnation pattern, which causes distending, migrating pain that eases after belching or passing gas. TCM addresses this by smoothing the flow of Liver Qi with herbs like Chai Hu and acupuncture points like Taichong (LR-3). Many patients notice that as their emotional tension eases, their belly pain does too.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a big role in TCM because the Spleen and Stomach are directly affected by what we eat. While pattern-specific advice varies, most people benefit from eating warm, cooked foods, avoiding icy drinks and raw salads, and chewing thoroughly. Your practitioner will guide you on which foods to favor and which to avoid based on your specific pattern.

### Is it safe to combine TCM with my current medication?
In most cases, yes. Acupuncture is very safe alongside conventional treatments. Herbal formulas can also be combined, but you must tell both your TCM practitioner and your doctor everything you are taking. Some herbs - especially those that move blood - can interact with anticoagulants. Never stop a prescribed medication without consulting your doctor first.

### Can TCM help if doctors can't find a cause?
Absolutely. Functional abdominal pain - where tests come back normal - is one of the areas where TCM shines. Because TCM diagnoses based on the pattern of symptoms, not just lab results, it can offer a clear treatment plan even when Western medicine has no name for the condition. Many people with IBS or functional dyspepsia find significant relief through TCM.

### Will the pain come back after treatment?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance, not just mask the pain. When the root pattern is resolved, the pain should not return as long as you maintain a balanced lifestyle. Some chronic patterns may need occasional maintenance sessions, but many patients remain pain-free long after their course of treatment ends.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for irritable bowel syndrome: systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Manheimer E, Wieland LS, Cheng K, et al. Am J Gastroenterol. 2012;107(6):835-847.*
   A comprehensive meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials showing that acupuncture significantly reduces abdominal pain and improves overall IBS symptoms compared to sham acupuncture or conventional medication.

2. **Tong Xie Yao Fang for diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review**
   *Journal/Publication: Liu J, Wang L, Li J, et al. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2014;2014:789024.*
   This systematic review evaluated the efficacy of the classical Chinese herbal formula Tong Xie Yao Fang for IBS-D, finding that it reduces abdominal pain and normalizes bowel movements with a good safety profile.

3. **Efficacy of Chinese herbal medicine for functional dyspepsia: a meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Xiao Y, Liu Y, Huang S, et al. J Gastroenterol Hepatol. 2015;30(3):468-478.*
   A meta-analysis of double-blind, placebo-controlled trials demonstrated that Chinese herbal medicine is more effective than placebo in relieving postprandial fullness, epigastric pain, and abdominal bloating in functional dyspepsia.
