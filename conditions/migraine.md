## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Migraine is a neurological condition characterised by recurrent episodes of moderate to severe headache, typically on one side of the head, often pulsating in quality. Episodes last between 4 and 72 hours and are frequently accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and sensitivity to light and sound. Some patients experience an aura — visual disturbances such as zigzag lines or blind spots — in the 20-60 minutes before the headache begins.
Migraine affects roughly 12% of the global population, with women affected about three times more frequently than men. It typically first appears in adolescence or early adulthood. The condition is understood as a disorder of neurovascular regulation, involving cortical spreading depression, activation of the trigeminovascular system, and release of neuropeptides including CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide). Genetic factors play a significant role, with a family history present in the majority of patients.

### Treatments
Acute treatment typically involves triptans (sumatriptan, rizatriptan), NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), or combination analgesics, taken at the onset of symptoms. For patients with frequent attacks, preventive medications are prescribed daily — these include beta-blockers (propranolol), antidepressants (amitriptyline), anticonvulsants (topiramate), and the newer CGRP monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab). Non-pharmaceutical approaches include identifying and avoiding triggers, maintaining regular sleep and meal schedules, and cognitive behavioural therapy.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands migraine primarily through the Liver and its relationship with other organ systems. The Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body, and when this flow is disrupted — by emotional stress, frustration, or constitutional tendency — Qi stagnates and transforms into Heat or Wind that rises to the head. This is why stress is such a universal migraine trigger: it directly disrupts the Liver's core function.
But the Liver is only part of the story. The Gallbladder channel (Shào Yáng) physically traverses the temporal region where migraine pain most commonly strikes — the temples and the side of the head. When pathogenic factors lodge in this channel, whether internal (Liver Fire, rising Yang) or external (Wind-Cold, Wind-Heat), pain follows that characteristic one-sided pathway.
Beyond excess patterns, migraines also arise from deficiency. When Blood or Qi are insufficient — from overwork, poor diet, chronic illness, or heavy menstruation — the head simply doesn't receive adequate nourishment. These deficiency migraines tend to feel different: duller, often worse with fatigue, and relieved rather than aggravated by rest. Women are particularly susceptible to Blood Deficiency migraines around menstruation, which aligns with the observed Western epidemiology.
A third mechanism involves obstruction. Blood stagnation (old, stuck blood) or Phlegm-Dampness can physically block the channels running through the head. Stagnation migraines are characteristically fixed in location, stabbing in quality, and often have a long history. Phlegm migraines come with a heavy, foggy quality, nausea, and a feeling that the head is "wrapped in cotton." These obstruction patterns often explain why some migraines are so resistant to conventional treatment — the underlying blockage hasn't been addressed.
Most migraines that have lasted more than a few years are not pure single patterns — they are combinations. The most common chronic-migraine picture seen in TCM clinics worldwide is Liver Yang Rising layered over Phlegm-Dampness: stress drives Yang upward, which over years weakens the Spleen's ability to transform fluids, and the accumulated Phlegm then provides the substrate that the rising Yang stirs. Patients with this combination get the throbbing temple pain of Yang Rising AND the dull heaviness, nausea, and weather sensitivity of Phlegm — both at once, or alternating. Effective treatment must address both simultaneously, which is why a single-formula approach often plateaus in chronic cases.
Menstrual migraines have their own internal logic that maps onto the cycle. In the days before the period, Liver Qi tends to stagnate as the body prepares to discharge, producing the classic premenstrual one-sided throbbing attack. During the bleed itself, the moving Blood can become stuck — Blood Stagnation migraines arrive on the heaviest flow days, often with clots and cramping. After the period, the freshly emptied Blood reserves leave many women in a transient state of Blood Deficiency, producing dull post-menses headaches that respond to rest and nourishment but not to painkillers. The three menstrual sub-patterns share an underlying root but call for very different treatments, which is why generic painkillers help some women's monthly migraines and not others.
Migraine with aura — the visual zigzags, blind spots, or shimmering lights some patients experience 20-60 minutes before the pain — is understood in TCM as Liver Wind stirring. Just as physical wind moves leaves before a storm arrives, internal Liver Wind moves the spirit and the sensory orifices before the migraine itself strikes. This is one of the few cases where TCM and Western neurology converge on similar mechanisms: the cortical spreading depression of the Western model is a propagating disturbance, much like Wind. Patients with aura migraine typically need Wind-extinguishing herbs (Tiān Má, Gōu Téng) alongside their pattern-specific treatment.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM diagnostic consultation for migraine is a series of pattern-finding questions — the practitioner is matching your specific presentation to one (or a combination) of the five patterns. The questions can sound surprisingly specific:
- Where the pain sits. Temples and side-of-head, vertex (top), forehead, or occiput (back of head) — each maps onto a different channel and points to a different organ system.
- What worsens or relieves it. Stress, food, weather, alcohol, menstrual phase, lying down, pressure, sleep — the trigger profile narrows the pattern faster than any other question.
- Other body signals. Nausea, vision changes, mood, energy, digestion, sleep quality, menstrual regularity — accompanying symptoms confirm the pattern.
- How attacks cycle. Frequency, time of day, relationship to the cycle, response to previous treatments — these distinguish recent from chronic, single-pattern from combined.
The tongue and pulse examination then confirms the picture. A red tongue with wiry pulse points to Liver excess; a pale tongue with thin pulse confirms Qi and Blood Deficiency; a swollen tongue with greasy coat indicates Phlegm; a purple tongue with choppy pulse marks Blood Stagnation.
Pain location → channel → likely pattern, at a glance:
- Temples and sides → Gallbladder (Shào Yáng) channel → Liver Yang Rising, Liver Fire Blazing, or Blood Stagnation
- Vertex (top of head) → Liver (Jué Yīn) channel → Liver Yang Rising or Qi and Blood Deficiency
- Forehead → Stomach (Yáng Míng) channel → Phlegm-Dampness or sinusitis-pattern
- Back of head / occiput → Bladder (Tài Yáng) channel → external Wind-Cold invasion or cervicogenic component
- Whole head, heavy and wrapped → Spleen pattern with Phlegm → Phlegm-Dampness
- Inside the head, deep and persistent → Kidney pattern → constitutional deficiency root

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Throbbing temple pain, Distending pressure outward, Dizziness during attacks, Tinnitus during attacks, Irritability before headache, Worse with anger or stress, Dream-disturbed sleep, Heat rising to face, Worse late afternoon or evening
- **Worse with**: Stress, Anger, Frustration, Overwork, Insufficient sleep, Alcohol, Skipping meals, Loud noise, Bright light, Premenstrual phase
- **Better with**: Dark quiet room, Sleep, Stress reduction, Cool compress on temples, Regular meal times, Gentle outdoor walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Liver's Yin (its cooling, anchoring aspect) becomes depleted — through chronic stress, overwork, insufficient sleep, or constitutional tendency — the Liver's Yang (its active, rising energy) is no longer held in check. This Yang energy surges upward to the head like heat rising, producing distending, throbbing pain typically at the temples or the side of the head. This is the classic stress migraine: it builds with tension and frustration, often erupts at the end of a demanding week, and comes with a characteristic feeling of pressure expanding outward from inside the skull.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, especially at the sides (the Liver area), with a thin yellow coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, sometimes rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress, anger and frustration directly surge Liver Qi upward — they ARE the mechanism for this pattern, not just associated triggers. Alcohol and skipped meals amplify the surge by adding heat and depleting the Yin that would otherwise anchor it. The relief side is the inverse: a dark quiet room removes the sensory drivers (light, noise) that keep Yang ascending; sleep restores the Yin reserves that cool the Liver; cool compresses physically draw Yang downward; and regular meals stabilise the Yin-depletion cycle that sets up the next attack.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: The single most actionable change for this pattern: run a one-week trial of cutting alcohol, coffee, chocolate, aged cheese, and spicy food entirely. These all generate Heat that fuels rising Yang, and a week is long enough to see whether your attack frequency drops. From there, favour foods that nourish Liver Yin and calm Yang: celery, chrysanthemum tea, mulberries, dark leafy greens, mung beans, peppermint tea. Regular moderate exercise — especially walking, swimming, or tai chi — helps smooth Liver Qi and prevent stagnation from transforming into rising Yang. Stress management is not optional for this pattern; it is treatment. Regular sleep schedules matter more than total sleep hours.

### Liver Fire Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Severe burning pain, Bursting quality, Bitter taste in mouth, Constipation with dry stools, Thirst for cold drinks, Intense irritability, Anger outbursts, Red face during attacks, Red eyes during attacks, Dark scanty urine, Possible nosebleeds
- **Worse with**: Anger, Loud arguments, Suppressed frustration, Alcohol, Spicy food, Greasy food, Hot weather, Sleep deprivation
- **Better with**: Rest, Emotional calm, Cool environment, Cold compress on head, Bland diet, Bitter cool foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  This is the more intense cousin of Liver Yang Rising. Where Yang Rising is like a pot slowly boiling over, Liver Fire is an explosion. Prolonged emotional frustration, suppressed anger, or excessive alcohol and spicy food cause Liver Qi stagnation to transform into actual Fire. This Fire blazes upward along the Gallbladder and Liver channels to the head, producing intense, severe migraines with a burning quality. These tend to be the most painful migraines patients describe — many report feeling like their head might burst open.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red with a dry yellow coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and rapid (数 shuò), often forceful.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Liver Fire is what Liver Qi stagnation turns into when it's been suppressed too long — like a pressure cooker eventually whistling. Anger, alcohol and spicy/greasy food add fuel to a Fire that's already burning, which is why each one can trigger an attack on its own and the combination can be devastating. A cool environment, cold compresses and bitter-cool foods (cucumber, watercress, chrysanthemum tea) physically and energetically draw Fire downward and outward. Bland food gives the Liver nothing to ignite. Emotional calm is the long-term cure; suppressed frustration is the long-term cause.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: The single most impactful change: strictly avoid alcohol, chilli, pepper, garlic, onion, lamb, and deep-fried foods for the duration of treatment. All of these add fuel to the Fire. Favour bitter-cool foods: bitter melon, dandelion greens, watercress, cucumber, watermelon, green tea, chrysanthemum tea. Emotional regulation is essential — this pattern demands that the patient find healthy outlets for frustration rather than suppressing it. Vigorous exercise can help discharge accumulated Liver energy between attacks, but should be avoided during active episodes.

### Damp-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy wrapped head feeling, Dull pressure pain, Strong nausea with attacks, Possible vomiting, Poor appetite, Bloating after eating, Mental fogginess, Heaviness in body and limbs, Excessive mucus, Worse in damp weather, Worse after heavy meals
- **Worse with**: Humid weather, Rainy days, Dairy, Greasy food, Fried food, Overeating, Alcohol especially beer, Sedentary lifestyle, Excessive mental work
- **Better with**: Light eating, Ginger tea, Regular meal times, Physical activity, Warmth, Dry weather
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's ability to transform and transport fluids weakens — from poor diet, overthinking, or constitutional tendency — fluids accumulate and thicken into Phlegm. Phlegm in TCM is not limited to respiratory mucus; it includes any pathological fluid accumulation. This Phlegm rises and obstructs the clear orifices of the head, producing a migraine with a distinctive quality: heavy, foggy, as if the head were wrapped in a wet towel. This is the migraine that arrives with damp weather, after heavy or dairy-rich meals, and in people who feel generally sluggish and mentally foggy. The prominent nausea and dizziness reflect Phlegm's turbid, downward-pulling nature.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and swollen, with a thick greasy white coat. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), sometimes also wiry.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm is made — every day, by what you eat and how active you are. Humid and rainy weather is external Dampness that adds to your internal load; dairy, fried and greasy food directly produce Phlegm in the Spleen; overeating and irregular meals exhaust the Spleen's ability to transform what you're eating. Ginger, light eating, physical activity and warm dry weather all do the opposite — they support the Spleen's transformative function and physically move accumulated Damp out of the body. This pattern is by far the most diet-responsive of all migraine types; the same patient can cut their attack frequency in half within a month of strict dietary change.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: This pattern is the most diet-responsive of all migraine types — give the dietary change a fair trial before assuming you've plateaued on treatment. Strictly avoid dairy products, greasy or fried food, refined sugar, excessive wheat and gluten, cold or raw food, and alcohol (especially beer). Favour Spleen-strengthening, Dampness-draining foods: cooked barley, mung beans, aduki beans, cooked vegetables, ginger (fresh, in cooking or as tea), white rice congee, lotus seeds. Eat smaller meals at regular times — the Spleen weakens with large or irregular meals. Regular vigorous exercise is strongly recommended as it moves Qi and helps the body transform Dampness. Avoid damp living environments if possible.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing pain, Always in the same exact spot, Worse at night, Long-standing migraine history, Cold hands and feet, Dark or dusky complexion, Visible small veins on cheeks, Purple or dark tongue, Treatment-resistant in conventional medicine, History of head injury or surgery
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Night-time, Sitting still for long periods, Premenstrual phase, Alcohol
- **Better with**: Gentle movement, Light exercise, Massage, Warmth, Heated compresses, Regular acupuncture
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Blood Stagnation in the head channels is the residue of any process that has slowed circulation in the cranium for long enough: years of recurrent Yang-Rising attacks, an old head injury, post-surgical scar tissue, or chronic Liver Qi stagnation that finally crossed over from impeding Qi to impeding Blood itself. The headache it produces is unmistakable — the pain stays in the exact same square inch of head every time, has a stabbing or boring quality rather than a throbbing one, and is characteristically worse at night when circulation slows. This is most commonly the diagnosis in patients who have had migraines for ten or more years and have tried multiple preventive medications without lasting benefit.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or with visible purple spots, often with distended sub-lingual veins. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè), feels rough or uneven under the finger.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood moves when the body is warm, when Qi is flowing, and when there's gentle activity. Cold contracts the vessels and slows the flow; sitting still removes the muscular pump that helps Blood return; night-time naturally slows circulation. Each of these allows existing stagnation to consolidate and produces a worse attack the next day. The reliefs are the inverse — anything that warms, moves, or massages the area helps disperse the stagnation. This is the one migraine pattern where staying physically active during the build-up phase actually helps, rather than resting and waiting it out as the Liver excess patterns require.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favour Blood-moving foods: black beans, eggplant, vinegar, turmeric, saffron (cooking quantity), aduki beans, chives, leeks, hawthorn berries, dark grape juice. Limit cold and raw foods and iced drinks, which contract circulation. The single most important lifestyle change is daily gentle aerobic activity — even a brisk 20-minute walk moves Blood through stagnant areas in a way that no herb fully replicates. Avoid prolonged sitting; if your work requires it, set a timer to stand and move every 30 minutes. Heated compresses (not cold) on the painful area during the build-up phase help disperse stagnation; if cold compresses helped during a Yang Rising migraine years ago, that doesn't apply here.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull aching pain, Cold hands and feet, Frequent fatigue, Light-headedness on standing, Palpitations, Attacks after menstruation, Pale complexion, Easy bruising, Dry hair and brittle nails
- **Worse with**: Fatigue, Exertion, Prolonged standing, Sleep deprivation, Skipping meals, After menstruation, Blood loss
- **Better with**: Rest, Sleep, Lying down, Eating, Nourishing food, Gentle pressure on painful area, Warmth
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The head is the highest and most metabolically demanding part of the body, and it depends on a continuous upward supply of Qi (energy) and Blood (nourishment) to function pain-free. When Qi and Blood are insufficient — from overwork, prolonged illness, post-partum recovery, heavy menstruation, vegetarian diets without compensating planning, or chronic dieting — the head simply doesn't receive enough nourishment, and a dull aching migraine develops. The signature of this pattern is the opposite of every excess pattern: the migraine gets BETTER with rest, food and gentle pressure on the painful area. In women, attacks reliably cluster in the days after the period finishes, when Blood stores are at their lowest point of the cycle.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, often thin or with teeth marks at the edges, with a thin white coat. Pulse: thin (细 xì) and weak (弱 ruò), sometimes also slow.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is a deficit problem, not an excess problem. Fatigue, exertion, blood loss and skipping meals all deplete the already-insufficient Qi and Blood reserves; the migraine is the head signalling that it has run out of supply. Rest and sleep allow the body to rebuild reserves; food (especially nourishing iron-rich, Blood-building food) provides raw material; gentle pressure on the painful area supports local Blood flow when the systemic supply is too weak to maintain it on its own. This is why the same actions that worsen a Liver Yang Rising migraine — pressing on the painful spot, lying down without a dark room — actively help a Qi and Blood Deficiency migraine.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: The single most impactful change: eat warm, cooked, iron-rich and protein-adequate food at consistent times every day. Smoothies, raw salads, intermittent fasting and skipped meals all directly worsen this pattern, regardless of how healthy they sound. Build daily meals around: red meat or organ meats once or twice a week (or generous plant iron with vitamin C if vegetarian — lentils, black beans, dark leafy greens, tahini), eggs, bone broth, congee with goji berries and red dates, beetroot, dark grape juice. Eat breakfast within an hour of waking. Avoid working through lunch. Limit vigorous cardio during the rebuilding phase — it depletes Qi faster than gentle yoga, tai chi or walking can replace it. If your migraines reliably arrive after your period, plan extra rest and nourishment for the three days after the bleed ends, which is the highest-risk window.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Does acupuncture for migraines hurt?
Acupuncture needles are far thinner than the needles used for injections or blood tests — most people feel a brief pinch followed by a dull, heavy or tingling ache. TCM calls that ache de qi and treats it as a sign the point is working, not a problem. For migraines, many of the key points sit on the hands, feet and lower legs rather than on the head itself, so sessions are usually relaxing — a lot of patients doze off on the table. If you have a needle phobia, say so: acupressure, ear seeds and gentler techniques can achieve much of the same effect.

### How long before I see results?
It depends on your pattern, and being specific is more useful than being optimistic. Excess patterns — Liver Yang Rising, Liver Fire, Phlegm-Dampness — often respond within 2–4 weeks of weekly acupuncture plus daily herbs, usually as shorter, milder attacks before the frequency itself drops. Deficiency (Qi and Blood Deficiency) and long-standing Blood Stagnation take longer — typically 3–6 months — because the body is rebuilding reserves or clearing an old blockage rather than simply calming something down. Chronic migraines that combine patterns (commonly Liver Yang layered over Phlegm) sit in between and usually need both addressed at once.

### Can I take Chinese herbs at the same time as my migraine medication?
Usually yes — TCM is most often used alongside conventional treatment, not instead of it, and many people combine herbs with triptans, painkillers or preventives such as propranolol or amitriptyline without trouble. That said, some herbs can interact with medication, so don't start a formula without telling both your prescriber and a qualified TCM practitioner exactly what you take. And never stop a prescribed migraine medication on your own — the aim is to gradually need it less as the underlying pattern improves, ideally with your doctor involved.

### Is TCM safe for migraines during pregnancy?
Acupuncture is generally regarded as a safe, drug-free option for migraine in pregnancy and is often preferred over herbs — particularly in the first trimester. But several acupuncture points and many herbs are contraindicated during pregnancy, so this is not a do-it-yourself situation. Always work with a practitioner experienced in treating pregnant patients, and tell them you're pregnant (or trying) before any treatment. The Safety section above has more on what changes during pregnancy.

### Do I have to take herbs forever?
No. Herbal treatment for migraines is meant to be a course, not a life sentence. Once the pattern is corrected and attacks have settled, most people taper off the herbs and hold the result with diet, lifestyle and the occasional short top-up around known triggers — the premenstrual week, for example. Deficiency patterns can need a longer rebuilding phase, but the goal is always to need less over time, not more.

### I get an aura before my migraines — can TCM help with that?
Yes. In TCM the visual aura — the zigzags, blind spots or shimmering lights that arrive 20–60 minutes before the pain — is understood as Liver Wind stirring, much like wind moving leaves before a storm. Treatment typically adds Wind-extinguishing herbs such as Tiān Má and Gōu Téng to whichever pattern-specific formula you need. One important caveat: if your aura is brand new, suddenly different from your usual one, or comes with weakness, slurred speech or vision loss that doesn't fully clear, treat it as a medical emergency first — see the Safety section.

### Will I have to change my diet?
Some dietary change usually helps, but it's targeted to your pattern rather than a single "migraine diet". Liver Fire and Liver Yang types are typically asked to cut alcohol, coffee and very spicy or greasy food; Phlegm-Dampness types benefit from less dairy, sugar and cold or raw food; deficiency types do better with regular, warm, nourishing meals and not skipping breakfast. The General dietary guidance and the per-pattern Diet & lifestyle notes above give the specifics. Most people find that a few consistent changes matter more than a drastic overhaul.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for the prevention of episodic migraine (Cochrane Review, 2016)** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001218.pub3))
   *Journal/Publication: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2016, (6), CD001218.*
   Acupuncture works as well as preventive migraine drugs — and causes fewer side effects. If you're already treating attacks when they hit, adding regular acupuncture on top cuts how often the attacks happen in the first place.

2. **Acupuncture versus topiramate for chronic migraine prophylaxis (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2017)** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.9378))
   *Journal/Publication: Zhao L, Chen J, Li Y, et al. JAMA Internal Medicine, 2017, 177(4), 508–515.*
   Twenty real-acupuncture sessions over six months cut migraine days by three more per month than fake acupuncture, and matched topiramate (a standard preventive drug) for effectiveness — with far fewer side effects than the drug.

3. **Manual acupuncture versus sham acupuncture for migraine without aura (BMJ, 2020)** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m697))
   *Journal/Publication: Li Y, Liang F, Yang X, et al. BMJ, 2020, 368, m697.*
   Eight weeks of real acupuncture (20 sessions in total) significantly reduced attack frequency compared to fake acupuncture — and the benefit was still measurable five months after treatment ended, suggesting the underlying pattern is being corrected rather than just suppressed.
