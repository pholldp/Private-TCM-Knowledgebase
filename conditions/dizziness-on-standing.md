## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Western medicine typically views dizziness on standing as orthostatic hypotension-a temporary drop in blood pressure when you stand up, often from dehydration, medications, or nervous system dysfunction. It can also be linked to inner ear disorders, anemia, or cardiac problems. Diagnosis usually involves measuring blood pressure changes from sitting to standing, and may include blood tests or tilt-table testing.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment focuses on managing underlying causes: increasing fluid and salt intake for dehydration, adjusting medications, wearing compression stockings, or using drugs that raise blood pressure. For inner ear issues, vestibular rehabilitation or medication may be prescribed. Lifestyle advice includes standing up slowly and avoiding triggers.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, dizziness on standing is primarily a failure of upward nourishment. Your head needs a constant supply of clear Qi and Blood, and the Spleen is responsible for lifting these upward. When you stand, gravity pulls blood down; if your Qi is too weak to push back quickly, the brain is momentarily starved, and dizziness strikes.
This is why the vast majority of postural dizziness is rooted in deficiency-a shortage of the very resources that keep you steady.
Different organ systems play key roles. The Spleen and Stomach produce Qi and Blood from food, so weakness here leads to Qi and Blood Deficiency or Spleen Qi Deficiency. The Kidneys store Essence, which fills the brain and marrow; when Essence runs low, the brain is undernourished and struggles to stay clear on rising. In each case, the common thread is that the body lacks the vital energy needed to counteract gravity's pull.
Excess patterns can also cause dizziness on standing, but through a different mechanism. Liver Yang Rising sends fiery energy rushing upward, and standing can intensify this surge, making the head feel heavy and throbbing. Phlegm-Dampness, a turbid byproduct of weak digestion, sits in the middle and blocks clear Yang from ascending-like fog preventing sunlight from reaching the mountain peak. So the same symptom can stem from too little nourishment or from something obstructing the path.
This is why a Western diagnosis of orthostatic hypotension doesn't tell the whole story. One person may feel dizzy with a pale face, fatigue, and a weak pulse; another with a flushed face, irritability, and a wiry pulse. TCM identifies which pattern is at play and treats the root imbalance-not just the blood pressure number-so that your body can maintain its own equilibrium naturally.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner first asks whether the dizziness strikes mainly when you stand up or change position. Dizziness that appears or worsens with any movement strongly points toward deficiency patterns, where the body lacks the Qi and Blood needed to reach the head quickly. If the room spins only on rising and eases with rest, the root is often a shortage of vital resources rather than an excess problem.
Qi and Blood Deficiency is a very common cause. The practitioner will look for a pale face, fatigue, heart palpitations, and poor sleep. The tongue is typically pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels weak and thready. This pattern explains why even a simple movement can leave you light-headed-there simply is not enough Blood to nourish the brain.
Spleen Qi Deficiency brings a similar postural dizziness, but the focus shifts to digestion. The practitioner asks about appetite, bloating, and loose stools, and checks for a heavy sensation in the limbs. The tongue often appears pale and puffy with teeth marks, and the pulse is weak. Here the Spleen fails to raise clear Yang upward, so the head feels empty upon standing.
Kidney Essence Deficiency also causes dizziness on standing, but the clues are lower back and knee soreness, tinnitus, poor memory, and thinning hair. This pattern is more common with aging or after long illness. The tongue may be pale or red with little coating, and the pulse is deep and thready. The brain’s “marrow sea” is underfilled, making postural changes difficult.
Liver Yang Rising and Phlegm-Dampness are less tied to standing up. Liver Yang dizziness tends to be constant or triggered by stress, with a pounding headache, irritability, and a wiry pulse. Phlegm-Dampness creates a heavy, foggy head, nausea, and a greasy tongue coating. If your dizziness is purely postural, these patterns are less likely, though they can still contribute.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness triggered by standing up, Profound fatigue and weakness, Pale or sallow complexion, Heart palpitations, Poor appetite and digestion
- **Worse with**: Standing up quickly, Overexertion or overwork, Skipping meals, Cold or raw foods, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Lying down to rest, Warm, nourishing meals, Adequate sleep, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi provides the upward force that lifts clear Yang and Blood to the head. When both Qi and Blood are deficient, that lifting power is weak. Standing up suddenly pulls blood downward with gravity, and the depleted Qi cannot push enough Blood back up to the brain quickly, causing a momentary dizziness or lightheadedness. This is especially noticeable after prolonged sitting or lying down.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially in the right Guan (Spleen-Stomach) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Standing up quickly demands a rapid upward push of Qi and Blood to the brain; in deficiency, this fails, leading to dizziness. Rest and lying down remove the demand, allowing the scant Qi and Blood to circulate without strain. Warm nourishing foods support the Spleen to produce Qi and Blood, while cold raw foods weaken the Spleen and deplete Qi further. Stress and worry consume Spleen Qi, worsening the deficiency.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digestible, nourishing foods such as bone broths, congees, stews, and iron-rich vegetables like spinach. Avoid cold, raw foods and excessive dairy which weaken the Spleen. Gentle daily movement like walking or Tai Chi helps Qi flow without depleting it. Prioritize adequate sleep and avoid overwork.

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness triggered by standing or exertion, Poor appetite and bloating after meals, Fatigue and heaviness in the limbs, Loose stools, Pale, puffy tongue with tooth marks
- **Worse with**: Standing up quickly, Overexertion or overwork, Heavy or greasy meals, Cold or raw foods, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Lying down to rest, Warm, nourishing meals, Gentle rest after eating, Warm compress on abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Spleen transforms food into Qi and blood, and it also has the job of lifting clear, nourishing energy up to your head. When Spleen Qi is weak, that upward lift fails, so the moment you stand the head is left undernourished and dizziness strikes.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with tooth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: deficient and soft, especially weak at the right Guan (Spleen/Stomach) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Standing up demands that your Spleen's clear Yang rise to the head; when Qi is deficient, that upward push fails, causing dizziness. Lying down removes the demand and allows Qi to circulate more easily. Rest conserves Qi, while overexertion and heavy lifting further deplete it. Warm, cooked foods are gentle on the Spleen and easy to transform into Qi, whereas raw and cold foods dampen the digestive fire, making the weakness worse. Worry knots the Qi and specifically injures the Spleen, so a calm mind is protective.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees that are easy to digest. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Eat small, regular meals and chew thoroughly. Gentle daily movement like walking can help Qi flow, but avoid overexertion. Prioritize rest and manage worry, as overthinking further drains Spleen Qi.

### Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness that strikes especially when standing up, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Poor memory and difficulty concentrating, Tinnitus or gradual hearing loss, Frequent urination, especially at night
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, Excessive sexual activity, Standing for long periods, Cold or raw foods
- **Better with**: Lying down to rest, Warm, nourishing meals, Gentle movement or walking, Adequate sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys store your deepest reserves of Essence, which produce marrow and fill the brain. When this Essence runs low, the brain is undernourished and struggles to stay clear when you stand, because the body cannot maintain a steady upward supply of nourishment. The dizziness tends to come with a deep, hollow ache in your lower back and knees-the classic sign that the Kidneys themselves are depleted.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with little or no coating; fine cracks may appear centrally or toward the root. Pulse: fine, deep, and weak at both Chi (rear) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Essence is the body's deepest fuel. Overwork, prolonged standing, and excessive sexual activity directly burn through this fuel, making the dizziness worse. Rest, especially deep sleep, and warm, building foods allow the Kidneys to slowly replenish their reserves, so the brain receives enough nourishment to stay clear when you rise.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Build meals around deeply nourishing, warm foods like black sesame, walnuts, goji berries, bone broths, and small amounts of high-quality animal protein. Avoid late nights and overwork, which drain the Kidneys further. Gentle, consistent movement like tai chi or walking supports marrow without exhausting your reserves.

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness feels like upward pressure or throbbing, Head feels heavy, feet feel light and unsteady, Flushed face or sensation of heat rising to the head, Irritability, short temper, or feeling easily angered, Ringing in the ears (tinnitus) or dry mouth, Dizziness is constant or triggered by stress, not only on standing
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or emotional stress, Alcohol, coffee, or spicy food, Overexertion or overwork, Hot, stuffy environments
- **Better with**: Resting in a quiet, dark room, Cool drinks and light, non-spicy meals, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In this pattern, the Liver's Yin is too weak to anchor its Yang, so fiery Yang energy surges upward to the head. This creates a constant, often throbbing dizziness, not just on standing. However, when you stand, the upward momentum of rising Yang can intensify, making your head feel heavy, your feet light, and your balance unsteady. The dizziness is more about a feeling of internal pressure and heat than the faint, empty sensation of blood deficiency.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially at the sides, with a thin dry yellow or scanty coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), often forceful at the left middle position, and may be fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is pronounced.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Liver Yang Rising is fundamentally a pattern of heat and upward rebellion. Stress, anger, and stimulants like caffeine or alcohol fan the flames, causing Yang to surge more violently to the head. Cooling foods and calm environments help drain the excess and protect the depleted Yin. Gentle movement encourages Qi to flow smoothly rather than stagnating and then bursting upward, which is why competitive or intense exercise backfires.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid alcohol, coffee, and spicy, greasy foods that add heat and stir up Liver Yang. Prioritize cooling, yin-nourishing foods like celery, spinach, seaweed, and pear. Regular, gentle exercise such as walking or tai chi helps move Qi without overheating, but avoid intense, competitive activities that fuel frustration. Make winding down before bed a non-negotiable ritual.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness with a heavy, foggy sensation, as if the head is wrapped in cloth, Nausea or queasiness, especially after eating, Feeling of fullness and stuffiness in the chest and upper abdomen, Heavy, sluggish feeling in the body and limbs, Greasy, sticky white coating on the tongue
- **Worse with**: Heavy or greasy meals, Damp, humid weather, Standing for long periods, Overexertion or overwork
- **Better with**: Lying down to rest, Warm, dry weather, Light, easily digested meals, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weakened, it fails to transform fluids, which then accumulate into Phlegm-Dampness. This turbid, heavy substance sits in the Middle Burner and blocks the clear Yang Qi from rising to the head. Upon standing, the body needs a quick upward surge of Qi and blood to the brain, but the Phlegm obstruction prevents this, resulting in a heavy, foggy dizziness that feels like your head is wrapped in a cloth.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 Huá), slow or moderate (緩 Huǎn) or soggy (濡 Rú), especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Dampness is heavy and turbid, obstructing the upward flow of clear Yang. Resting reduces the demand on that flow, while warmth and gentle movement help disperse the Dampness. A light diet starves the Phlegm of its source, whereas heavy, greasy foods feed it. Standing still allows the turbid Phlegm to sink and block, worsening the fog.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid dairy, fried foods, sweets, and cold raw foods, which create more Dampness and Phlegm. Favor warm, cooked meals with spices like ginger and cardamom. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi circulate and clear the fog.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Why do I get dizzy when I stand up, but feel fine lying down?
In TCM, this is a classic sign of deficiency. When you're lying down, the body needs less upward force to supply the head. Standing up suddenly demands a quick surge of Qi and Blood to the brain. If your Qi, Blood, or Kidney Essence is depleted, that surge falls short, and dizziness hits. The good news is that this pattern responds well to tonifying herbs and acupuncture over several weeks.

### Can acupuncture really help with dizziness on standing?
Yes. Acupuncture strengthens the Spleen's ability to lift clear Yang, nourishes Blood, and calms rising Liver Yang when needed. Points like Baihui (DU-20) on the top of the head directly raise Qi, while Zusanli (ST-36) and Sanyinjiao (SP-6) build Qi and Blood. Most patients notice less severe and less frequent dizziness within a few sessions.

### How long does it take for Chinese herbs to work for dizziness?
For deficiency patterns like Qi and Blood Deficiency or Spleen Qi Deficiency, you may feel a subtle improvement in energy and less light-headedness within 2-4 weeks, but full rebuilding often takes 6-12 weeks. Excess patterns like Phlegm-Dampness or Liver Yang Rising may respond faster-sometimes within days to a couple of weeks-as the obstruction clears. Consistency with daily herbs is crucial.

### Can I combine TCM with my blood pressure medication?
Generally, yes. TCM herbs and acupuncture can complement conventional treatment. However, some herbs that tonify Qi and Blood (like Huang Qi and Dang Gui) may interact with anticoagulants or affect blood pressure. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor about all medications and supplements you're taking. Never stop prescribed medications abruptly.

### Are there any foods I should eat or avoid to help with dizziness?
Favor warm, cooked, easily digestible foods that build Qi and Blood: soups, stews, whole grains, lean meats, dark leafy greens, and small amounts of red dates or goji berries. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Limit caffeine and alcohol, which can aggravate Liver Yang or dehydrate. Eating small, regular meals helps maintain stable blood sugar and Qi.

### Is dizziness on standing a sign of something serious?
Most of the time, it reflects a manageable deficiency. But if your dizziness is sudden, severe, accompanied by chest pain, fainting, slurred speech, or occurs after a head injury, seek emergency care immediately. For persistent, mild dizziness, TCM can often address the root cause even when conventional tests are normal-but always rule out serious conditions first.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for orthostatic hypotension: a systematic review**
   *Journal/Publication: Chen Y, et al. Acupuncture for orthostatic hypotension: a systematic review. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2021.*
   This systematic review evaluated several small trials using acupuncture for orthostatic hypotension, including in Parkinson's disease and elderly populations. Results suggested that acupuncture may modestly improve dizziness and stabilize blood pressure upon standing, but the evidence was limited by small sample sizes and lack of blinding.

2. **Clinical observation on Gui Pi Tang for dizziness due to Qi and Blood Deficiency**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, et al. Clinical observation on Gui Pi Tang for dizziness due to Qi and Blood Deficiency. Chin J Integr Med. 2019.*
   In this observational study, patients with chronic dizziness on standing and a TCM diagnosis of Qi and Blood Deficiency received modified Gui Pi Tang. After 4 weeks, dizziness severity scores decreased significantly, and accompanying symptoms such as fatigue and palpitations improved, with no serious adverse events reported.
