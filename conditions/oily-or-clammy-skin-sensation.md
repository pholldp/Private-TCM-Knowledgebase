## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Western medicine views oily skin as overproduction of sebum by sebaceous glands, often linked to hormones, genetics, or skincare products. Clammy skin, on the other hand, is usually associated with excessive sweating or a sensation of cool moisture, which can be triggered by anxiety, autonomic nervous system imbalances, or metabolic conditions. Diagnosis typically relies on patient history and physical exam, and treatment targets the surface symptoms.

### Treatments
Standard treatments for oily skin include topical retinoids, salicylic acid, benzoyl peroxide, and in severe cases oral isotretinoin. For clammy or sweaty skin, antiperspirants, iontophoresis, Botox injections, or medications that reduce sweating may be recommended. Lifestyle adjustments like gentle cleansing and avoiding heavy moisturizers are also common advice.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, oily or clammy skin is almost always a sign of Dampness - a heavy, sticky pathological fluid that builds up when the body's fluid metabolism is impaired. The Spleen and Stomach are the main organs responsible for transforming food and drink into usable energy and moisture. When they become sluggish, often from a diet rich in greasy, sweet, or cold foods, they fail to separate the clear from the turbid. The resulting Dampness can rise to the skin like steam from a boiling pot, leaving it greasy, clammy, and prone to breakouts.
But Dampness rarely acts alone. When it combines with Heat, often from spicy foods, alcohol, or emotional stress, the skin becomes not just oily but also inflamed, red, and itchy - this is Damp-Heat, commonly involving the Stomach, Spleen, or Liver and Gallbladder. If the Spleen is weak and cannot generate enough Qi to push fluids, you get a clammy, cool sensation with fatigue and poor digestion - a pattern of Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. And when Dampness lingers for a long time, it thickens into Phlegm, which can clog the channels and lead to deep, cystic bumps and a heavy, sticky skin texture.
This is why one person's oily skin flares after a heavy meal, another's worsens with stress, and a third's feels clammy all day regardless of what they eat. The same Western symptom can have multiple TCM roots, and treatment must match the specific pattern. Your tongue and pulse give the practitioner a clear map of where the dampness is coming from and whether heat is involved.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner first asks about the skin’s feel - is it greasy, clammy, or both - and what makes it worse. The tongue and pulse offer crucial clues: a yellow, greasy coating points to heat, while a pale, swollen tongue with white fur suggests deficiency. Digestive symptoms, energy levels, and emotional patterns then help localize the dampness to a specific organ system.
When the skin is shiny with red, inflamed acne and the person craves rich or spicy foods, Damp-Heat in the Stomach and Spleen is likely. The tongue is red with a thick yellow greasy coat, and the pulse feels slippery and rapid. Bloating after meals, a heavy body sensation, and a tendency toward constipation or sticky stools confirm that heat and dampness are steaming upward.
If the skin is clammy but the person feels chronically tired, has loose stools, and a poor appetite, Spleen Deficiency with Dampness fits better. The tongue is pale and puffy with teeth marks, coated in a white or slightly greasy fur. The pulse is weak and slippery. The oily feeling is often worst on the face and scalp, but there is little redness or inflammation - the body simply lacks the strength to manage fluids.
Emotional stress and irritability that flare with oily, red, and itchy skin suggest Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat. A bitter taste, dry mouth, and a red tongue with yellow greasy coating are common. The pulse becomes wiry and rapid.
In contrast, when the oily film feels thick and heavy, with cystic acne or nodules, Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner is the root. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, the pulse is slippery, and a sense of chest or head heaviness often accompanies it.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Oily, greasy skin especially on face and scalp, Bitter or sticky taste in the mouth, Bloating and fullness in the upper abdomen after meals, Sticky, incomplete bowel movements, Heavy, foggy sensation in the head
- **Worse with**: Rich, greasy, or fried foods, Spicy or pungent foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overeating or irregular meals
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Drinking plenty of water, Gentle exercise like walking, Cool, dry environment, Eating smaller, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen and Stomach are overloaded by rich, greasy, or spicy foods, they fail to transform and transport fluids properly. This creates a sticky, turbid Dampness that combines with Heat and rises upward like steam. The result is skin that feels oily, clammy, and greasy to the touch, especially on the face and scalp.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, thick yellow greasy coating (especially at centre and root). Pulse: soggy (濡 ru) and rapid (数 shu), often slippery (滑 hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, spicy, and heavy foods directly burden the Spleen and Stomach, generating Damp-Heat that rises to the skin. Light, bland meals and hydration reduce this burden, allowing the Spleen to transform fluids properly. Gentle movement encourages Qi flow and helps drain Dampness, while a cool environment keeps Heat from intensifying.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like steamed vegetables, congee, and mung beans. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and overly sweet foods, as well as dairy and alcohol. Drink plenty of warm water and herbal teas like chrysanthemum. Gentle daily exercise such as walking or stretching helps move Qi and drain Dampness. Keep the skin clean and dry, and avoid hot, humid environments when possible.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Oily or clammy skin on face and scalp, Fatigue that worsens after meals, Loose, unformed stools, Abdominal bloating and heaviness, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Pale, puffy tongue with teeth marks and a greasy white coating
- **Worse with**: Overeating or irregular meals, Rich, greasy, or fried foods, Dairy and cold raw foods, Hot, humid weather, Mental overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Gentle exercise like walking, Light, bland meals, Warm drinks and soups, Rest and adequate sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Spleen is the organ that transforms food and fluids into usable energy and moisture. When the Spleen is weak-often from fatigue, worry, or an irregular diet-it cannot manage fluids properly, and a heavy, sticky dampness builds up inside. This internal dampness rises to the skin, making it feel clammy or greasy, especially on the face and scalp, without the redness or heat of an inflammatory condition.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with scalloped edges (teeth marks), a white greasy coating, and a moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru, 濡) and slow (Huan, 缓), soft and lacking force, possibly slippery (Hua, 滑).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: A weak Spleen cannot manage dampness, and overeating or consuming cold, greasy foods further overwhelms it, directly producing the clammy, oily skin. Warmth, gentle movement, and easily digested meals support the Spleen's ability to transform fluids, while rest prevents the Qi depletion that allows dampness to accumulate.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and congees, and avoid cold, raw, or greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Gentle daily movement such as walking or yoga helps circulate Qi and dry dampness. Do not skip meals or overeat; a regular, moderate eating schedule is key.

### Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-and-gallbladder-damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Irritability and short temper, Bitter taste in the mouth, especially in the morning, Oily skin on face and scalp, often with redness and itching, Pain or distension under the ribs, especially on the right side, Dark yellow urine, Symptoms worsen with emotional upset
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress and frustration, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and caffeine, Hot, humid weather, Suppressed anger
- **Better with**: Calming activities (meditation, yoga), Cooling foods (cucumber, mung beans), Cool showers, Gentle exercise like walking, Avoiding alcohol and spicy foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress and frustration cause Liver Qi to stagnate. Over time, this stagnation generates Heat, which then combines with Dampness that often arises from dietary habits or constitutional weakness. This Damp-Heat rises along the Liver and Gallbladder channels, reaching the face and skin, resulting in an oily, red, and itchy complexion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially red at the sides, with a thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), slippery (滑 huá), and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress stagnates Liver Qi, which generates Heat that combines with Dampness, causing the oily, red skin. Greasy and spicy foods directly add Dampness and Heat, while alcohol and hot weather exacerbate these pathogens. Calming activities help smooth Liver Qi flow, preventing stagnation and Heat buildup, and cooling, damp-draining foods help clear the existing Damp-Heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on cooling, damp-draining foods like cucumber, celery, mung beans, and bitter greens. Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods, as well as alcohol, which all fuel Damp-Heat. Prioritize stress management through gentle movement, meditation, or spending time in nature to keep Liver Qi flowing smoothly.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Skin feels coated with a thick, greasy film, not just shiny, Heavy, sluggish body and limbs, Bloating and fullness after eating, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, no thirst, Thick white greasy tongue coating, Cystic acne or nodules under the skin
- **Worse with**: Rich, greasy, or fried foods, Dairy and sugar, Cold, raw foods, Hot, humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Light, warm cooked meals, Warm, dry environment, Gentle exercise like walking, Ginger tea, Dry skin brushing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and Dampness lingers for a long time, it thickens into a sticky, turbid substance called Phlegm. This Phlegm-Dampness clogs the Middle Burner - the body's digestive and fluid-processing center. Because the fluids cannot be properly transformed and eliminated, they rise to the skin's surface as a heavy, greasy film that feels clammy and coated, not just shiny. Over time, the Phlegm can even clump under the skin, forming cystic acne or nodules.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 Huá), often slow or moderate, and soggy (濡 Rú) under pressure, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Dampness forms when a weak Spleen fails to process fluids, especially after heavy or cold foods. Greasy and dairy products directly add to the Dampness burden, while cold foods impair the Spleen's digestive fire. Damp weather adds external moisture. Warmth, light exercise, and ginger tea activate the Spleen and help the body transform and eliminate the sticky Phlegm-Dampness, clearing the skin from within.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and heavy foods, as well as dairy, sugar, and cold raw foods, which create more Dampness and Phlegm. Focus on warm, cooked meals with plenty of lightly steamed vegetables and whole grains. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi circulate and dry out internal dampness.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Why does my skin get oilier after eating greasy food?
In TCM, greasy and fried foods directly overload the Spleen and Stomach, creating Dampness and Heat that rise to the skin. It's a direct cause-and-effect relationship. Cutting back on these foods is one of the fastest ways to see improvement, often within days.

### Can acupuncture really reduce oily skin?
Yes, acupuncture helps by strengthening the Spleen, clearing Heat, and promoting proper fluid metabolism. It doesn't just dry out the skin; it rebalances the internal environment so oil production normalizes over time. Many people notice their skin feels less greasy and more calm after 3-4 weekly sessions.

### Will I need to take herbs forever?
No. Herbal formulas are typically used for a few weeks to a few months to correct the underlying imbalance. Once the pattern shifts and your body can manage fluids on its own, you can stop or taper off. Many people maintain results with diet alone afterward, though some choose to use herbs seasonally for a tune-up.

### Is it safe to combine TCM with my dermatologist's treatments?
Generally yes, but always inform both practitioners. Some herbs may interact with medications like isotretinoin, so coordination is important. Topical treatments like retinoids or salicylic acid are usually fine to continue alongside acupuncture and herbs.

### What should I eat if my skin is oily and clammy?
Focus on bland, lightly cooked foods like steamed vegetables, congee, barley, and mung beans. Avoid dairy, sugar, alcohol, and deep-fried items. Warm ginger tea can help the Spleen's digestive fire. A light, warm diet is one of the most powerful tools for reducing dampness.

### How long before I see a difference?
Many people notice less oiliness and a calmer complexion within 2-4 weeks of starting herbs and acupuncture. The full benefit, especially for deeper deficiency patterns, may take 6-8 weeks of consistent treatment. Your practitioner will track your tongue and pulse to gauge progress even before you see dramatic skin changes.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for acne vulgaris: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Cao H, Yang G, Wang Y, et al. Acupuncture for acne vulgaris: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2016.*
   This meta-analysis of 43 RCTs found that acupuncture significantly reduced acne lesion counts compared to no treatment or placebo, and was comparable to standard topical treatments. Several studies also noted a reduction in skin oiliness, though this was a secondary outcome.

2. **Efficacy of a Chinese herbal formula on sebum secretion and acne severity: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, Zhang Y, et al. Efficacy of a Chinese herbal formula on sebum secretion and acne severity: a randomized controlled trial. J Tradit Chin Med. 2018.*
   In this 12-week trial of 120 participants with moderate acne, the herbal group showed a significant decrease in sebumeter readings and acne lesion counts compared to placebo. The formula targeted Damp-Heat in the Stomach and Spleen, suggesting that clearing internal damp-heat reduces skin oiliness.
