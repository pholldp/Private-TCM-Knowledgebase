## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Conventional medicine defines frequent infections as an above-average number of colds, flu, sinusitis, or other respiratory illnesses, often without an identifiable immune deficiency. When standard blood tests come back normal and the person does not have chronic fatigue, doctors may attribute the susceptibility to stress, poor nutrition, or exposure. Treatment typically focuses on managing each acute episode rather than preventing the next one.

### Treatments
Management includes rest, hydration, over-the-counter cold and flu remedies, and sometimes prophylactic measures like annual flu vaccines. For recurrent sinus or ear infections, a doctor might prescribe a prolonged course of antibiotics or recommend surgery in rare cases. However, no standard medication is approved specifically to reduce the overall frequency of common infections in otherwise healthy adults.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, your body’s outermost layer is guarded by a special type of Qi called Wei Qi (卫气, defensive Qi). Think of it as a protective shield that circulates just beneath the skin, controlling your pores and fending off external pathogens like wind, cold, and germs. When Wei Qi is strong, you can be exposed to a sick colleague or a chilly draft without getting ill.
When it is weak or out of balance, that shield becomes porous - so you sweat easily, feel chilled by the slightest breeze, and catch every bug that goes around the office.
The interesting thing about Wei Qi is that it can be deficient even when your overall energy feels fine. That is because Wei Qi is produced by the Spleen and distributed by the Lungs, and a problem in that production line does not always drain your general vitality. You might feel energetic enough to work and exercise, yet still come down with a cold every few weeks. This surface-level weakness is exactly what TCM looks for when your immune system seems to be underperforming despite adequate daily energy.
Four distinct patterns can lead to this state. The most common is straightforward Protective Qi Deficiency, where the defensive guard is simply understaffed. Yang Deficiency adds a cold component - the body’s warming fire is too low to circulate Wei Qi effectively, so you feel chilly and your hands and feet are always cold.
Ying-Wei Disharmony is a temporary desynchronization between the outer protective layer and the inner nourishing layer, often triggered by exposure to wind, causing spontaneous sweating and muscle aches. Finally, Damp-Heat invading the Spleen gums up the organ that produces Wei Qi, so the shield cannot be properly supplied, leaving you sticky-sweating and heavy-limbed while still catching frequent infections.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner starts by listening for the quality of the sweat and the feeling of wind. In Protective Qi Deficiency, the core picture is spontaneous sweating that worsens with the slightest movement, a strong aversion to drafts, and a history of catching colds easily. The tongue is pale with a thin coat, and the pulse feels floating and weak, confirming that the defensive layer is too porous.
When Yang Deficiency is the driver, coldness becomes the standout clue. The person feels chilled even in warm rooms, has cold hands and feet, and may look pale or puffy. The tongue is pale and wet, and the pulse is deep and slow. This tells the practitioner that the body’s warming fire is too low to hold the exterior secure.
Ying-Wei Disharmony often shows up after exposure to wind. Sweating comes with a distinct ache in the muscles and a low-grade feverish feeling, yet the person still dreads drafts. The pulse is floating and relaxed, not forceful. The practitioner will ask about recent weather or travel, because this pattern mimics a mild wind invasion that the body cannot properly resolve.
Damp-Heat invading the Spleen is rare in this picture but has its own unmistakable signs. The sweat feels sticky, the mouth tastes bitter, and the urine is dark yellow. The tongue may have a greasy yellow coat, and the pulse is slippery and fast. Here the blockage of Wei Qi comes from internal humidity and heat, not from simple weakness, so the approach shifts to clearing rather than tonifying.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Protective Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/protective-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Spontaneous sweating that worsens with movement, Frequent colds or infections, Aversion to wind and drafts, Bright pale complexion, Mild fatigue and low stamina, Slow recovery from illness
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Exposure to wind and cold, Raw and cold foods, Stress and worry, Lack of sleep
- **Better with**: Warmth and avoiding drafts, Rest after activity, Warm, easily digested foods, Gentle movement like Tai Chi, Adequate sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your body's defensive layer, called Wei Qi, is like a security guard that patrols the surface, closing your pores and fending off germs. When that guard is understaffed, your pores fail to seal properly, so you sweat for no reason and feel chilled by the slightest breeze. Pathogens that would normally be turned away walk right in, which is why you catch every cold going around-even when your general energy levels feel okay.
This pattern often starts with weak Lung and Spleen Qi, the two systems that generate Wei Qi. Without enough raw material, the protective shield stays thin, leaving you vulnerable to frequent infections and a slow, stubborn recovery.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, slightly puffy body, often with tooth marks along the edges; thin white coat. Pulse: floating (浮 fú) and empty (虚 xū), soft and hollow under pressure.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overexertion and lack of sleep drain Qi, leaving the exterior even more vulnerable. Raw and cold foods damage Spleen Yang, the very engine that produces Wei Qi, while stress knots up the Qi flow needed to patrol the surface. Warmth, rest, and gentle movement conserve and build Qi, and avoiding drafts prevents pathogens from slipping through a weakened barrier.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews, and add a little ginger or astragalus root to your cooking. Avoid raw salads, iced drinks, and dairy, which can chill the Spleen and weaken Qi production. Gentle daily movement such as walking or Tai Chi builds Qi without draining it. Protect your neck and upper back from drafts, and aim for consistent, restful sleep.

### Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cold hands and feet even in warm weather, Aversion to cold and desire for warmth, Frequent clear urination, especially at night, Loose stools or undigested food in stool, Low back soreness or weakness, Spontaneous cold sweat after light activity
- **Worse with**: Exposure to cold weather, Eating raw or chilled foods, Overwork and exhaustion, Damp living conditions
- **Better with**: Warm drinks and soups, Keeping the body warmly dressed, Rest and adequate sleep, Gentle warming exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Yang is the body's warming fire. When it is deficient, the defensive Qi (Wei Qi) lacks the heat needed to circulate strongly at the surface. Even if daily energy seems okay, the immune barrier is left cold and unguarded, so external pathogens easily invade, causing frequent colds or infections. The person feels constantly chilled, especially in the hands and feet, and may break into a cold sweat after any exertion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, and moist with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yang deficiency is essentially a lack of the body's warming fire. Cold environments and raw foods directly drain that fire, making the exterior even more vulnerable. Overwork further depletes Yang, leaving the defensive barrier weak. Warmth, rest, and warm nourishment conserve and gently rebuild the Yang, allowing the defensive Qi to regain its strength and protect against illness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews. Add warming spices such as ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper. Avoid raw, cold, and frozen foods that chill the interior. Keep the lower back and abdomen warm, and consider using a hot water bottle or moxibustion at home. Gentle exercise like walking or qigong generates internal warmth without depleting Yang.

### Ying-Wei Disharmony
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/ying-wei-disharmony)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Spontaneous sweating that feels chilly, Alternating chills and low-grade fever, Aversion to wind and drafts, Generalized body aches, Frequent colds despite adequate energy, Slight thirst without desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Exposure to wind and drafts, Cold or raw foods, Overexertion, Emotional stress, Sudden temperature changes
- **Better with**: Warmth and covering up, Rest and avoiding drafts, Warm ginger tea or congee, Gentle exercise to promote Qi flow
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your body’s protective shield (Wei Qi) and its nourishing inner layer (Ying Qi) normally work as a team. When they fall out of sync, the outer defenses can’t properly seal the pores, so sweat leaks out easily and wind-cold slips in. This leaves you feeling chilled, achy, and prone to catching colds over and over - even when your overall energy isn’t severely low.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-red or slightly pale body with a thin, white, moist coating. Pulse: floating and moderate (浮缓 fú huǎn), sometimes described as floating and weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-cold easily disrupts the already fragile balance between Ying and Wei, making sweating and chills worse. Warmth, rest, and warm fluids help the Qi settle and re-harmonize, while light sweating can help guide the defensive Qi back to the surface where it belongs.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep your neck and back covered to block drafts, and dress in layers you can adjust as your temperature shifts. Eat warm, easy-to-digest foods like congee with a little ginger and spring onion; avoid raw salads, iced drinks, and dairy until the pattern settles. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow without overexerting you.

### Damp-Heat invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Stuffiness and fullness in the upper abdomen, Heavy sensation in body and limbs, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, Dark yellow urine, Loose, sticky stools that feel incomplete
- **Worse with**: Humid or damp weather, Greasy, sweet, or heavy foods, Alcohol, Overeating, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Dry, well-ventilated environment, Gentle exercise, Bitter greens and barley water
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Dampness and Heat clog the Spleen, the organ's ability to transform food into clear Qi is impaired. The Spleen is the source of defensive Wei Qi, so when it's bogged down, Wei Qi cannot reach the skin and muscles to guard against pathogens. This leaves the body vulnerable to frequent colds and infections, even though you may feel heavy rather than weak. The sticky sweat, bitter taste, and yellow urine are tell-tale signs that damp-heat is the root cause.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating thickest at centre and root. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), or slippery (Hua) and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness and Heat accumulate when the Spleen is overwhelmed by heavy, greasy, or sweet foods and by humid environments. Light, bland foods and dry surroundings reduce the burden on the Spleen, allowing it to transform Dampness. Gentle exercise moves Qi, which helps the body expel Dampness and clear Heat. Avoiding alcohol and overeating prevents further clogging of the middle burner, so Wei Qi can be distributed to the exterior again.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible meals: favour barley, adzuki beans, mung beans, bitter greens, and lightly cooked vegetables. Avoid dairy, sugar, alcohol, greasy or deep-fried foods, and cold raw foods which create more Dampness. Stay in a dry environment and use a dehumidifier if necessary. Gentle exercise like walking or yoga helps move Qi and transform Dampness, but avoid overexertion in humid weather.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Why do I keep getting sick even though I have plenty of energy?
In TCM, your defensive Wei Qi can be weak or out of balance even when your overall vitality feels fine. This surface-level deficiency means your body’s shield against pathogens is porous, so you catch frequent colds or infections without feeling exhausted. The root is often in the Spleen and Lungs, which produce and distribute Wei Qi, and the specific pattern determines whether the shield needs building up, warming, harmonizing, or clearing.

### How long does it take to see results with acupuncture and herbs?
Many people notice fewer colds and less sensitivity to wind within 4 to 6 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs. Some patterns respond faster - Ying-Wei Disharmony may improve in just 2 to 3 weeks. Deeper deficiencies like Yang Deficiency can take 2 to 3 months to rebuild. The first sign of progress is often that you stop feeling chilled by every draft, then that infections become milder and shorter.

### Will I need to take herbs forever?
Typically no. Herbal formulas are used to correct the underlying pattern, and once your Wei Qi is strong and stable, you can stop taking them. Some people with a constitutional tendency to deficiency may benefit from a maintenance formula during cold and flu season, but this is not a lifelong prescription. Your practitioner will guide you on when to taper off.

### Can I combine TCM with vaccines or conventional medications?
Yes. Acupuncture and herbal formulas do not interfere with vaccines or common medications like antibiotics or antivirals. Always tell your TCM practitioner about any drugs you are taking, and let your doctor know you are using TCM. This coordinated approach ensures your safety and the best possible outcome.

### What foods should I eat to strengthen my immune system?
Warm, cooked foods are the foundation - soups, stews, congee, and steamed vegetables support the Spleen in making Wei Qi. Ginger tea, cinnamon, and a little scallion can warm the surface and help keep pores closed. Avoid cold, raw, and icy foods, as well as excessive dairy, sugar, and greasy meals, which can create dampness and weaken your defenses.

### Can children be treated for frequent colds with TCM?
Yes, TCM is very effective for children who seem to catch every bug at daycare or school. Pediatric acupuncture often uses non-needle techniques like acupressure or low-level laser, and herbal formulas are given in child-friendly doses. The patterns are similar - often Protective Qi Deficiency or Ying-Wei Disharmony - and treatment can significantly reduce the number of sick days over a season.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Yu Ping Feng San for recurrent respiratory tract infections in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang L, et al. Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2018.*
   A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials found that Yu Ping Feng San significantly reduced the number of respiratory tract infections per year and shortened the duration of each episode compared to placebo or no treatment. The effect was most pronounced in children with a history of frequent colds.

2. **Immunomodulatory effects of Yu Ping Feng San: regulation of cytokine release in macrophages** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0078636))
   *Journal/Publication: Du CY, et al. PLOS ONE. 2013.*
   This preclinical study demonstrated that Yu Ping Feng San modulates the release of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in murine macrophages, providing a mechanistic basis for its traditional use in strengthening defensive Qi and preventing infections.

3. **Clinical observation of Yu Ping Feng San combined with conventional therapy for allergic rhinitis**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang Y, et al. Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine. 2016.*
   In a randomised controlled trial, patients with perennial allergic rhinitis who received Yu Ping Feng San in addition to standard antihistamines had significantly lower symptom scores and fewer recurrences over a six-month follow-up compared to antihistamines alone.
