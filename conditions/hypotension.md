## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Hypotension is generally defined as a blood pressure reading below 90/60 mmHg. Many people live with low numbers and feel fine, but for others it causes dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting, fatigue, blurred vision, and difficulty concentrating. It is diagnosed with a simple blood pressure cuff, sometimes with tilt-table tests if postural drops are suspected.
Common triggers include dehydration, prolonged bed rest, pregnancy, heart problems, endocrine disorders, and certain medications. When no underlying disease is found, it is called chronic idiopathic hypotension. Treatment often focuses on managing symptoms and avoiding triggers.

### Treatments
Standard advice includes increasing fluid and salt intake, wearing compression stockings, eating smaller meals, and standing up slowly. When symptoms are disabling, doctors may prescribe medications like fludrocortisone to boost blood volume or midodrine to tighten blood vessels. The goal is to raise pressure enough to prevent fainting, not to hit a specific number.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands blood pressure as the visible result of two invisible forces: Qi, the vital force that propels blood through the vessels, and Blood, the substance that fills and nourishes them. When Qi is weak, the heart lacks the push to maintain a steady pressure. When Blood is insufficient, the vessels are underfilled, like a hose with low water volume. Either way, the brain and limbs don't get the nourishment they need, and dizziness, fatigue, and coldness follow.
The Spleen plays a starring role because it transforms food into Qi and Blood and lifts clear Yang upward to the head. If the Spleen is weak, not only is production low, but the upward lift fails - explaining why dizziness often worsens on standing.
The Kidney stores Essence, the body's deepest reserve, and when it is depleted through aging, overwork, or constitution, the foundation crumbles and chronic low pressure sets in. The Heart's Yang is the pilot light; if it dims, the whole circulation runs cold and sluggish.
Phlegm-Dampness adds a twist: here the problem is not a lack of Qi but a blockage. When digestion is sluggish and fluids congeal, thick turbidity can clog the middle burner and prevent clear Yang from ascending, causing a heavy, foggy dizziness. This is why one person's low blood pressure feels like an empty, floaty lightheadedness while another's feels like a wet blanket on the head - they are different patterns with different treatments.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When a person complains of low blood pressure and dizziness, a TCM practitioner first looks at the overall energy and complexion. If the face is pale, the lips and nails lack color, and the person feels tired and breathless, this points to a Qi and Blood Deficiency pattern. The tongue is pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels thin and weak.
If the dizziness is clearly worse when standing up, and the person feels a dragging sensation in the abdomen or a constant fatigue that improves with lying down, the practitioner suspects Spleen Qi Sinking. The tongue is often pale and swollen with teeth marks, and the pulse is deep and weak, indicating the clear Yang is not being lifted.
In older adults or those with chronic low blood pressure, if there is also tinnitus, sore lower back and knees, and frequent nighttime urination, the problem is likely Kidney Essence Deficiency. The tongue may be red with little coating or pale, and the pulse is deep and thin. This pattern reflects a deeper depletion of the body’s foundational reserves.
When low blood pressure comes with a feeling of cold, chest tightness, and palpitations, the practitioner looks for Heart Yang Deficiency. The person often has cold hands and feet, and the tongue may be pale with a bluish tinge. The pulse is slow and deep, showing the heart’s pumping action is weakened by a lack of warming Yang.
If the dizziness feels heavy, like a fog or a weight on the head, and is accompanied by chest stuffiness, nausea, and a greasy tongue coating, the practitioner considers Phlegm-Dampness obstructing the middle burner. This pattern is less about emptiness and more about a sluggish, damp metabolism clogging the upward flow of clarity.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fatigue worsened by exertion, Pale or sallow complexion, pale lips and nail beds, Dizziness that worsens with exertion, not only on standing, Heart palpitations and shortness of breath, Poor appetite and reduced food intake
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and prolonged standing, Skipping meals or eating cold foods, Standing up quickly, Excessive worry and mental strain, Cold environments
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle movement or walking, Sitting or lying down when dizzy, Warmth
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi and Blood are the body's two most vital substances-Qi provides the force that moves blood through the vessels, and Blood fills those vessels to maintain pressure. When both are deficient, the heart lacks the power to pump strongly and the vessels lack the volume to sustain pressure, leading to low blood pressure. The brain and senses are not properly nourished, which causes dizziness, fatigue, and a pale complexion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially at the right Guan position (Spleen/Stomach).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Qi and Blood are the body's fuel and building blocks. Rest and warm, nourishing food replenish them, allowing the heart to pump with more force and the vessels to fill. Gentle movement circulates Qi without draining it, while sitting down when dizzy prevents a drop in blood flow to the brain.</p><p>Overexertion, skipping meals, and cold foods drain Qi and weaken the Spleen's ability to produce Blood, worsening the deficiency. Standing up quickly demands a sudden surge of Qi to the head, which a deficient body cannot supply, causing dizziness. Excessive worry knots the Spleen Qi and further impairs blood production, while cold environments constrict and slow the already weak flow.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Build blood pressure gently with warm, nutrient-dense foods like bone broth, red dates, goji berries, dark leafy greens, and small amounts of high-quality red meat. Avoid raw, cold, and iced foods that weaken the Spleen. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi boosts Qi without depleting it, but rest is equally important-aim for consistent sleep and sit down at the first sign of dizziness. Rise slowly from lying or sitting to give your body time to adjust.

### Spleen Qi Sinking
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-sinking)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness on standing, Bearing-down sensation in abdomen, Chronic loose stools or diarrhea, Profound fatigue with weak limbs, Reluctance to speak, Organ prolapse
- **Worse with**: Standing up quickly, Skipping meals, Cold raw foods and drinks, Overwork, Damp or humid weather
- **Better with**: Lying down, Warmth, Small frequent meals, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen’s job is to raise clear Qi upward to nourish the head and keep organs in place. When Spleen Qi sinks, that upward lift fails-clear Yang can’t reach the brain, causing dizziness that worsens on standing, and the heart lacks the Qi support to maintain a strong pulse, leading to low blood pressure.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: weak and forceless, especially at the right Guan position, may feel deep.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Standing or rising quickly demands that Spleen Qi lift clear Yang to the head; when Qi is already sinking, this demand cannot be met, causing dizziness. Lying down removes that demand, allowing Qi to return. Warmth and cooked, easy-to-digest foods nourish the Spleen and support Qi production, while cold raw foods further weaken the Spleen. Overwork drains Qi, making the sinking worse. Gentle walking stimulates Qi flow without overtaxing the body.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like congee with red dates (jujube), yam, and a little ginger. Avoid cold drinks and raw salads. Stand up slowly from lying or sitting to prevent dizziness. Gentle daily walking helps lift Qi.

### Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chronic dizziness (not just on standing), Soreness and weakness of lower back and knees, Tinnitus or hearing loss, Poor memory and concentration, Frequent urination at night
- **Worse with**: Overwork, Prolonged standing, Sexual excess, Cold environments
- **Better with**: Gentle movement or walking, Warm, nourishing foods, Rest and early nights
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Kidney Essence is the body's deepest reserve, responsible for producing marrow and filling the brain. When Essence is depleted-whether from aging, overwork, or constitutional weakness-the brain and blood vessels lose their foundational nourishment. This leads to chronic dizziness, a hollow sensation in the head, and low blood pressure that does not easily recover with rest alone.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with little or no coating, possible fine cracks. Pulse: fine, deep, and weak at both Chi positions; may be slightly rapid if Yin deficiency is dominant.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Essence is the foundation of all Yin and Yang in the body. Overwork, prolonged standing, and sexual excess drain this deep reserve, further depleting the marrow that nourishes the brain and vessels, causing blood pressure to drop. Warm, nourishing foods and adequate rest replenish Essence and allow the body to rebuild its foundational energy. Gentle movement stimulates Qi and blood flow without exhausting the Kidneys, helping to gradually raise and stabilize blood pressure.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, nourishing foods like black sesame, walnuts, goji berries, bone broths, and small amounts of lamb. Avoid raw, cold foods and drinks. Prioritize deep rest and gentle, consistent movement like Tai Chi or walking rather than intense workouts. Protect the lower back from cold drafts.

### Heart Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cold hands and feet, Chest stuffiness or mild chest pain, Palpitations worsened by effort, Bright pale face, Listlessness and desire to sleep
- **Worse with**: Cold environments, Overexertion or heavy exercise, Cold raw foods and drinks, Prolonged standing, Emotional stress and fear
- **Better with**: Warmth, Gentle movement or walking, Warm drinks and soups, Rest and lying down, Moxibustion on lower abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Heart's Yang is the body's internal fire that pumps blood and keeps you warm. When it becomes weak, the heart lacks the strength to push blood through the vessels, so blood pressure drops. Without enough warm blood reaching the head and limbs, dizziness, cold hands and feet, and a heavy tiredness set in. The chest feels stuffy or tight because the heart muscle itself isn't getting enough warming energy.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender with teeth marks, white slippery coating. Pulse: deep and weak, especially at the left cun (Heart) position; may be knotted (slow with irregular pauses).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yang is like a small fire that drives circulation. Cold weather, raw foods, and overwork douse that fire, making the heart pump even weaker and blood pressure drop further. Warmth, warm meals, and rest protect and stoke the fire, so the heart can push blood more forcefully. Gentle movement stirs Yang without depleting it. Moxibustion adds direct heat to the body's core, rekindling the Heart Yang. Stress and fear consume Heart Qi, which further weakens Yang, so calming the mind is essential.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and sip warm drinks throughout the day. Add gentle warming spices like ginger, cinnamon, and a pinch of cloves to meals. Avoid raw, cold foods and iced drinks that douse the body's fire. Gentle daily walking helps stir Yang without exhausting it. Keep the chest and feet warm, and consider moxibustion at home on the lower abdomen (Guanyuan REN-4) or below the navel.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, foggy-headed dizziness, Abdominal fullness after eating, Greasy, sticky mouth, Loose, sticky stools, Excessive drowsiness and body heaviness
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or rich foods, Damp or humid weather, Overeating or large meals, Cold raw foods and drinks, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Light, easily digested meals, Ginger tea or mild spices, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids, and dampness congeals into phlegm that bogs down the middle burner. This turbid phlegm-dampness blocks the normal ascent of clear Yang to the head, leaving you with a heavy, foggy dizziness and a sensation as if your head is wrapped in cloth. At the same time, the obstruction and underlying Spleen weakness slow the generation of Qi and blood, which can cause the blood pressure to drop.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating. Pulse: Slippery (滑 Huá) and soggy (濡 Rú), especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is a heavy, sticky pathogen that accumulates when the Spleen is overloaded by rich, cold, or damp-producing foods and a lack of movement. Greasy and raw foods directly add to the dampness, while cold and humidity congeal fluids into phlegm. Warm, dry conditions and spices like ginger help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, preventing stagnation. Light meals and gentle movement stimulate the Spleen and Stomach, encouraging the ascent of clear Yang to the head and relieving the heavy dizziness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid heavy, greasy, and cold raw foods that create more dampness and phlegm. Favor warm, cooked meals with gentle spices like ginger and cardamom. Include barley, adzuki beans, and lightly cooked vegetables to help drain dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi keeps Qi flowing and prevents stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really raise my blood pressure?
Yes, acupuncture can help regulate blood pressure by strengthening the organ systems that maintain it. Points like Baihui (DU-20), Zusanli (ST-36), and Qihai (REN-6) are commonly used to tonify Qi and lift clear Yang. Many patients notice they feel less dizzy and more grounded after a few sessions, even before the numbers change.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs alongside my blood pressure medication?
In most cases, yes, but you must tell both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about everything you are taking. Some herbs, like licorice root (Gan Cao), can naturally raise blood pressure, so if you are already on a medication like fludrocortisone, your practitioner will adjust the formula. Never stop or change your medication without medical supervision.

### Why do I get dizzy when I stand up?
In TCM, this is often a sign of Spleen Qi Sinking - the Spleen's normal job is to lift clear Yang to the head, and when that lift is weak, standing up briefly robs the brain of nourishment. Other patterns like Qi and Blood Deficiency can also contribute. A practitioner will check your tongue and pulse to pinpoint the exact cause.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet makes a big difference. Generally, warm, nourishing foods like soups, stews, root vegetables, and small frequent meals help support Spleen Qi and Blood production. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks which can weaken digestion. Adding gentle spices like ginger and cinnamon can also give your circulation a small boost.

### Will the results last, or will my pressure drop again when I stop treatment?
When the underlying pattern is fully corrected, the results tend to hold. For milder deficiencies, a course of treatment may be enough. For deeper constitutional weakness, periodic tune-ups or long-term gentle herbs might be recommended, much like maintaining any chronic condition. Your practitioner will give you a realistic plan.

### What if I faint? Is that an emergency?
A single brief fainting spell without injury is not necessarily an emergency, but it should be evaluated. If you faint repeatedly, feel chest pain, have a severe headache, or lose consciousness for more than a minute, seek urgent medical care. Please see our Safety section for a full list of red flags.
