## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Skin inflammation, or dermatitis, is a broad term for any condition that makes the skin red, swollen, itchy, and sometimes blistered or scaly. The most common forms include atopic dermatitis (eczema), contact dermatitis, and seborrheic dermatitis. Diagnosis is usually made by examining the skin and asking about triggers, though patch testing or skin biopsies may be used for stubborn cases. While the exact cause varies, Western medicine views dermatitis as a combination of an overactive immune response, a weakened skin barrier, and environmental irritants or allergens.

### Treatments
Conventional care typically starts with emollients to repair the skin barrier and topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors to calm inflammation. Antihistamines help with itching, especially at night. For moderate to severe cases, phototherapy or systemic medications like oral steroids, methotrexate, cyclosporine, or newer biologics (dupilumab) may be prescribed. The goal is to control flares and maintain remission, but treatment is often long-term and focused on suppressing symptoms rather than addressing root causes.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands skin inflammation as a conversation between your internal organs and the outside world. The Lungs govern the skin and its protective barrier, the Spleen manages the fluids that keep it moist and healthy, the Liver stores and moves the blood that nourishes it, and the Heart houses the spirit and blood that influence heat and redness. When any of these organ systems falls out of balance - from emotional stress, poor diet, overwork, or external climate factors - the skin becomes the place where that imbalance shows up. A sudden, angry-red rash after a stressful week points to the Liver and Heart; a chronic, weepy rash that flares with rich food points to the Spleen.
The core mechanisms are Wind, Dampness, Heat, and Dryness - pathogenic factors that can invade from outside or brew internally. Wind causes itching that moves around and comes on suddenly; Dampness makes the skin weep, ooze, and feel heavy; Heat creates redness, burning, and rapid onset; Dryness leads to scaling, cracking, and a tight sensation. Often these combine, so a Damp-Heat rash is moist, hot, and intensely itchy, while a Wind-Dryness rash is flaky and itchy but without much redness. The tongue and pulse give the final clues: a red tongue with yellow greasy coating confirms Damp-Heat; a pale, thin tongue points to Blood Deficiency; a red tongue with little coating suggests Yin Deficiency.
This is why the same Western diagnosis of dermatitis can have so many different TCM roots. A young adult with acute contact dermatitis - bright red, hot, and weeping - is likely in a Heat in the Blood or Damp-Heat pattern. A middle-aged person with chronic atopic dermatitis that’s dry, thickened, and unbearably itchy at night is more likely in a Blood Deficiency with External Wind pattern. A person with recurrent hand eczema plus bloating and loose stools fits a Spleen Deficiency with Dampness picture. By matching the pattern rather than the label, TCM aims to not just clear the current flare but to correct the underlying susceptibility so the skin stays calm longer.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner listens for the story of the rash - when it started, what it looks like, and what makes it better or worse. The tongue and pulse provide the final clues. These details sort the inflammation into one of several patterns, each reflecting a different internal imbalance that needs its own approach.
If the rash erupts suddenly with bright red, hot, and very itchy papules that worsen with heat or stress, Heat in the Blood is likely. The tongue tip is often red with a thin yellow coating, and the pulse feels rapid. This pattern suggests excess heat stirring up wind in the skin, often triggered by spicy food or emotional upset.
When lesions weep, ooze, or form crusts and the skin feels heavy and sticky, Damp-Heat is the usual culprit. A yellow, greasy tongue coating and a slippery, rapid pulse confirm the diagnosis. This pattern is often linked to a rich diet or humid environments that overload the body with dampness and heat, causing the skin to break down.
Chronic dry, scaly, thickened skin that itches intensely, especially at night, points to Blood Deficiency with External Wind. The tongue is pale with a thin coat, and the pulse is thin or wiry. Here, the skin is undernourished because the blood lacks the richness to moisten it, allowing external wind to invade and cause persistent itching.
Recurrent dull, oozing lesions accompanied by bloating, loose stools, and fatigue suggest Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. The tongue is pale, swollen, and often has teeth marks on the edges, while the pulse feels slow or deep. This pattern arises when the digestive system fails to transform fluids, creating internal dampness that seeps out through the skin.
When the skin is chronically red, dry, and flaky with fine scaling, and the person feels hot at night or sweats during sleep, Yin and Blood Deficiency is at play. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. This pattern reflects a deep depletion of cooling, moistening yin and blood, leaving the skin parched and prone to low‑grade inflammation.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Oozing, weeping, crusted skin lesions, often with yellow discharge, Red, swollen, hot skin with a burning sensation, Feeling of heaviness in the body or limbs, as if wrapped in a cloth, Sticky or greasy sensation in the mouth, poor appetite, loose sticky stools
- **Worse with**: Hot, humid weather, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol, Dairy products, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Cool, dry weather, Cooling foods (e.g., cucumber, watermelon, mung beans), Drinking cool water or barley water, Gentle movement or exercise, Keeping the affected area clean and dry
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body's ability to process fluids weakens, dampness accumulates internally. If this dampness combines with heat-often from diet, alcohol, or a hot humid climate-it creates a sticky, turbid condition that rises to the skin. The dampness makes the skin weep, ooze, and crust, while the heat creates redness, swelling, and intense burning or itching.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating that is difficult to scrape off; may be swollen with teeth marks. Pulse: soggy-rapid (Ru Shu) or slippery-rapid (Hua Shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-heat thrives in hot, humid conditions and is directly worsened by rich, greasy, or spicy foods that add more dampness and heat to the body. Alcohol and dairy are particularly clogging and heat-generating. Cooling, dry environments and a bland, light diet help the body drain dampness and clear heat. Gentle movement keeps Qi and fluids circulating, preventing stagnation that turns into damp-heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods, alcohol, and dairy, which create dampness and heat. Eat light, cooling foods such as mung beans, bitter melon, cucumber, and barley. Keep the skin clean and dry, and wear breathable fabrics. Gentle daily movement helps Qi flow and drains dampness.

### Heat in the Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heat-in-the-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bright red, hot skin papules or patches that appear suddenly, Intense itching that worsens with warmth, Feeling of internal heat, worse at night, Thirst with desire for cold drinks, Restlessness and irritability
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol, Hot showers or sauna, Emotional stress, Scratching
- **Better with**: Cool compresses, Drinking cool water or barley water, Cooling foods (e.g., cucumber, watermelon, mung beans), Adequate rest and sleep, Loose, breathable clothing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When pathogenic Heat penetrates deeply into the Blood level, it agitates the blood and stirs up internal Wind. This turbulent Wind-Heat surges upward and outward to the skin, causing sudden red papules or patches that feel hot and itch intensely. The Heat accelerates blood flow and damages tiny vessels, which leads to the bright redness, swelling, and a burning sensation characteristic of acute skin inflammation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with red spots or prickles, thin yellow dry coating. Pulse: rapid (Shu), often wiry (Xian) and possibly slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Heat in the Blood is a pattern of excess heat that agitates the blood and generates Wind. Anything that adds heat to the body-spicy foods, alcohol, hot environments-will intensify the blood heat and worsen skin inflammation. Emotional stress, especially anger and frustration, stirs up Liver fire which can further heat the blood. Cooling measures like cold compresses, cool drinks, and cooling foods directly counter the heat, calming the blood and reducing redness and itching. Rest and relaxation help prevent the emotional stirrings that fan the flames.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat cooling, light foods like cucumber, watermelon, mung bean soup, and leafy greens. Avoid spicy, greasy, or fried foods, alcohol, and excessive meat, which add heat. Keep skin cool with loose cotton clothing and lukewarm showers. Manage stress with gentle activities like walking or meditation, as emotional upset stirs up Liver fire and heats the blood.

### Blood Deficiency with External Wind
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-deficiency-with-external-wind)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry, flaky, or rough skin, Intense itching that worsens at night, Rashes that appear and disappear suddenly, Pale or sallow complexion, Aversion to wind or drafts, Dizziness or light-headedness
- **Worse with**: Wind and drafts, Dry, cold air, Emotional stress, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Nighttime (itching intensifies), Menstrual blood loss
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Nourishing, warm foods, Moisturizing the skin, Avoiding wind and drafts, Gentle movement or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Blood nourishes and moistens the skin. When Blood is deficient, the skin becomes dry, flaky, and poorly protected, creating a weakness that allows external Wind to invade. Wind stirs the skin and causes intense itching that moves around and appears suddenly, often worsening at night when Blood is less active.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with a thin white and slightly dry coating. Pulse: fine (thin), often wiry at the left middle position (Liver) and floating at the right front position (Lungs).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood Deficiency fails to nourish and moisten the skin, creating an opening for external Wind to invade and trigger itching. Wind is aggravated by drafts, dry air, and emotional stress, while nighttime and blood loss further deplete Blood. Nourishing foods and rest build Blood to strengthen the skin barrier, and moisturizing plus avoiding wind help keep external pathogens at bay.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat blood-nourishing foods like dark leafy greens, red meat, eggs, black sesame seeds, and goji berries. Avoid spicy, greasy, and drying foods that consume Blood and generate Wind. Keep skin well-moisturized with gentle, fragrance-free products, and protect yourself from wind and drafts with appropriate clothing. Prioritize restful sleep and manage stress, as both overwork and emotional strain deplete Blood and stir Wind.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, oozing skin lesions, not intensely red, Bloating and loose stools, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Poor appetite and fatigue
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid weather, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Cold, raw foods and drinks, Overeating or irregular meals
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Eating warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement or exercise, Avoiding overeating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into usable energy and moisture. When the Spleen is weak, it fails to manage this process, allowing excess dampness to build up internally. This dampness then spills outwards and saturates the skin, leading to recurrent, oozing, and dull-looking lesions that lack the intense redness of heat-driven inflammations.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks and a white, greasy coating. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), soft and lacking force.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: A weak Spleen cannot process heavy, greasy, or cold foods, which directly generate more internal dampness and worsen oozing skin. Damp environments add external dampness to an already waterlogged system. Warm, cooked meals and gentle movement support the Spleen's transformative function, helping to dry up the excess moisture that feeds the skin lesions.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked, and easily digestible meals like soups, stews, and congee. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Gentle daily movement like walking or yoga helps circulate Qi and dry dampness, while keeping the skin clean and dry prevents external dampness from worsening the lesions.

### Yin and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/yin-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chronic red, dry, finely scaling skin, Night sweats, Warm palms, soles, and chest (five-centre heat), Dry mouth and throat, Red, thin, cracked tongue with little or no coating
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Overwork and late nights, Dry, heated indoor air, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Cool, humid air, Moistening foods (pears, bone broth), Adequate rest and sleep, Gentle movement or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Yin and Blood are the body's cooling, moistening resources. When they run low over time, the skin loses its nourishment and becomes dry, thin, and prone to fine scaling. The internal emptiness also allows a low-grade heat to develop, which rises to the surface and causes lingering redness and inflammation that never quite settles.
This is not the acute, fiery heat of an infection but a slow-burning dryness-heat that flares when the body is stressed or depleted. The skin reflects the deeper deficit: it is red, rough, and scaly, and the person often feels restless, warm at night, and easily fatigued.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale to pale-red, thin, and dry with cracks; coating is scanty or peeled. Pulse: fine (Xi) and rapid (Shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin and Blood are built during rest and with moistening foods. Late nights, overwork, and drying habits deplete them further, allowing the low-grade heat to flare. Stress stirs internal heat, which rises to the skin. Cooling, moistening, and calming routines replenish the deficit and quiet the inflammation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize moistening, cooling foods: pears, black sesame, goji berries, spinach, and bone broths. Avoid spicy, fried, or drying foods, and limit caffeine and alcohol. Go to bed early and protect sleep quality-this is when Yin and Blood are rebuilt. Gentle exercise like walking or qigong is better than intense sweating.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM really help with eczema or dermatitis?
Yes, many people find significant relief. TCM doesn’t just suppress the rash - it works to correct the internal imbalance that allows the skin to flare. While results vary, studies and clinical experience show that herbal formulas and acupuncture can reduce itching, redness, and the frequency of flares, especially for chronic or recurrent cases that haven’t fully responded to conventional creams alone.

### How long before I see results?
It depends on the pattern and how long you’ve had the condition. Acute, hot rashes often calm within days to two weeks. Chronic dry eczema may take 4-8 weeks before you notice a real shift, with continued improvement over months. Your practitioner will adjust your formula as your skin changes, so you’re not stuck with the same approach if progress is slow.

### Will I need to change my diet?
Most likely, yes - but not forever. TCM links skin inflammation to diet because certain foods create Dampness and Heat internally. You’ll typically be asked to reduce spicy, greasy, and sugary foods, as well as dairy and alcohol, at least during the active treatment phase. Your practitioner will give you specific guidance based on your pattern, and as your skin heals, you can often reintroduce foods gradually.

### Can I use my steroid cream while doing TCM?
Absolutely. Most people continue their prescribed creams while starting herbs and acupuncture. The goal is to use the TCM treatment to reduce your need for steroids over time, not to stop them abruptly. Just let both your dermatologist and TCM practitioner know everything you’re using so they can coordinate care safely.

### Are there side effects from Chinese herbs?
When prescribed by a qualified practitioner who matches the formula to your pattern, side effects are uncommon and usually mild - perhaps some digestive adjustment at first. However, certain herbs can interact with medications or be unsuitable during pregnancy, so always give a full health history. We source herbs from reputable suppliers to avoid contaminants.

### Does acupuncture hurt?
Most people feel only a tiny pinch or a dull ache when the needle goes in, and many find the sensation deeply relaxing. The needles used for skin conditions are often placed on the arms, legs, and back, not directly into the rash, so even sensitive skin is not further irritated. Any discomfort fades within seconds.

### Can TCM cure my skin condition permanently?
TCM aims to bring the body back into balance so that the skin can heal and stay clear without constant medication. For many, this means long-term remission rather than a permanent “cure” - your skin may still react if you’re under extreme stress, eat poorly, or encounter strong triggers, but the flares tend to be less frequent and less severe. Ongoing maintenance with occasional herbs or acupuncture can help keep you stable.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for atopic eczema**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang W, Leonard T, Bath-Hextall F, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for atopic eczema. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2015, Issue 2. Art. No.: CD002291.*
   Cochrane systematic review evaluating oral and topical Chinese herbal medicine for atopic eczema. Included 28 RCTs with over 2,300 participants. Some herbal preparations showed improvements in symptom severity and quality of life compared to placebo, but the evidence was rated low to moderate quality due to methodological limitations.

2. **Acupuncture for atopic dermatitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Kim KH, Lee MS, Choi TY, et al. Acupuncture for atopic dermatitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Acupuncture in Medicine 2019;37(1):3-13.*
   Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs involving 1,015 patients. Acupuncture significantly reduced itch intensity (visual analogue scale) and EASI scores compared to sham acupuncture or conventional treatments. No serious adverse events were reported, though most trials had small sample sizes and unclear blinding.

3. **Efficacy and safety of Xiao Feng San in acute dermatitis: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li Y, Chen X, Wang J, et al. Efficacy and safety of Xiao Feng San in acute dermatitis: a randomized controlled trial. Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine 2020;40(2):267-273.*
   RCT of 120 patients with acute dermatitis comparing oral Xiao Feng San plus mometasone cream versus mometasone cream alone. At 4 weeks, the combined group showed significantly greater reduction in itching, erythema, and lesion area. No serious adverse effects were observed; mild gastrointestinal discomfort was reported in 6% of the herbal group.
