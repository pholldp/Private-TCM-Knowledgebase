## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, dizziness is a broad term that encompasses vertigo (a spinning sensation), disequilibrium (loss of balance), presyncope (feeling faint), and lightheadedness. Common causes include inner ear disorders like benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), Ménière's disease, and vestibular neuritis, as well as cardiovascular issues, neurological conditions, medication side effects, and anxiety. Diagnosis typically involves a detailed history, physical examination including positional testing, and sometimes imaging or balance tests to rule out serious underlying pathology.

### Treatments
Treatment depends on the cause. Vestibular suppressants such as meclizine or benzodiazepines may be prescribed for acute vertigo, along with antiemetics for nausea. BPPV is often resolved with canalith repositioning maneuvers like the Epley maneuver. Ménière's disease may be managed with diuretics and dietary salt restriction. For persistent dizziness that doesn't respond to these measures, vestibular rehabilitation therapy is commonly recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees dizziness as a disturbance of the head's clear orifices - the sensory gateways that should be filled with pure, nourishing Qi and Blood. When something blocks this clarity or when the head isn't properly nourished, dizziness results. The Liver, Spleen, Heart, and Kidneys are the organ systems most commonly involved, each influencing the head through different pathways.
The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi and for anchoring the body's Yang energy. When Liver Yin is weak - often from overwork, chronic stress, or aging - the unanchored Yang can surge upward like a flame, bringing heat and wind to the head and causing sudden, intense dizziness with a throbbing headache and irritability. This is the Liver Yang Rising pattern.
The Spleen transforms food and fluids into Qi and Blood. When it's weakened by poor diet or fatigue, fluids accumulate and thicken into phlegm-dampness. This turbid substance can rise and cloud the head, producing a heavy, foggy dizziness - like your head is wrapped in cotton - often accompanied by nausea and a greasy tongue coating. This is the Phlegm-Dampness pattern.
Deficiency patterns - Qi and Blood Deficiency, and Kidney Essence Deficiency - arise when the body's reserves are too low to nourish the brain. Qi and Blood Deficiency causes lightheadedness that worsens with exertion, pale complexion, and heart palpitations. Kidney Essence Deficiency creates a hollow, chronic dizziness with poor memory and a sore back, reflecting the deep depletion of the 'sea of marrow' that fills the brain. A less common pattern, Blood Stagnation, occurs when sluggish circulation blocks the vessels that carry clear Yang to the head, often after a head injury or long-standing emotional stress.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner starts by asking you to describe the dizziness in detail: Is it a spinning sensation, a floating feeling, or a heavy fog? When does it happen - during stress, after eating, when you stand up? These clues point toward which organ system is out of balance.
If dizziness comes with a pounding headache, ringing ears, and a quick temper, especially after emotional stress, that suggests Liver Yang Rising. The face may look flushed, the tongue appears red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid. The practitioner asks about anger, frustration, and sleep quality to confirm this pattern.
When dizziness feels like a heavy, foggy head with chest tightness and nausea, particularly after rich meals or in damp weather, Phlegm-Dampness is likely. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. Questions about appetite, bloating, and phlegm production help distinguish this from other patterns.
Dizziness that worsens with physical activity or standing, accompanied by pale skin, fatigue, and heart palpitations, points to Qi and Blood Deficiency. The tongue is pale and the pulse is weak and thin. The practitioner will ask about diet, menstrual history, and recovery from illnesses to assess overall vitality.
A long-standing, chronic dizziness with tinnitus, sore lower back and knees, and forgetfulness suggests Kidney Essence Deficiency. This often develops gradually with aging or overwork. The tongue looks pale or red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and weak. The practitioner may inquire about growth, sexual function, or age-related changes.
Dizziness with sharp, fixed pains in the head or body, a dark or bruised complexion, and a choppy pulse indicates Blood Stagnation. The tongue may show dark spots. A history of trauma, surgery, or chronic pain supports this pattern. The practitioner checks for any circulatory signs that confirm stagnant blood.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Throbbing headache at the temples or top of the head, Flushed face and red eyes, Irritability and quick temper, Ringing in the ears (tinnitus), Soreness and weakness of lower back and knees, Dizziness worse with stress or anger
- **Worse with**: Stress and anger, Alcohol and coffee, Spicy or greasy foods, Overwork and lack of sleep, Hot, stuffy environments, Premenstrual phase
- **Better with**: Rest in a quiet, dark room, Cool, fresh air, Chrysanthemum or peppermint tea, Gentle neck and shoulder stretches, Deep breathing and meditation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for keeping Qi flowing smoothly and anchoring the body's Yang energy. When Liver Yin is weak, it can no longer hold the Yang down, so the Yang energy surges upward to the head like a flame, bringing heat and wind with it. This upward rush disturbs the clear orifices and creates the spinning, lightheaded sensation of dizziness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, especially at the sides, with a thin dry yellow or scanty coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), often rapid and forceful at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger directly agitate the Liver, causing Qi to stagnate and then surge upward as Yang, which is the core mechanism of this pattern's dizziness. Alcohol, coffee, and spicy foods add heat to the body, further fueling the rising Yang. Overwork and insufficient sleep drain the Yin that is needed to anchor Yang, making the upward rush worse. Rest in a quiet, dark environment, cooling foods and drinks, and calming practices like deep breathing help to cool the Liver, subdue the rising Yang, and allow the head to clear.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat more cooling, leafy greens and vegetables like spinach and cucumber, and sip chrysanthemum tea to gently cool the Liver. Cut back on coffee, alcohol, and spicy or fried foods, which can fan the rising Yang. Regular gentle exercise such as walking or tai chi helps Qi flow smoothly without overheating. Prioritize winding down before bed to nourish Yin and anchor Yang.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of heaviness in the head, as if wrapped in a cloth, Nausea or queasiness, especially after meals, Chest and upper abdominal fullness and oppression, Greasy, thick white tongue coating, Loose or sticky stools
- **Worse with**: Heavy, greasy, or sweet foods, Damp or humid weather, Overeating or large meals, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold raw foods and iced drinks
- **Better with**: Light, warm cooked meals, Gentle walking or stretching, Ginger or cardamom tea, Dry, crisp weather
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's ability to transform food and fluids weakens, moisture builds up and thickens into phlegm-dampness. This turbid, heavy substance clogs the middle of the body and can rise upward to cloud the head's clear orifices. The result is a distinctive dizziness that feels like a heavy fog or a cloth wrapped around the head, often accompanied by nausea and a sense of fullness in the chest.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, covered by a thick white greasy coating that is moist or wet. Pulse: slippery (Hua) and may be slow or soggy; the right Guan position (Spleen and Stomach) often feels especially weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness forms when the Spleen is overwhelmed by dietary and lifestyle burdens. Heavy, greasy, and cold foods directly add dampness and strain the Spleen, while damp weather mirrors the internal environment and worsens symptoms. Light, warm foods and gentle movement support the Spleen's transforming function, helping to dry up internal dampness and allow clear Yang to rise to the head.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid heavy, greasy, sweet, and cold raw foods that create more dampness. Favor warm, cooked grains, lightly steamed vegetables, and small amounts of ginger or cardamom in cooking. Eat moderate portions and avoid eating late at night. Gentle daily movement such as walking or stretching helps the Spleen Qi move and transform dampness.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness worse with exertion or standing, Pale face, lips, and nail beds, Persistent fatigue and weakness, Heart palpitations, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or prolonged standing, Skipping meals or poor nutrition, Cold raw foods and iced drinks, Excessive mental strain or worry
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warm, nourishing meals, Adequate sleep and rest, Gentle walking or stretching, Stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your body's Qi and Blood are the fuel and nourishment that keep your brain clear and steady. When both are low, the head doesn't receive enough vital resources, so you feel lightheaded and unsteady, especially when you stand up or exert yourself because your body struggles to pump Blood upward against gravity.
This pattern often develops after chronic illness, poor diet, or excessive worry that weakens the Spleen and Stomach-the organs that produce Qi and Blood. Without enough of these building blocks, the upward flow to the head falters, leaving you dizzy and foggy.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or show teeth marks, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine, weak, or empty-like a thread that collapses under slight pressure.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern stems from a deficit of Qi and Blood, so anything that further drains these resources-like overwork, poor diet, or mental stress-makes the dizziness worse by depleting the very substances needed to nourish the brain. Rest, warm nourishing meals, and gentle movement conserve and slowly rebuild Qi and Blood, allowing the head to receive adequate nourishment and the dizziness to ease.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, easily digestible meals like congee, soups, and stews with ingredients such as chicken, dates, goji berries, and dark leafy greens. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and avoid pushing through fatigue; gentle activities like walking or tai chi can help build Qi gradually over time.

### Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness with a hollow, empty sensation, Soreness and weakness of lower back and knees, Poor memory and difficulty concentrating, Tinnitus like a low, constant hum, Premature greying or hair loss, Frequent urination, especially at night
- **Worse with**: Overwork and chronic fatigue, Excessive sexual activity, Late nights and poor sleep, Prolonged stress and anxiety, Aging
- **Better with**: Adequate sleep and rest, Warm, nourishing meals, Gentle walking or stretching, Reducing mental strain, Moxibustion on the lower back
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys store your deepest reserves-the Essence (Jing). This Essence produces marrow, which fills the brain and keeps the senses sharp. When Kidney Essence runs low, the 'sea of marrow' becomes empty, and the head loses its nourishment. That emptiness is what you feel as a hollow, chronic dizziness, often accompanied by a faint ringing in the ears and a foggy memory.
Because the lower back and knees are considered the 'palace of the Kidneys', soreness and weakness there are telltale signs that the underlying depletion is in the Kidney system. This pattern usually develops slowly, from aging, overwork, or a constitutional weakness, and the dizziness tends to be constant rather than coming in sudden attacks.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with little or no coating, possible fine cracks. Pulse: fine (thready) and deep, especially weak at both rear positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Essence is the body's deepest reserve, built slowly through rest and nourishment, and spent by overwork, sexual excess, and aging. Sleep and warm, nutrient-dense foods help replenish the marrow that fills the brain, which directly eases the hollow dizziness. Conversely, anything that further drains the Kidneys-late nights, excessive mental or physical strain, or chronic stress-deepens the depletion and makes the head feel emptier.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Make rest and deep sleep a priority-the Kidneys recharge during the night. Eat warm, nourishing foods like bone broth, black beans, walnuts, and goji berries. Gentle daily movement such as Tai Chi or walking helps circulate Qi without draining reserves. Avoid overwork, late nights, and excessive sexual activity, which deplete Essence.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing head pain, Dizziness that worsens at night, Dark or purplish lips, nails, or complexion, History of head injury or chronic stabbing pain
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or air conditioning, Sitting or standing still for long periods, Emotional stress and frustration, Cold raw foods and iced drinks, Recent injury or trauma
- **Better with**: Gentle walking or stretching, Warmth on the head and neck, Warm, lightly spiced meals, Massage or acupressure, Rest in a quiet, dark room
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or obstructed, the vessels and tiny collaterals that carry nourishing Qi to the head get blocked. The brain is then starved of the clear Yang it needs to stay steady, and dizziness results. This pattern often follows a head injury, long-standing emotional stress, or a chronic illness that has congealed the blood, and the dizziness is typically accompanied by a fixed, stabbing pain.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, and a thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩, Sè), rough like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦, Xián) when Qi stagnation is also present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold causes blood vessels to constrict and blood to congeal, making stagnation worse-so warmth and gentle movement are natural antidotes. Movement, even light stretching, actively pumps blood through the collaterals and helps clear the obstruction that starves the head. Emotional stress tightens the Liver Qi, which is the engine of blood flow; when the Qi stagnates, the blood follows, so calming the mind and keeping the body warm directly unblock the stasis behind this type of dizziness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep moving gently throughout the day-short walks, stretching, and neck rolls help prevent blood from pooling and stagnating. Avoid sitting in one position for hours. Eat warm, lightly spiced foods like ginger, turmeric, and black pepper, and steer clear of icy drinks and raw, cold meals that congeal the blood. A warm compress on the back of the neck can bring quick relief during a dizzy spell.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does TCM distinguish between different types of dizziness?
TCM pays close attention to the quality and context of your dizziness. A spinning or whirling sensation that comes on suddenly with anger and a throbbing headache suggests Liver Yang Rising. A heavy, foggy feeling with nausea and a greasy tongue coating points to Phlegm-Dampness. Lightheadedness that worsens with standing or exertion, accompanied by fatigue and pale skin, indicates Qi and Blood Deficiency. A chronic hollow sensation with poor memory and low back soreness reflects Kidney Essence Deficiency. By matching your unique experience to one of these patterns, a practitioner can select the most effective herbs and acupuncture points.

### Can acupuncture and herbs really help with dizziness?
Yes. Acupuncture at points like Baihui (DU-20) and Fengchi (GB-20) helps regulate the flow of Qi and Blood to the head, while herbs address the underlying pattern. Many patients report a reduction in both the frequency and intensity of dizzy episodes within a few weeks. The key is accurate pattern diagnosis - treating the root cause rather than just masking the sensation - which is why TCM can be effective even when conventional tests come back normal.

### How long does it take for TCM to work on dizziness?
It depends on the pattern. Excess patterns like Liver Yang Rising often respond within 2-4 weeks. Deficiency patterns, where the body's reserves need to be rebuilt, may require 3-6 months of consistent treatment. Acupuncture sessions are typically weekly, and herbal formulas are taken daily. Your practitioner will track your progress and adjust the formula as your symptoms change.

### Can I take TCM herbs with my vertigo medication?
In most cases, yes, but it's essential to coordinate with both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor. Some herbs with sedative properties, such as Tian Ma (Gastrodia) or Suan Zao Ren (Sour Jujube Seed), may enhance the drowsiness caused by vestibular suppressants like meclizine or benzodiazepines. Always bring a full list of your medications to your TCM consultation so the formula can be tailored safely.

### What dietary changes can help with dizziness?
Generally, avoid greasy, heavy, or cold foods that create dampness and phlegm, which can cloud the head. Limit spicy and fried foods that stir up Liver Yang. Instead, favor warm, cooked meals like soups and congees, and include foods that strengthen the Spleen and nourish Blood - such as ginger, sweet potato, dark leafy greens, and lean meats. Eating regular, moderate meals supports stable Qi and Blood production.

### Is dizziness during pregnancy treatable with TCM?
Yes, TCM can often help with pregnancy-related dizziness, which commonly stems from Qi and Blood Deficiency as the body's resources are redirected to the fetus. Acupuncture and gentle herbal formulas can be used safely during pregnancy by an experienced practitioner. Always inform your practitioner that you are pregnant, and never self-prescribe herbs during pregnancy.
