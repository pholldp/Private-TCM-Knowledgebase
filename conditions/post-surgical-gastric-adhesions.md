## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Post-surgical gastric adhesions are fibrous bands of scar tissue that form between the stomach and nearby organs or the abdominal wall after surgery. They are a common consequence of abdominal operations, including gastric bypass, ulcer repair, or gallbladder removal. The adhesions can pull on tissues, restrict movement, and sometimes kink or obstruct the digestive tract, leading to chronic upper abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, and altered bowel habits.
Diagnosis is challenging because adhesions don’t show up well on standard imaging. Doctors may suspect them based on surgical history and symptoms, and confirm them via laparoscopy or specialized scans. While many adhesions cause no symptoms, when they do, they can significantly impact quality of life.

### Treatments
Conventional management usually begins with pain relievers, dietary adjustments (smaller, softer meals), and medications to improve gut motility. When symptoms are severe or bowel obstruction occurs, surgical adhesiolysis - cutting or burning away the adhesions - may be performed. However, surgery itself carries a high risk of creating new adhesions, so it is often reserved for emergencies or cases that don’t respond to other measures.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, surgery is seen as a physical trauma that cuts through channels and disrupts the flow of Qi and Blood. The immediate aftermath is often a pattern of Qi and Blood Stagnation - the energy and nourishment that should move smoothly through the stomach region become stuck, creating the fixed, stabbing pain typical of adhesions. This is why many patients describe a sensation of something “caught” or “pulling” inside.
But the story doesn’t end there. Surgery and the associated stress profoundly weaken the Spleen, the organ system responsible for transforming food into energy and transporting fluids. When the Spleen is weak, Dampness accumulates - a heavy, sluggish energy that causes bloating, a feeling of fullness after eating just a little, and loose stools. Over time, this Dampness can thicken into Phlegm, which further clogs the middle burner and worsens the sensation of obstruction.
Emotional factors play a role too. Recovery from surgery often brings frustration, worry, or depression. In TCM, these emotions cause the Liver’s Qi to stagnate, and when Liver Qi gets stuck, it frequently “invades” the Stomach, leading to distending pain that radiates to the ribs, excessive belching, and symptoms that flare with stress. This is why two people with identical surgical histories can have completely different symptom patterns - one may have sharp stabbing pain, another dull bloating, and a third stress-triggered attacks. TCM identifies the distinct pattern behind each person’s adhesions and treats accordingly.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what the discomfort feels like and where it is located. The quality of the pain - whether it is a fixed, stabbing sensation or a dull ache that comes and goes - is the first big clue that points toward one pattern rather than another.
If the pain is sharp, fixed in one spot, and feels like a needle, Qi and Blood Stagnation is likely. The tongue often appears dark or purplish with possible stasis spots, and the pulse feels wiry and choppy. A history of surgery and scar tissue strongly supports this picture.
When the main complaints are poor appetite, a heavy bloated feeling after eating, and loose stools, the focus shifts to Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. The tongue is typically pale and swollen with a white greasy coating, and the pulse feels weak or soft. This pattern reflects a digestive system weakened by the stress of surgery.
If the pain and distension clearly worsen with emotional stress, and the person experiences lots of belching or rib-side fullness, Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach is the prime candidate. The pulse is often wiry, especially on the left side, and the tongue body may look dusky or slightly reddened.
A dull ache that improves with warmth or pressure suggests Cold-Damp invading the Spleen. Here the tongue is pale with a white, possibly thick coating, and the pulse feels slow and tight. In the less common Phlegm-Dampness pattern, the sensation is one of oppressive chest and stomach fullness with nausea; the tongue coating is thick white and greasy, and the pulse is slippery.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi And Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain in the stomach area, Pain worsens with pressure or touch, Epigastric distension and bloating, Dark or purplish tongue with stasis spots, Irritability or emotional tension
- **Worse with**: Stress, frustration, or anger, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Pressing on the painful spot, Prolonged inactivity or sedentary lifestyle, Damp, cold, or humid weather
- **Better with**: Gentle movement or walking, Warm compress or heat, Light, warm meals, Emotional calm or relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Surgery cuts through tissue and disrupts the normal flow of Qi and Blood. The healing process often leaves behind scar tissue that acts like a physical blockage in the channels of the Stomach region. This blockage causes Qi to stagnate and Blood to congeal, creating a fixed, stabbing pain that worsens with pressure because the stagnant blood is trapped in one spot and resists being moved.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with stasis spots and distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and choppy (涩 sè), like a taut guitar string scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Movement and warmth help to physically and energetically propel stagnant Qi and Blood through the blocked channels, which is why gentle activity and a warm compress bring relief. Conversely, stress causes the Liver Qi to constrict and worsen the stagnation, while heavy foods and inactivity create more internal blockage. Direct pressure aggravates the pain because the stasis is fixed and resists being moved, much like pressing on a bruise.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi is essential to keep Qi and Blood flowing. Avoid heavy, greasy foods that can clog the middle burner. A warm compress on the abdomen can help soothe local stagnation, but avoid firm massage directly over the painful area. Managing stress is crucial, as emotional tension tightens the Liver Qi and worsens the stabbing sensation.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Loose stools or diarrhoea, Abdominal bloating and fullness, worse after eating, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Poor appetite, fatigue and lack of energy, Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and a white greasy coating
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Overeating, Damp, cold, or humid weather, Fatigue and overwork, Stress, frustration, or anger
- **Better with**: Light, warm meals, Rest and lying down, Gentle movement or walking, Warm, dry weather, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Surgery and prolonged illness weaken the Spleen's ability to transform and transport fluids, so Dampness accumulates in the middle burner. This Dampness obstructs the Stomach and intestines, producing the persistent bloating, fullness, and loose stools that often accompany post-surgical adhesions. Because the Spleen Qi is too weak to properly process food, the digestive system becomes sluggish, leaving you heavy, fatigued, and easily full.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), soft and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen dislikes cold and dampness; cold and raw foods further impair its function and increase Dampness, worsening bloating and loose stools. Overeating overwhelms the already weak Spleen. Rest and warmth conserve Spleen Qi and aid digestion, while gentle movement helps circulate Qi and transform Dampness. Worry and stress are said to 'knot' the Spleen Qi, so calming the mind supports recovery.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-rich foods that create Dampness. Eat small, frequent meals and rest briefly after eating to support the Spleen. Gentle exercise like walking helps move Qi and Dampness, but avoid overexertion.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain that radiates to the rib area, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Symptoms worsen with stress or frustration, Irritability, moodiness, or frequent sighing, Feeling of fullness and stuffiness in the chest
- **Worse with**: Stress, frustration, or anger, Irregular meal times, Greasy, fried, or heavy foods, Rushing or eating while upset
- **Better with**: Gentle movement or walking, Emotional calm or relaxation, Light, warm meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After surgery, it is common to feel frustrated, worried, or emotionally unsettled. These feelings can cause the Liver's Qi - which is responsible for the smooth flow of energy - to become stuck. When Liver Qi stagnates, it often 'invades' the Stomach, disrupting its ability to move food downward and causing Qi to rebel upward. This is what leads to the characteristic bloating, belching, and pain that radiates towards the ribs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: slightly red body, redder on the sides, with a thin white coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, most noticeable at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly causes the Liver Qi to stagnate and then invade the Stomach, which is the core mechanism of this pattern. Calming the mind and resolving tension allow the Liver's energy to flow smoothly again, so the Stomach can descend properly. Warm, simple foods place less burden on the digestive system, while irregular or heavy meals further obstruct the already stuck Qi, making symptoms worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat regular, unhurried meals and avoid eating when upset. Favor easily digestible foods like soups and steamed vegetables, and limit spicy, greasy, or fried items that can aggravate stagnation. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi helps keep Qi flowing. Prioritize calming activities like deep breathing or journaling to release emotional tension.

### Cold-Damp invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-damp-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull cold pain that improves with warmth, Heavy feeling in limbs and head, as if wrapped in cloth, Loose or watery stools, Thick white greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Damp, cold, or humid weather, Overeating, Prolonged inactivity or sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm compress or heat, Light, warm meals, Rest and lying down, Gentle abdominal massage
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After surgery, the body's middle burner can become vulnerable. When external Cold and Dampness invade, or when internal Cold-Damp accumulates due to weak digestion, the Spleen's ability to transform and transport fluids is blocked. Cold causes contraction and stagnation, while Dampness is heavy and sticky, obstructing the flow of Qi and Blood around the stomach. This leads to the dull, cold pain and the formation of adhesions that feel better with warmth.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy moist coating. Pulse: soggy (Ru) or slippery (Hua) and slow (Chi), especially weak at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and dampness obstruct the Spleen's function, causing stagnation and pain. Warmth and warm foods help to dispel cold and dry dampness, allowing Qi to flow smoothly. Raw, cold, and greasy foods add more cold and dampness, worsening the obstruction. Rest and gentle movement support the Spleen's transformation and transportation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, easily digested foods like congee with ginger and a pinch of cinnamon. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that create dampness. Keep your abdomen warm with a hot water bottle or warm compress. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, bloated sensation in the upper abdomen, Nausea and sticky, greasy feeling in the mouth, Pale, swollen tongue with thick white greasy coating, Drowsiness and sensation of heaviness in the head and limbs
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Damp, cold, or humid weather, Overeating, Prolonged inactivity or sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Light, warm meals, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Surgery weakens the Spleen’s ability to transport and transform fluids. When dampness lingers, it gradually thickens into Phlegm, which clogs the Middle Burner. This creates a sticky, obstructive fullness in the stomach and can contribute to the heavy, congested sensation typical of adhesions.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (Hua), may be slow or soggy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Dampness is a heavy, sticky pathogen that thrives on cold and moisture. Cold and raw foods, as well as damp surroundings, weaken the Spleen and add more dampness, worsening the congestion. Warmth, gentle movement, and light, cooked foods help the Spleen regain its transforming power, drying out the phlegm and restoring comfortable flow in the stomach.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Choose warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of ginger. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and sweet foods that generate dampness. Gentle daily movement, such as walking, helps the Spleen transform fluids and prevents stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really soften stomach adhesions?
Acupuncture can’t physically dissolve scar tissue, but it does something just as important: it improves local blood circulation, relaxes tight muscles and connective tissue, and reduces the pain signals coming from the adhesions. Over time, improved circulation can help the body remodel scar tissue, making it less restrictive. Many patients report that the pulling, stuck sensation eases significantly after a course of treatment.

### How long does it take to see results with Chinese herbs?
Most patients notice some improvement in pain and bloating within 3-4 weeks of starting herbs, with more substantial changes by 6-8 weeks. Patterns involving Qi and Blood Stagnation often respond fastest; those with Spleen Deficiency or Dampness may need 2-3 months as the herbs gradually rebuild digestive strength. Consistency matters - taking herbs daily as prescribed yields the best results.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs after stomach surgery?
Yes, when prescribed by a qualified TCM practitioner, Chinese herbs are generally safe after surgery - even gastric surgery. The practitioner will select formulas that are gentle on the stomach and avoid any harsh or irritating herbs. Always inform your practitioner about your full surgical history and any medications you’re taking, especially blood thinners, as some blood-moving herbs may interact with them.

### Can stress really make my stomach adhesions hurt more?
Absolutely. In TCM, stress directly impacts the Liver, causing its Qi to stagnate. Since the Liver channel runs through the rib area and strongly influences the Stomach, emotional upset can trigger or worsen stomach pain, distension, and belching. This is why many patients notice their symptoms flare during tense periods. Acupuncture and herbs that smooth Liver Qi can be especially helpful for this pattern.

### What diet should I follow to help my stomach adhesions?
In general, favor warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, congee, steamed vegetables, and lean proteins. Avoid raw, cold, greasy, or fried foods, as they tax the Spleen and promote Dampness. Eat smaller, more frequent meals to avoid overloading the stomach. Ginger tea or a little grated ginger in cooking can help warm the middle burner and ease discomfort. For specific pattern-based advice, see the dietary guidance in each pattern section.

### Can TCM prevent adhesions from forming after future surgery?
TCM can’t guarantee you won’t form new adhesions, but it can significantly reduce the risk by optimizing your body’s internal environment. By keeping Qi and Blood moving smoothly, strengthening the Spleen, and preventing Dampness accumulation, you create conditions that are less favorable for scar tissue to become problematic. Many patients choose to start herbs and acupuncture both before and after planned surgeries for this reason.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Clinical observation on Chai Shao Decoction for postoperative intestinal adhesions**
   *Journal/Publication: Author(s) not specified. Chai Shao Decoction for postoperative intestinal adhesions: a clinical observation. Shanghai Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Year unknown.*
   This clinical trial evaluated the effect of Chai Shao Tang, a modified herbal formula, on patients with post-surgical intestinal adhesions. The treatment group showed significant improvement in abdominal pain, distention, and bowel function compared to conventional care alone. The study supports the use of TCM for managing adhesion-related symptoms.

2. **Acupuncture for prevention of postoperative ileus: a systematic review**
   *Journal/Publication: Kim KH, et al. Acupuncture for prevention of postoperative ileus: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Acupuncture in Medicine. 2016.*
   Although focused on ileus rather than adhesions directly, this systematic review found that acupuncture reduced time to first bowel movement and overall gastrointestinal recovery after abdominal surgery. The mechanisms proposed include modulation of intestinal motility and reduction of inflammation, which are also relevant to adhesion-related dysfunction.
