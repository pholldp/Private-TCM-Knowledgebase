## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Tracheitis is inflammation of the trachea, the main airway leading to the lungs. It is usually caused by a viral or bacterial infection and often follows a cold or flu. The primary symptom is a persistent, often painful cough that may start dry and become productive with thick mucus. Other symptoms can include a raw sensation behind the breastbone, mild fever, and hoarseness. Diagnosis is typically made based on symptoms and a physical exam, and in some cases a chest X-ray or sputum culture may be used to rule out pneumonia or identify a bacterial cause.

### Treatments
Conventional care focuses on symptom relief and, if a bacterial infection is confirmed, a course of antibiotics. For a dry, hacking cough, doctors may recommend cough suppressants; for a productive cough, expectorants like guaifenesin can help thin mucus. Rest, increased fluid intake, and using a humidifier are standard supportive measures. Inhaled bronchodilators or corticosteroids are sometimes prescribed if there is significant airway irritation or wheezing.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, tracheitis falls under the category of “cough” (咳嗽, ké sòu) and is understood primarily as a disorder of the Lung’s two vital functions: dispersing and descending Qi. The Lungs are the “tender organ,” directly open to the outside world through the nose and throat, and therefore the first to be attacked by external pathogens like Wind, Cold, and Heat. When these invaders lodge in the Lung, the normal downward flow of Qi is blocked, and the body tries to expel the obstruction through coughing.
Which pathogen is involved changes everything. A Wind-Cold invasion constricts the Lung Qi and produces a harsh cough with thin, white phlegm and chills. Wind-Heat dries up fluids, creating a hacking cough with sticky yellow phlegm and a sore throat. If the initial invasion is not fully cleared, the pathogen can smolder and transform into Phlegm-Heat, leading to a deeper, rattling cough with thick, yellow-green sputum and chest oppression.
The tongue and pulse become crucial diagnostic tools at this stage - a red tongue with a yellow, greasy coating and a rapid, slippery pulse confirm that Heat and Phlegm are trapped in the Lung.
The story doesn’t end with acute attacks. When the digestive system (Spleen) is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly, and a heavy, chronic Damp-Phlegm pattern can set in, causing a persistent productive cough with lots of white, sticky phlegm. After prolonged bouts of tracheitis, the fever and relentless coughing burn through the body’s Qi and Yin, leaving a lingering dry cough with scanty sputum and deep fatigue. This is why one Western diagnosis of tracheitis can have five completely different TCM causes - and why the right treatment for a friend might be wrong for you.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner first asks about the cough’s sound and the sputum. A harsh, loud cough with thin, clear or white phlegm, chills, no sweating, and a floating tight pulse points to Wind-Cold invading the Lungs. The tongue coating is thin and white, and the person often feels cold and achy rather than feverish.
When the cough is more forceful with sticky yellow phlegm, a sore throat, and mild fever, the picture shifts to Wind-Heat invading the Lungs. The tongue is red with a thin yellow coating, and the pulse feels floating and rapid. The person usually feels hot, thirsty, and may sweat a little.
If the initial pathogen is not cleared, heat can brew into Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. Here the sputum becomes thick, yellow, and copious, the chest feels tight, and the tongue coating turns yellow and greasy. The pulse is slippery and rapid, and the cough often sounds rattling or wet.
A lingering, productive cough with profuse white, sticky sputum and a greasy tongue coating suggests Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs. This pattern often stems from weak digestion, so the person may also feel heavy, bloated, or tired. The pulse is typically slippery but not rapid.
Prolonged tracheitis can exhaust the body, resulting in Qi and Yin Deficiency. The cough becomes weak and dry, with scanty or no sputum, fatigue, and night sweats. The tongue is red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. This pattern is more common in the late recovery phase.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Heat invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thick yellow phlegm, Sore throat that feels raw or swollen, Fever more prominent than chills, Thirst or dry mouth, Nasal congestion with yellow mucus
- **Worse with**: Stress, frustration, or anger, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Cold wind, drafts, or cold weather, Overexertion and talking too much
- **Better with**: Rest and avoiding drafts, Cool, humidified air, Warm pear or mint tea, Steam inhalation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Heat enters through the nose and mouth, lodging in the Lung where it disrupts the normal downward flow of Qi. The Heat dries up fluids and turns phlegm thick and yellow, while the Wind element causes the cough to come in sudden bouts. This gives the typical acute tracheitis picture: a hacking cough, sore throat, and a fever that feels more prominent than any chill.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red tip, thin yellow coating, body slightly red. Pulse: floating and rapid (浮数 fú shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Heat is an exterior pathogen, so rest and avoiding wind help the body push it out. Cooling drinks and steam soothe the throat and clear Heat from the Lung, while spicy and greasy foods add more Heat and Phlegm, making the cough worse. Overexertion depletes the Qi that is fighting the infection, allowing the pathogen to linger.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Drink plenty of warm fluids like pear juice or mint tea to cool the Lungs and thin phlegm. Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods that create more Heat and Phlegm. Rest in a comfortable, draft-free room, and consider steam inhalation to ease the throat.

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thin white watery phlegm, Chills more prominent than fever, Absence of sweating, Clear watery nasal discharge, Itchy throat, Mild headache and body aches
- **Worse with**: Cold or icy drinks, Cold wind, drafts, or cold weather, Raw foods and dairy, Overexertion
- **Better with**: Hot ginger tea, Steam inhalation, Keeping the neck and chest warm, Resting in a warm room
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Cold is an external pathogen that enters through the skin and nose, lodging in the Lungs. The Cold constricts the Lung Qi, blocking its normal downward flow, which triggers a harsh cough as the body tries to expel the obstruction. The Lungs also fail to warm and transform fluids, so thin, white, watery phlegm forms and the nose runs clear.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (fu) and tight (jin).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold constricts the Lung Qi, trapping the pathogen and worsening the cough, so warmth is the antidote. Hot fluids and steam relax the airways, help the Lung Qi descend, and thin the phlegm so it can be coughed out more easily. Rest conserves the body's defensive Qi, while raw and cold foods introduce more Cold into the system, making the phlegm thicker and the cough more stubborn.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep your neck and chest warmly covered, especially in drafts. Sip hot ginger tea with a touch of brown sugar to warm the Lungs and gently promote sweating. Avoid cold drinks, raw salads, and dairy, which can congeal phlegm and make the cough stick around longer.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with thick, sticky yellow or green sputum, Chest oppression and fullness, Fever or feeling of body heat, Thirst with desire to drink, Rapid or labored breathing
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Dairy products, Smoking or smoky environments, Stress, frustration, or anger
- **Better with**: Cool, light meals, Steam inhalation, Rest and staying hydrated, Gentle, slow breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Often, an initial cold or flu is not fully cleared, and the pathogen lingers, transforming into Heat. This Heat scorches the body fluids, turning them into thick, sticky Phlegm that clogs the Lungs. The result is a deep, productive cough with yellow or green sputum, chest tightness, and a feeling of heat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating, possibly with red prickles on the front third. Pulse: slippery and rapid (滑数, huá shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, greasy, and dairy foods generate internal Heat and Phlegm, directly worsening the pattern. Cool, light foods help clear Heat and reduce Phlegm. Steam inhalation moistens and loosens thick sputum, making it easier to expel. Resting conserves Qi so the body can fight the pathogen, while gentle breathing exercises help open the chest without exhausting the Lungs.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, cooling foods like pear, radish, and water chestnut. Avoid greasy, spicy, and dairy foods that create more Phlegm. Steam inhalation with a few drops of eucalyptus oil can help loosen thick sputum.

### Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious white sticky phlegm that is easy to expectorate, Feeling of fullness and heaviness in the chest, Poor appetite, bloating, loose stools, Body heaviness and fatigue, Nausea or queasiness
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid environments, Dairy and greasy foods, Overeating or heavy meals, Prolonged sitting or inactivity
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Light, easily digestible foods, Gentle exercise like walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly. These fluids accumulate into Dampness and then congeal into Phlegm, which rises to clog the Lungs. The Lungs cannot descend Qi smoothly, so the body tries to clear the obstruction with a heavy, productive cough that brings up lots of white sticky sputum.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating that looks slimy. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), like beads rolling, especially at the right cun (Lung) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness and Phlegm are heavy and sticky, so they worsen with damp environments and rich foods that burden the Spleen. Warmth and movement help Qi transform fluids, while cold and raw foods damage the Spleen's digestive fire, generating more Dampness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid dairy, greasy, and sugary foods that create Dampness. Eat warm, cooked meals with moderate spices like ginger. Gentle daily movement helps Spleen Qi circulate and transform fluids.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry, weak cough with scanty or no sputum, Fatigue and shortness of breath, worse on exertion, Dry mouth and throat, often with night sweats, Warm sensation in palms, soles, and chest
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and talking too much, Dry, heated indoor air, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods
- **Better with**: Rest and gentle breathing exercises, Warm, moist air (humidifier), Sipping warm water or pear juice
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After a prolonged bout of tracheitis, the fever and relentless coughing burn through the body's Qi and Yin fluids. The Lungs lose their moistening and their strength to descend Qi properly. This leaves a lingering, weak dry cough with very little sputum, because the Lung's nourishing Yin has been dried up and its Qi is too depleted to expel phlegm effectively.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red or pale-red body, thin or absent coating, dry surface, possibly cracked. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, often rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin are already deficient, so any activity that consumes Qi (like overexertion) or dries Yin (like spicy food, dry air) worsens the cough. Rest and moistening foods rebuild the body's reserves, allowing the Lungs to regain their downward-moving, moistening function and finally quiet the cough.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the air humidified and sip warm fluids throughout the day. Eat moistening foods like pear, honey, lily bulb, and congee. Avoid late nights and overwork, and practice gentle, slow breathing to conserve Qi.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM treat tracheitis without antibiotics?
Yes. For viral tracheitis - which is the majority of cases - TCM herbs and acupuncture can help your body fight the infection, reduce inflammation, and expel phlegm without antibiotics. Even when a bacterial infection is present, TCM can be used alongside antibiotics to shorten recovery and protect your digestive system from side effects. Always consult your doctor before stopping any prescribed medication.

### How does acupuncture help a cough?
Acupuncture points on the Lung, Large Intestine, and Bladder meridians help restore the normal downward flow of Lung Qi, relax the airways, and reduce the urge to cough. Points like Feishu (BL-13) and Dingchuan (EX-B-1) directly calm the respiratory system, while distal points like Hegu (LI-4) expel external pathogens. Many patients feel their chest loosen and cough ease during or shortly after the first session.

### What herbs are used for tracheitis?
The herbs depend entirely on your pattern. For Wind-Heat with a dry cough, cooling herbs like Sang Ye (mulberry leaf) and Ju Hua (chrysanthemum) are used. For Wind-Cold, warming and dispersing herbs like Jing Jie and Bai Qian are chosen. If thick yellow phlegm is present, Huang Qin and Gua Lou clear Heat and transform Phlegm. A trained TCM practitioner will always prescribe a personalized formula - never take a single herb or a friend’s prescription.

### Is it safe to combine Chinese herbs with my cough medicine?
In most cases, yes - but you must inform both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor about everything you are taking. Some herbs with expectorant or sedative effects could amplify the effects of conventional cough medications. If you are on antibiotics, herbs can often be used to support your gut and reduce side effects. Never stop a prescribed medication without your doctor’s guidance.

### My cough keeps coming back every few months. Can TCM help?
Recurrent tracheitis is a strong sign of an underlying deficiency, usually in the Lung and Spleen systems, that leaves your respiratory tract vulnerable to every passing bug. TCM doesn’t just treat the acute episode - it uses the quiet periods between infections to strengthen your defensive Qi (Wei Qi) with tonic herbs and regular acupuncture. Many patients find their cycle of recurrent coughs breaks after a few months of consistent treatment.

### What should I eat when I have tracheitis?
Generally, avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-heavy foods, which can create more Phlegm. Favor warm, cooked meals and plenty of fluids. For a dry, tickling cough, pears and honey can be soothing. For a wet cough with white phlegm, ginger tea helps warm the Lungs. Your practitioner will give you specific dietary advice based on your pattern, but these basics apply across the board.
