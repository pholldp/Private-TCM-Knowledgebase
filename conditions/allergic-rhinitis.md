## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Allergic rhinitis, commonly known as hay fever, is an IgE-mediated inflammatory response of the nasal mucosa to airborne allergens such as pollen, dust mites, or pet dander. Symptoms include sneezing, itching, nasal congestion, and clear watery discharge. It is typically diagnosed based on clinical history and confirmed with skin prick or blood tests for specific allergens.

### Treatments
Conventional management includes allergen avoidance, intranasal corticosteroids, oral antihistamines, decongestants, and leukotriene receptor antagonists. For severe or persistent cases, allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or sublingual tablets) may be recommended to desensitize the immune system.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands allergic rhinitis primarily as a disorder of the Lung, Spleen, and Kidney systems, with the nose as the external orifice of the Lung. The Lung governs the skin and the body's protective Wei Qi. When Lung Qi is strong, Wei Qi forms a shield that keeps out wind, cold, and allergens. When it is weak, the nose becomes the first point of contact, triggering sneezing and clear discharge as the body tries to expel invaders.
The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids. If the Spleen is deficient, it fails to process fluids properly, leading to dampness that rises and pools in the nasal passages, causing a heavy, stuffy nose and sticky mucus. The Kidneys, as the root of Yang energy, warm the body and manage water metabolism. When Kidney Yang is deficient, cold fluids are not transformed and instead overflow upward, resulting in profuse, watery discharge that worsens in cold weather.
External triggers like pollen, dust, or cold air are seen not as the sole cause but as the "branch" that attacks a weakened "root." An acute sneezing fit after a cold wind is often a Wind-Cold invasion, while chronic year-round symptoms point to deeper deficiency. This is why TCM identifies several distinct patterns-Wind-Cold, Lung Qi Deficiency, Spleen Qi Deficiency, Kidney Yang Deficiency, Lung Heat, and even Qi Deficiency with Blood Stagnation-each requiring a different herbal formula and acupuncture strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner begins by asking what the nasal discharge looks like and what triggers the symptoms. The quality of the mucus - clear and watery versus thick and yellow - is one of the most important early clues that points toward one pattern rather than another.
When sneezing and a torrent of clear, watery discharge strike suddenly after exposure to cold wind, and the person feels a slight aversion to cold, the picture suggests Wind‑Cold invading the Lungs. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels floating and tight. This is an acute, externally‑driven flare‑up.
If the clear discharge is chronic and less dramatic, and the person tires easily, catches colds frequently, and may sweat spontaneously during the day, the root is likely Lung Qi Deficiency. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak. Here the protective Wei Qi is too frail to shield the nose from everyday triggers.
When the mucus is profuse, sticky‑clear, and accompanied by a heavy sensation in the nose, poor appetite, bloating, and loose stools, Spleen Qi Deficiency is the key pattern. The tongue is pale and swollen with teeth marks on the sides, and the pulse is weak. The spleen fails to manage fluids, so dampness collects in the nasal passages.
A deep, long‑standing rhinitis that worsens in cold weather and is paired with cold hands and feet, a sore low back, and frequent nighttime urination points to Kidney Yang Deficiency. The tongue is pale and puffy with a slippery white coat, and the pulse is deep and thin. The body’s warming fire is too low to control fluids.
If the discharge turns yellow and thick, and the person feels dry mouth, thirst, and a sensation of heat, Lung Heat is trapping warmth inside. The tongue is red with a yellow coat, and the pulse is rapid. This heat‑type allergic rhinitis often flares in warm, stuffy environments rather than in cold air.
In rare chronic cases where the congestion feels fixed and the nasal mucosa looks purplish, Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation may be present. The tongue is dark or has purple spots, and the pulse is choppy. Long‑standing weakness has allowed the flow of blood in the nose to become sluggish.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden sneezing fits, Profuse clear watery nasal discharge, Nasal itching triggered by cold air, Chills and aversion to wind, Absence of sweating, Headache and body aches
- **Worse with**: Cold air or wind, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Damp or cold damp weather, Sudden temperature changes
- **Better with**: Warm drinks or soups, Steam inhalation, Staying warm, Light sweating, Spicy foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Wind-Cold attacks the body's surface, it blocks the Lung's ability to diffuse and descend Qi. Because the Lung opens into the nose, this blockage traps fluid in the nasal passages, causing sudden, profuse clear discharge and sneezing as the body tries to expel the invader. The cold constricts the pores and channels, leading to chills, an absence of sweating, and a tight, floating pulse.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (浮 fú) and tight (紧 jǐn).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Cold is an external pathogen that attacks the body's surface and obstructs the Lung, which opens into the nose. Warmth and sweating help open the pores and expel the pathogen, while cold and damp environments allow it to penetrate deeper. Spicy foods and steam inhalation also disperse the Cold and unblock the nasal passages.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Drink warm ginger and scallion tea at the first sign of sneezing to help sweat out the Cold. Avoid cold drinks, raw salads, and dairy, which can increase phlegm. Keep the neck and nose covered in windy weather.

### Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chronic clear watery nasal discharge, Fatigue and low, weak voice, Sensitivity to wind and cold, Spontaneous daytime sweating, Pale complexion
- **Worse with**: Cold air or wind, Overwork or fatigue, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Emotional stress or worry, Seasonal transitions to autumn/winter
- **Better with**: Staying warm, Rest and adequate sleep, Warm drinks or soups, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi), Consistent daily routine
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Lungs govern the skin and open into the nose. When Lung Qi is deficient, the protective Wei Qi at the surface becomes weak, leaving the nose vulnerable to allergens, wind, and cold. This allows external triggers to easily penetrate, causing chronic clear nasal discharge, sneezing, and fatigue. Because the root is a Qi deficiency, symptoms are persistent and worsen with exertion or exposure to wind.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, tender body with possible teeth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: weak and empty, especially at the right cun (Lung) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Lung Qi Deficiency weakens the protective Wei Qi, so cold and wind easily penetrate the nose, triggering symptoms. Overexertion further depletes Qi, while raw cold foods damage the Spleen's ability to produce Qi. Stress and worry stagnate Qi and weaken Lung function. Warmth, rest, and gentle movement nourish Qi, and warm foods support the Spleen and Lung to strengthen the body's defenses.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals and avoid raw, cold foods that weaken Spleen and Lung Qi. Add mild Qi-tonifying ingredients like astragalus root (Huang Qi) to soups. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi builds Qi without depleting it. Keep the neck and nose warm in windy or cold weather, and avoid overexertion.

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Profuse, sticky-clear nasal discharge, Nasal congestion worse after eating, Bloating and loose stools, Fatigue and heaviness in the limbs, Pale, puffy tongue with tooth marks
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Dairy and greasy foods, Damp or cold damp weather, Overwork or fatigue
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Small, frequent meals, Rest after eating, Dry, warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into usable energy and moisture. When Spleen Qi is weak, it cannot properly manage fluids, and they accumulate as dampness. This dampness rises and pools in the nasal passages, causing a constantly runny or stuffy nose with thick, sticky-clear mucus. Because the Spleen also supports the Lung's defensive Qi, the weakened Spleen leaves the nose vulnerable to environmental triggers.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy body with tooth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: Soft and weak, especially at the right Guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen thrives on warmth and routine. Cold, raw, and heavy foods force it to work harder, generating more dampness that overflows into the nose. Warm, cooked meals and rest give the Spleen the support it needs to transform fluids properly. A dry environment and gentle movement help Qi circulate and prevent dampness from settling, while overexertion and damp conditions further deplete Spleen Qi and worsen symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible meals like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, as well as dairy, which burden the Spleen and create more dampness. Gentle daily movement, such as walking or stretching, helps Qi circulate without depleting it. Resting briefly after meals allows the Spleen to focus on digestion.

### Kidney Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Profuse watery nasal discharge, especially in cold weather, Cold limbs and low back soreness, Frequent urination, especially at night, Worse in winter and early morning, Deep fatigue and low spirits, Loose stools or early morning diarrhea
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or drafts, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overwork or fatigue, Excessive sexual activity, Prolonged standing
- **Better with**: Warmth on the lower back and abdomen, Warm drinks or soups, Rest and adequate sleep, Moxibustion, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys are the root of the body's Yang energy and govern water metabolism. When Kidney Yang is deficient, the body's warming function fails, and fluids are not properly transformed. Cold fluids accumulate and rise to the nose, causing profuse, watery discharge that is worse in cold weather. The lack of warmth also leads to cold limbs, low back soreness, and fatigue, while the weakened root fails to support Lung Qi, leaving the nose vulnerable to allergens.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a wet white coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak, especially at the rear (Kidney) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Yang is the body's internal fire, responsible for warmth and fluid transformation. Cold weather, cold foods, and overwork further deplete this fire, worsening fluid leakage and cold symptoms. Warmth, rest, and warming foods and herbs replenish the fire, helping the body transform fluids and stop the nasal discharge.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warming foods like ginger, cinnamon, lamb, and walnuts; avoid cold drinks and raw foods. Keep the lower back and feet warm, and practice gentle movement like tai chi. Moxibustion at home on the lower back and lower abdomen can be very helpful.

### Lung Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Yellow or greenish thick nasal discharge, Dry mouth and thirst, Sensation of heat in the nose or face, Irritability and restlessness
- **Worse with**: Spicy or greasy food, Alcohol, Hot, stuffy environments, Smoking or pollution
- **Better with**: Cool drinks, Fresh, cool air, Rest and calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Heat becomes trapped in the Lung channel, it scorches the body's fluids and disrupts the Lung's natural descending function. This causes the thin, watery mucus of a typical allergy to thicken and turn yellow or greenish. The rising Heat irritates the nasal passages, triggering intense itching and sneezing, while the fluid damage leads to a dry mouth and thirst.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially at the tip, with a dry yellow coating and possible prickly papillae on the front third. Pulse: rapid (shu), and may feel slippery (hua) or overflowing (hong) at the right cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Lung Heat is an excess Heat condition. Cooling foods and environments help clear the pathogenic Heat and soothe the irritated nasal passages. Conversely, hot, spicy, or stimulating substances add more Heat to the body, thickening the discharge and intensifying the itching and dryness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and smoking, which all generate internal Heat. Embrace cooling foods like pear, cucumber, and mint tea. Keep your living space well-ventilated and cool, and use a humidifier if the air is dry.

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark purple or dusky nasal mucosa, Fixed, unremitting nasal blockage, Stabbing pain in the nose or head, Pale face with a greyish tinge, Fatigue and shortness of breath
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Overwork or fatigue, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Damp or cold damp weather, Emotional stress or worry
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi), Rest and adequate sleep, Warmth on the face and neck
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In chronic allergic rhinitis, long-standing Qi deficiency-often from the Lungs and Spleen-fails to move blood through the delicate vessels of the nose. This leads to blood stasis, which shows up as a stubborn, fixed nasal congestion and a purplish, swollen nasal lining. The underlying Qi weakness also causes fatigue and a pale complexion, while the stasis creates a sensation of deep, unrelenting blockage that does not shift with weather or triggers like other patterns.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body, often with stasis spots and teeth marks, thin white coat. Pulse: deep, fine, and choppy (涩 sè).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi deficiency is the root, so anything that further drains Qi-like overwork, stress, or cold foods that damage the Spleen-worsens the condition. Cold also congeals blood, making stasis more entrenched. Warmth, rest, and gentle movement do the opposite: they support Qi, keep blood flowing, and prevent the stagnation that blocks the nose. Spices and warm foods gently stoke the body's metabolic fire, helping to push blood through the nasal passages.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews, and include gentle blood-moving spices such as turmeric, ginger, and a little garlic. Dark leafy greens and small amounts of lean protein help build blood. Avoid cold, raw foods and iced drinks, which chill the Spleen and worsen Qi deficiency.
Gentle daily movement-walking, tai chi, or qigong-keeps Qi and blood circulating without exhausting you. Avoid prolonged sitting. Keep your face and neck warm in cold or windy weather, and use a warm compress over the nose to ease congestion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture and herbs really stop my sneezing?
Yes, acupuncture and specific herbal formulas can quickly relieve acute sneezing and nasal congestion, especially when the attack is triggered by wind or cold. For chronic allergies, treatment aims to reduce the frequency and intensity of episodes by strengthening your body's defenses. Many patients find they can reduce or eliminate their reliance on antihistamines over time.

### How long until I see results?
Acute symptoms often improve within a few days of starting herbs and acupuncture. For long-standing, year-round allergies, a course of 8-12 weeks is typical to see lasting change. You may notice that your nose is less reactive and your energy improves gradually as your underlying Qi strengthens.

### Can I use TCM alongside my allergy medications?
Yes, TCM can be safely combined with most over-the-counter antihistamines and intranasal steroids. Many patients work with their doctor to taper medications as symptoms improve. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your physician about everything you are taking, as some herbs like Ma Huang (Ephedra) can interact with decongestants or stimulants.

### Does TCM work for seasonal allergies only or year-round?
TCM can help both seasonal and perennial allergic rhinitis. The approach differs: acute seasonal flares are treated by expelling the external trigger, while year-round symptoms are addressed by correcting the underlying deficiency in the Lung, Spleen, or Kidney. Many patients start treatment before their allergy season to build resilience and prevent attacks.

### Will I need to change my diet?
Diet plays an important role in TCM because certain foods can create dampness and phlegm that worsen nasal symptoms. Dairy, cold raw foods, greasy items, and sugar are common culprits. Shifting to warm, cooked meals and drinking warm fluids can support your treatment and help keep your nose clear.

### Can TCM cure allergies permanently?
TCM does not promise a permanent 'cure' in the sense of erasing all allergic tendencies, but it can significantly reduce the frequency and severity of attacks by strengthening your body's constitution. Many patients find that after several months of treatment, their allergies become much milder or go into remission, especially when combined with lifestyle adjustments.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for the treatment of allergic rhinitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anai.2014.10.019))
   *Journal/Publication: Taw MB, Reddy WD, Omole FS, Seidman MD. Acupuncture for the treatment of allergic rhinitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol. 2015 Feb;114(2):110-6.*
   This meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials found that acupuncture significantly reduced nasal symptom scores and improved quality of life in patients with allergic rhinitis compared to sham acupuncture and antihistamines. The benefits persisted for up to 3 months after treatment.

2. **Xiao-Qing-Long-Tang alleviates allergic rhinitis by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and modulating the TRPV1 channel** ([Link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41455386))
   *Journal/Publication: Xiao-Qing-Long-Tang alleviates allergic rhinitis by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and modulating the TRPV1 channel. PubMed. 2025. PMID: 41455386.*
   This animal study demonstrated that the classical formula Xiao Qing Long Tang reduces allergic rhinitis symptoms by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and modulating the TRPV1 channel, providing a mechanistic basis for its traditional use in treating watery nasal discharge and sneezing.
