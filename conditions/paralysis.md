## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, paralysis is the loss of muscle function in part of your body, caused by a disruption in the nerve signals that travel from the brain to the muscles. It can be localized, affecting just one limb or the face, or generalized, impacting larger areas. Common causes include stroke, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy, and traumatic nerve damage. Diagnosis typically involves neurological exams, imaging like MRI or CT scans, and nerve conduction studies to pinpoint the location and extent of the injury.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment focuses on addressing the underlying cause when possible and maximizing remaining function. This often includes physical therapy to maintain muscle strength and prevent contractures, occupational therapy to adapt daily activities, and medications such as muscle relaxants or pain relievers. In some cases, surgery may be performed to relieve pressure on nerves or repair damaged tissue. Assistive devices like braces, wheelchairs, or electrical stimulation units are also commonly used.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands paralysis primarily as a blockage or collapse of the body's network of channels (meridians) that carry Qi and Blood to the muscles and sinews. When these pathways are obstructed, the limbs lose their command and nourishment. The organs most commonly involved are the Liver, which governs the smooth flow of Qi and stores Blood, and the Spleen, which produces the Qi and transforms fluids. If the Spleen is weak, it can generate phlegm, a sticky pathological substance that can physically clog the channels. If the Liver is imbalanced, it can generate internal Wind, which sweeps phlegm upward to suddenly block the orifices of the heart and brain, causing acute paralysis like a stroke.
But obstruction is only half the story. Paralysis can also arise from a profound deficiency, where the body simply lacks the resources to power movement. When Qi and Blood are severely depleted, the muscles become flaccid and weak, unable to respond even if the channels are open. This is why one person's paralysis may be stiff and painful while another's is limp and weak - they represent opposite poles of TCM pathology, excess versus deficiency.
That is why the same Western diagnosis of paralysis can have multiple TCM causes. A sudden, dense paralysis with a rattling throat and greasy tongue coating points to Wind-Phlegm. Gradual, flaccid weakness with fatigue and a pale tongue suggests Qi and Blood Deficiency. Paralysis after trauma with sharp, fixed pain indicates Blood Stagnation. Heavy limbs with constipation and a thick yellow tongue coat signal Phlegm-Heat in the middle burner. And paralysis that worsens in cold, damp weather with a heavy sensation is often Wind-Cold-Damp invasion. Each pattern requires a fundamentally different treatment strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner begins by asking how the paralysis started - suddenly or gradually - and what other signals the body is sending. The speed of onset, the quality of the weakness, and accompanying sensations like pain, heaviness, or dizziness are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the loss of movement struck abruptly, often alongside slurred speech or a heavy, dizzy head, the picture suggests Wind-Phlegm obstructing the meridians. The tongue may have a thin white or sticky coat, and the pulse feels wiry or slippery. This pattern is common in stroke-related paralysis, where internal wind sweeps phlegm upward to block the channels.
When the weakness crept in slowly and the limbs feel floppy rather than stiff, accompanied by pale skin, fatigue, and breathlessness, the practitioner suspects Qi and Blood Deficiency. Here the muscles and tendons are undernourished. The tongue looks pale and puffy, and the pulse is thready and weak, telling a story of depletion rather than obstruction.
Paralysis that follows an injury, with sharp fixed pain and a dark or purplish tongue with stasis spots, points to Blood Stagnation.
If instead the limbs feel heavy and the person is constipated, irritable, and has a thick yellow greasy tongue coat with a rapid slippery pulse, Phlegm-Heat in the Middle Burner is the likely culprit.
Joint pain that worsens with cold or damp, along with a white greasy tongue coat and a floating tight pulse, suggests an invasion of Wind-Cold-Damp.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden loss of movement (often one-sided), Severe dizziness or spinning sensation, Copious white, frothy sputum, Slurred or difficult speech, Rattling or gurgling sound in the throat, Numbness or tingling in the limbs
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp environment, Heavy, greasy foods, Emotional stress, Prolonged inactivity
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Light, easily digestible meals, Gentle movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform fluids, which thicken into phlegm. Stress or internal imbalance stirs up Liver Wind, and this Wind carries the phlegm upward to block the channels and orifices that control movement and speech. The sudden obstruction is what causes the paralysis, dizziness, and slurred speech typical of a stroke.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp and cold congeal fluids into phlegm, while greasy foods directly create more phlegm, worsening the obstruction. Stress stirs Liver Wind, which can carry phlegm upward and trigger symptoms. Warmth, light eating, and gentle movement help resolve phlegm, move Qi, and prevent Wind from rising, making them natural allies in recovery.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid damp-producing foods like dairy, sugar, cold drinks, and greasy fried items. Eat warm, light meals such as congee, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of lean protein. Keep the room warm and dry, and protect the neck from drafts. Once stable, gentle daily movement helps Qi flow and prevents phlegm stagnation.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Gradual, progressive limb weakness and muscle wasting, Deep fatigue and shortness of breath on even slight exertion, Pale or sallow complexion, pale lips and nail beds, Dizziness or lightheadedness, especially when standing, Heart palpitations and poor sleep, Numbness or tingling in the limbs, Poor appetite and spontaneous sweating
- **Worse with**: Overwork and pushing through fatigue, Cold, raw foods, Emotional stress, Cold, damp environment
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement, Acupuncture and herbal tonics
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi is the body's motive force, and Blood is the material that nourishes every tissue. When both are deeply depleted, the muscles and sinews no longer receive the fuel they need to contract and hold the body. This leads to a gradual, progressive weakness that can eventually make movement impossible.
This pattern often appears in the aftermath of a prolonged illness, after significant blood loss, or from years of overwork and poor nutrition. Without enough Qi to lift the limbs and enough Blood to moisten the tendons, the body simply cannot sustain voluntary movement.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or show teeth marks, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, lacking force, especially in the right Guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are the body's fuel and building blocks. Rest and warm, nourishing food directly replenish them, while acupuncture and herbs stimulate the Spleen and Stomach to produce more. Overwork, cold, and emotional strain burn through Qi and congeal Blood, deepening the deficiency and leaving the limbs even less supported. Gentle movement helps circulate what Qi and Blood are present without wasting them, which is why it helps while vigorous exercise worsens the condition.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize warm, easily digested, nutrient-dense foods: bone broths, congee with red dates (jujube) and goji berries, slow-cooked stews with root vegetables, and small amounts of high-quality protein. Avoid cold, raw foods and iced drinks, which chill the Spleen and impair digestion. Gentle daily movement like seated Qigong or slow walking can help Qi circulation, but rest is non-negotiable - stop well before exhaustion.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain in the paralyzed area, Pain worsens at night or with rest, Dark or purplish lips and nails, Dusky complexion or dark circles under eyes, History of trauma or surgery before paralysis
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp environment, Prolonged inactivity, Cold, raw foods, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Gentle movement, Warmth on the affected area, Light massage, Warm, cooked meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or blocked-often after an injury, surgery, or long-standing circulatory problems-the channels that carry Qi and nutrients to the muscles and nerves are starved. This stasis creates a fixed, stabbing pain in the affected limb and prevents it from receiving the signals needed for movement, leading to paralysis. The pain typically worsens at night because stillness allows the stagnation to settle deeper.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè) like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦 xián) if Qi stagnation is present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and stillness cause blood to congeal like a stream freezing over, deepening the stasis that blocks the channels. Warmth, gentle movement, and massage act like thawing that stream-they encourage blood to flow again, easing pain and gradually restoring function. Stress tightens the Liver Qi, which further constricts blood vessels and worsens stagnation, so calming the mind is a direct way to unblock the channels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the body warm and avoid iced drinks and raw foods, which can congeal blood. Incorporate gentle blood-moving spices like turmeric, ginger, and a little black pepper into meals. Gentle daily movement-even passive range-of-motion exercises if active movement is limited-helps prevent further stasis. Avoid sitting or lying in one position for too long.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Middle Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Limb heaviness with paralysis, Constipation, Epigastric fullness and bloating, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Bitter taste in the mouth
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Emotional stress, Hot, humid weather, Overeating or late-night meals
- **Better with**: Light, easily digestible meals, Regular bowel movements, Cool, airy environment, Gentle movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When greasy, spicy food and emotional stress generate heat and phlegm, they can clog the stomach and intestines-the Middle Burner. This phlegm-heat mass blocks the normal downward movement of Qi, causing constipation, bloating, and a heavy, sluggish feeling. The trapped heat and turbid phlegm then rise to disturb the mind and spill into the meridians, obstructing the flow that powers the limbs and resulting in paralysis with a sense of heaviness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, swollen body with a thick yellow greasy coating, especially in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, spicy, and heavy foods directly fuel the phlegm and heat that clog the middle burner, making the limbs feel even heavier and the bowels more stuck. Emotional stress stirs up Liver fire, which combines with phlegm to create more obstruction. A light, bland diet drains dampness and starves the heat, while regular bowel movements clear the blocked pathway, allowing Qi to descend and the channels to reopen. Cool, calm environments and gentle movement help disperse the stagnation without overheating the body.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods that create more phlegm and heat. Eat light, easily digested meals like congee, steamed vegetables, and small amounts of lean protein. Drink warm water throughout the day, but avoid iced or sugary drinks. Gentle daily movement, such as walking or stretching, helps keep Qi and bowels moving. Manage stress with deep breathing or quiet time, as frustration can fan the heat.

### Wind-Cold-Damp
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-damp)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Painful stiffness that improves with warmth, Worsening in cold, damp, or windy weather, Heaviness and aching of the limbs, Numbness or tingling, Aversion to cold and wind
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp environment, Prolonged inactivity, Cold, raw foods, Exposure to drafts or wet clothing
- **Better with**: Warmth on the affected area, Gentle movement, Warm, dry environment, Spicy warm foods (ginger, cinnamon)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind, Cold, and Dampness are external pathogens that can invade the body's channels. When they settle into the muscles and joints, they block the flow of Qi and Blood, making the limbs feel heavy, stiff, and painful. Over time, this severe obstruction can shut down movement entirely, resulting in a painful type of paralysis that worsens in cold or damp weather.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale or slightly swollen body with a white, greasy coating and a moist surface. Pulse: moderate (huan) or soggy (ru); may feel tight (jin) if Cold predominates.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind, Cold, and Dampness are the very pathogens obstructing the channels. Cold and damp environments, or cold foods, add more of these pathogens and thicken the obstruction, worsening paralysis and pain. Warmth, gentle movement, and warm foods help disperse the Cold and dry the Dampness, allowing Qi and Blood to flow again and gradually restoring movement.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the body warm and dry. Use moxibustion or hot compresses on stiff areas. Eat warm, cooked foods like stews and soups with ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods, and stay out of damp, chilly environments.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture help with paralysis after a stroke?
Yes, acupuncture is one of the most widely used TCM therapies for post-stroke paralysis. It aims to reopen blocked channels, stimulate nerve function, and improve blood flow to the affected limbs. Many patients begin treatment as soon as they are medically stable, and research suggests that starting early can enhance motor recovery. A typical course involves 2-3 sessions per week for several months, often in combination with herbal medicine and physical therapy.

### How soon after a stroke can I start TCM treatment?
In many cases, TCM treatment can begin within days or weeks of a stroke, as soon as the patient is medically stable and no longer in the acute emergency phase. Acupuncture and herbal formulas are often introduced alongside conventional rehabilitation in the hospital setting. However, it is crucial to coordinate with your neurologist and TCM practitioner to ensure safety, especially if you are on blood thinners or other medications.

### Does TCM work for spinal cord injury paralysis?
TCM can be a valuable supportive therapy for spinal cord injury, focusing on reducing inflammation, promoting nerve regeneration, and maintaining muscle tone in the affected areas. Herbal formulas that invigorate blood and open channels are often used. Acupuncture may help manage pain and spasticity. While complete reversal of severe spinal cord damage is rare, many patients report improvements in sensation, bladder control, and quality of life when TCM is integrated with conventional rehabilitation.

### Are there any dietary changes that can support recovery from paralysis?
Yes, diet plays a supporting role in TCM treatment. In general, it is best to avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, which can damage the Spleen and generate phlegm or dampness - both of which can obstruct the channels. Warm, cooked, and easily digestible meals are encouraged to support Qi and Blood production. Specific foods like walnuts, black sesame, and bone broths are often recommended to nourish the kidneys and sinews. Your practitioner may give more tailored advice based on your specific pattern.

### Is TCM safe to combine with my blood thinners or other medications?
TCM herbs and acupuncture can generally be used alongside conventional medications, but caution is needed with anticoagulants. Some herbs that invigorate blood, such as Hong Hua, may increase the risk of bleeding. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor about all medications and supplements you are taking. Acupuncture is generally safe when performed by a licensed professional, even for patients on blood thinners, though mild bruising may occur.

### Will I need to continue TCM treatment forever, or are the results lasting?
The goal of TCM is to correct the underlying imbalance so that the body can maintain function on its own. For acute conditions like post-stroke paralysis, a course of treatment lasting several months may be sufficient to achieve maximum recovery, after which you may only need occasional maintenance. For chronic degenerative conditions, longer-term or periodic treatment may be beneficial to sustain mobility and slow progression. Your practitioner will work with you to taper treatment as your condition stabilizes.
