## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, chronic headache is defined as head pain occurring on 15 or more days per month for at least three months. It encompasses several subtypes, including chronic migraine, chronic tension-type headache, and medication-overuse headache. Diagnosis relies on a detailed symptom history, neurological examination, and sometimes imaging to rule out secondary causes. Treatment typically combines acute pain relief, preventive medications, and lifestyle changes.

### Treatments
Acute attacks are managed with over-the-counter analgesics, NSAIDs, or migraine-specific drugs like triptans. Preventive strategies include daily medications such as beta-blockers, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, or CGRP inhibitors, as well as Botox injections for chronic migraine. Non-drug approaches like physical therapy, stress management, and cognitive behavioral therapy are also recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees chronic headache as a disturbance in the flow of Qi and Blood to the head. The head is the meeting place of all Yang channels and is nourished by Yin and Blood - when something blocks that flow or when the nourishment runs low, pain arises. The quality of the pain tells the story: a distending, throbbing ache points to rising Yang or Fire, a heavy foggy sensation points to Dampness and Phlegm, a fixed stabbing pain points to Blood Stagnation, and a dull empty ache points to deficiency.
Two common excess patterns are Liver Yang Rising and Damp-Phlegm. In Liver Yang Rising, the Liver's Yin is too weak to anchor its Yang, so the Yang surges upward like heat rising, causing throbbing temple or vertex pain that flares with stress and anger. In Damp-Phlegm, a weakened Spleen fails to process fluids, creating a heavy turbid phlegm that clouds the head's clear orifices - the pain feels like a damp cloth wrapped around the head, often with nausea and a foggy mind.
Blood Stagnation is another frequent cause, often following an old head injury or years of unresolved Qi stagnation. Here the pain is fixed, stabbing, and usually worse at night, as if a knife is stuck in one spot. The blood flow in the head's tiny vessels is simply obstructed.
Deficiency patterns - Qi and Blood Deficiency or Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency - produce a dull, nagging ache that feels hollow and empty. The head, being the highest part of the body, is starved of the nourishment it needs. This pain worsens with fatigue and improves with rest, and is often accompanied by pale skin, dizziness, or lower back soreness.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner begins by listening carefully to the quality of the pain and the story around it. A throbbing, distending headache that flares with stress or anger points very differently than a dull ache that worsens with exhaustion. The tongue and pulse are then checked to confirm the underlying pattern, because each pattern leaves its own signature on these diagnostic signs.
If the pain is distending or throbbing, especially at the temples or vertex, and comes with dizziness, irritability, or a bitter taste, Liver Yang Rising is likely. Stress and anger are common triggers. The tongue appears red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and forceful - signs of excessive yang energy surging upward.
When the headache feels fixed in one spot, stabbing, and intensifies at night, Blood Stagnation is the probable pattern. There may be a history of head injury or long‑standing pain. The tongue is dark purple, often with stasis spots, and the pulse is choppy or wiry, reflecting obstructed blood flow in the head’s fine vessels.
A heavy, foggy sensation - as if the head is wrapped in a damp cloth - together with chest oppression and nausea suggests Damp‑Phlegm clouding the clear orifices. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. This pattern often improves with movement and worsens with humidity or heavy meals.
A dull, persistent ache that feels worse after fatigue, prolonged work, or menstruation points to Qi and Blood Deficiency. The person looks pale, feels tired, and may have a pale tongue with a thin coating. The pulse is thready and weak, indicating the head is not receiving enough nourishment. In Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency, the headache is accompanied by dizziness, tinnitus, and lower back soreness; the tongue is red with little coating, and the pulse is thready and rapid, revealing an emptiness of yin that fails to anchor yang.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending or throbbing headache, especially at the temples or top of the head, Dizziness or vertigo, feeling top-heavy, Irritability and quick temper, Flushed face and red eyes, Ringing in the ears (tinnitus), Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Stress, anger, or frustration, Alcohol or spicy, greasy food, Overheating or hot weather, Lack of sleep or overwork
- **Better with**: Rest in a dark, quiet room, Cool compress on the forehead, Chrysanthemum or peppermint tea, Gentle neck and shoulder stretches
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In this pattern, the Liver's Yin and Blood are too weak to anchor its Yang. The unanchored Yang rises like hot air, surging upward along the channels to the head. This creates a distending, throbbing pain-often at the temples or top of the head-because the head is the meeting place of all Yang energy, and the sudden upward rush of Liver Yang disturbs the clear orifices.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, redder at the sides, with a thin dry yellow or scanty coat; may tremble slightly. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, often especially forceful at the left middle (Liver) position; may be fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is marked.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and anger directly stir Liver Qi upward, which is the very mechanism of this pattern-rising Yang surges to the head, causing the throbbing pain. Heat, whether from food, weather, or overwork, adds fuel to the Yang, making it flare more intensely. Cooling and calming habits, like rest in a dark room, cool compresses, and chrysanthemum tea, help anchor the Yang back down, clear heat, and soothe the Liver, which relieves the pressure in the head.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy foods, and greasy meals-they add heat and fuel the rising Yang. Eat more cooling, yin-nourishing foods like celery, spinach, cucumber, and chrysanthemum tea. Prioritize winding down before bed and keep a regular sleep schedule. Gentle, non-competitive movement like walking or tai chi helps keep Liver Qi flowing smoothly without overstimulating it.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain that does not move, Pain worsens at night, Pain worse with pressure, Dark or purplish lips and nails, Dark menstrual blood with clots, Rough, dry, scaly skin
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods and icy drinks, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Stress and frustration, Cold or damp weather, Nighttime
- **Better with**: Gentle exercise or movement, Warm compress on neck, Turmeric and ginger in diet, Rest with head slightly elevated
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or obstructed in the head's tiny collateral channels, the result is a fixed, stabbing pain like a knife in one spot. This often follows an old head injury or years of unresolved Qi stagnation that has congealed into blood stasis. Because blood circulation naturally slows during rest, the pain characteristically worsens at night, and pressure on the tender area makes it feel even more intense.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 Sè) - rough and uneven like a knife scraping bamboo; may be wiry (弦 Xián) if Qi stagnation is also present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold congeals and stagnates blood, which directly worsens the underlying stasis, so warmth and warming foods bring relief. Movement activates Qi and blood circulation, preventing further pooling. Stress causes Qi stagnation, which over time deepens into blood stasis, so managing emotional tension is essential. Nighttime is when blood flow naturally slows, making the pain more noticeable.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep moving with gentle daily exercise like walking or tai chi to promote blood flow. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which can congeal blood and worsen stasis. Incorporate warming spices like turmeric and ginger, and consider adding small amounts of black vinegar to meals to invigorate blood.

### Damp-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, wrapped or foggy pain, Nausea or vomiting, Chest and upper abdominal stuffiness, Muzzy-headedness or brain fog, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating, Slippery (Hua) pulse
- **Worse with**: Damp, humid environments, Rich, greasy or sweet foods, Overeating or skipping meals, Lying down right after eating
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Gentle exercise or movement, Warm cooked meals, Avoiding heavy, greasy foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's ability to process fluids weakens, dampness builds up and gradually condenses into phlegm. Phlegm-dampness is heavy and turbid, so it rises to cloud the clear orifices of the head, producing a headache that feels like a heavy, foggy weight rather than a sharp pain. This same phlegm obstructs the chest and stomach, often bringing nausea, chest stuffiness, and a thick greasy coating on the tongue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and swollen with teeth marks, a wet surface, and a thick white greasy coat. Pulse: slippery (滑 Huá), like beads rolling, or soggy (Ru) especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is a heavy, sticky pathogen that thrives in damp environments and is fed by greasy, sweet, or cold foods that overwhelm the Spleen. Warmth, movement, and simple cooked meals help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, preventing dampness from accumulating into phlegm and clouding the head.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and congees, and limit dairy, sugar, fried foods, and cold raw items that create dampness. Gentle daily movement such as walking or stretching helps keep Qi and fluids moving so phlegm does not settle. Avoid lying down immediately after meals.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, persistent ache, Worsens with fatigue or overwork, Worse after menstruation, Pale complexion and lips, Chronic fatigue and weakness, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overwork and exhaustion, Menstruation (blood loss), Skipping meals, Cold, raw foods and icy drinks, Excessive worry or stress
- **Better with**: Rest and sleep, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle exercise or movement, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi and Blood are the body's vital nourishment. When they are deficient, the head-the highest part of the body-is starved of the nutrients it needs to function comfortably. This leads to a dull, persistent ache that feels like a hollow emptiness, not a sharp or throbbing pain.
Because Qi is weak, the pain worsens whenever energy is low, such as after a long day, during menstruation, or when you are run down. The head simply lacks the strength to stay clear and pain-free.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or show teeth marks, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially at the right Guan position (Spleen).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This headache is caused by a shortage of Qi and Blood. Overwork, blood loss, and poor nutrition further deplete these essential resources, making the head feel empty and achy. Rest, warm meals, and blood-building foods replenish the body's supply, allowing the head to be properly nourished and the pain to lift.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals regularly; never skip meals. Include blood-nourishing foods like red dates, goji berries, dark leafy greens, and a little organic red meat or bone broth. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which weaken the Spleen. Prioritize rest and gentle movement like walking or tai chi-overexertion will drain your limited reserves.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, empty headache, Dizziness and ringing in the ears, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Night sweats with heat in the palms and soles, Red tongue with little or no coating
- **Worse with**: Lack of sleep or overwork, Spicy, fried, or heating foods, Stress and frustration, Hot, dry weather
- **Better with**: Rest and sleep, Cool, dim environment, Moistening foods (e.g., pear, black sesame)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys store essence and the Liver stores blood; together they nourish the brain (the 'sea of marrow'). When Kidney and Liver Yin become depleted, the brain loses its foundational nourishment, and the 'empty fire' of deficiency heat rises to the head. This creates a dull, empty headache that feels deep inside the head, often accompanied by dizziness, tinnitus, and a sore lower back. The pain is typically worse with mental strain or lack of sleep because these further drain Yin.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine, rapid, and often wiry; weak at the left Chi (Kidney) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver Yin are easily depleted by overwork, insufficient sleep, and emotional strain, which allows deficiency heat to rise and trigger headache. Rest and cooling, moistening habits replenish yin and calm the false fire, thus relieving the pain.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat a nourishing yin diet: black sesame seeds, goji berries, mulberries, walnuts, and dark leafy greens. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods that consume yin. Go to bed before 11 p.m. to protect yin, and avoid overwork and excessive screen time. Gentle practices like tai chi or restorative yoga help preserve essence.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does acupuncture work for chronic headaches?
Acupuncture works by unblocking the flow of Qi and Blood through the channels that run to the head. Specific points on the head, neck, hands, and feet are chosen based on your pattern - for example, Liver 3 (Taichong) to subdue rising Yang, or Stomach 40 (Fenglong) to clear Phlegm. Many patients feel a sense of relief during or shortly after a session, and regular treatments reduce the overall frequency and severity of attacks.

### Will I need to take Chinese herbs forever?
No. Herbal formulas are prescribed for the active treatment phase, which typically lasts a few weeks to a few months. Once the underlying pattern is corrected and headaches are under control, herbs are gradually reduced and then stopped. Some people choose to take a lighter maintenance formula or return for seasonal tune-ups, but long-term daily herbs are not the goal.

### Can TCM help if I've had headaches for many years?
Yes, though the timeline may be longer. Long-standing headaches often involve deeper patterns like Blood Stagnation or mixed deficiency and excess. While it may take several months to untangle these layers, many people with decades of headaches still achieve significant and lasting improvement.

### Is it safe to combine TCM with my current headache medications?
In most cases, yes. Acupuncture is very safe alongside conventional drugs. Herbal formulas can also be taken with medications, but it's essential to inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor. Some herbs, particularly those that move Blood, can interact with anticoagulants, so full disclosure is critical.

### What dietary changes will support my treatment?
Generally, avoid greasy, fried, and heavily processed foods that create Dampness and Phlegm, and limit spicy or heating foods that can stir up Liver Yang. Warm, cooked meals are easiest for the Spleen to digest. Your practitioner will give you more specific guidance based on your pattern - for example, chrysanthemum tea for Liver Yang or ginger tea for Dampness.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
Most patients begin with once-weekly sessions for 4-8 weeks. As headaches improve, the frequency is reduced to every two weeks, then monthly, until treatment is complete. The total number of sessions depends on the pattern and how long the headaches have been present.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for the prevention of episodic migraine** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001218.pub3))
   *Journal/Publication: Linde K, Allais G, Brinkhaus B, et al. Acupuncture for the prevention of episodic migraine. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2016;(6):CD001218.*
   This Cochrane systematic review of 22 trials with 4985 participants found that acupuncture is at least as effective as prophylactic drug treatment for reducing migraine frequency, and has fewer adverse effects. The benefit persisted over six months of follow-up.
