## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Mania is a state of abnormally elevated or irritable mood, energy, and activity that lasts at least one week and causes significant impairment. It is most often part of bipolar I disorder. Common symptoms include grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and impulsive, high-risk behaviors. In severe cases, psychotic features like delusions or hallucinations may appear.
Diagnosis is made through clinical interview based on DSM-5 criteria. The exact cause is unknown, but it is thought to involve complex interactions of genetics, brain chemistry, and environmental triggers.

### Treatments
Acute mania is typically managed with mood stabilizers such as lithium or valproate, often combined with antipsychotic medications like olanzapine or risperidone. Short-term benzodiazepines may be used for severe agitation. Long-term maintenance treatment aims to prevent future episodes and often includes the same medications, along with psychotherapy and psychoeducation to help patients manage their condition.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands mania primarily as a disturbance of the Heart-Spirit, or Shen. The Heart is said to house the mind, and when it is calm, sleep is restful and emotions are balanced. In mania, pathogenic factors like fire, phlegm, and blood stasis agitate the Heart, causing the mind to race, emotions to flare, and behavior to become disinhibited.
The Liver plays a key role too: it governs the smooth flow of Qi, and when that flow is blocked by prolonged frustration or stress, Qi can stagnate and transform into fire that rushes upward to the head.
Phlegm is another major player. In TCM, phlegm is not just mucus in the lungs - it is a thick, turbid substance that forms when the body's fluids aren't transformed properly, often due to a weak Spleen. This phlegm can drift upward and cloud the Heart's orifices, creating mental fog and confusion.
When phlegm combines with fire, the result is even more chaotic: a sticky, heated obstruction that produces severe agitation, shouting, and reckless behavior.
Blood stasis with heat adds yet another dimension. Imagine a traffic jam in the vessels that traps heat; the Heart-Spirit can't be nourished, and the trapped heat flares upward, causing manic outbursts that may be accompanied by fixed stabbing pains or a dark complexion. Because these root causes are distinct, the same Western diagnosis of mania can arise from very different TCM patterns - and each requires its own treatment strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When phlegm and fire combine, the disturbance is intense and chaotic. A practitioner looks for severe agitation, shouting, incoherent speech, and reckless behavior, often with chest oppression and thick yellow sputum. The tongue shows a yellow greasy coating, and the pulse feels slippery and rapid. This pattern stands out because the heavy, sticky signs of phlegm are just as prominent as the fire.
If the mania flares with explosive anger, a red face, bitter taste in the mouth, and a pounding headache, the focus shifts to the Liver. Here fire rises upward without the heavy phlegm component. The tongue is red with a thinner yellow coating, and the pulse is wiry and rapid. Constipation and dark urine are common clues. Unlike the phlegm-fire pattern, there is less mental cloudiness and more of a fierce, upward-rushing heat.
In this pattern, the mind is clouded rather than inflamed. The person may appear dull, confused, or have slurred speech, with episodes of agitation that feel more disorganized than explosive. The tongue coating is white and greasy, not yellow, and the pulse is deep and slippery or slow. Practitioners distinguish this from the fiery patterns by the absence of heat signs-no red face, no bitter taste-and the presence of copious phlegm rattling in the throat.
When blood flow is obstructed and heat builds, the mania is often accompanied by a dark or purplish complexion, fixed stabbing pains, and a hard, painful lower abdomen. The tongue is dark red or purple with stasis spots, and the coating may be dry and yellow. The pulse feels wiry, choppy, or deep. This pattern is set apart by the stasis signs-dark tongue, fixed pain-which are absent in the purely phlegm or fire patterns.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Phlegm-Fire harassing the Heart
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fire-harassing-the-heart)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Mental restlessness and agitation with incoherent speech, Copious thick yellow sticky phlegm, rattling in throat, Flushed red face and red eyes, Chest oppression and feeling of internal heat, Severe insomnia or disturbed sleep, Bitter taste in mouth, thirst
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress or anger, Hot weather or stuffy rooms, Sleep deprivation
- **Better with**: Cooling foods, Quiet, dark environment, Deep breathing or meditation, Gentle exercise or movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Phlegm and Fire are two pathological products that combine and rise upward to disturb the Heart-Spirit, which governs mental clarity and emotional balance. When thick, sticky Phlegm is heated by internal Fire, it becomes agitated and obstructs the Heart’s orifices, leading to severe restlessness, incoherent speech, and reckless behavior. This is why the person feels an intense internal heat, has a racing mind, and may act impulsively or violently.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, swollen with red prickly tip, thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: Slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò), full and overflowing at the left Cun (Heart) position, slippery at right Guan (Spleen/Stomach).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Fire thrives on heat and dampness, so spicy, greasy foods and alcohol add fuel to the Fire and create more Phlegm. Emotional stress stirs up Liver Qi, which can transform into Fire and further agitate the Heart. Cooling foods and a calm environment help clear Heat, while rest and meditation settle the mind and prevent the upward surge of Fire. Gentle movement keeps Qi flowing without overstimulating, and regular sleep restores Yin to anchor the Fire.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which generate Phlegm and Heat. Eat light, cooling meals with plenty of vegetables, and include foods like pear, water chestnut, and mung beans. Establish a regular sleep routine and practice calming activities such as gentle walking, tai chi, or meditation to help settle the mind.

### Liver Fire Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Intense irritability and explosive anger, Throbbing headache at the temples or top of head, Red face and bloodshot eyes, Bitter taste in the mouth, Loud ringing in the ears
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress or anger, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot weather or stuffy rooms
- **Better with**: Cooling foods, Deep breathing or meditation, Cold compress on the forehead
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Liver’s Qi is blocked by prolonged frustration or anger, it can ignite into excess Fire. This Fire has a natural tendency to flare upward, rushing to the head and disturbing the Heart-Spirit (Shen). The result is the explosive irritability, red face, and restless agitation seen in manic episodes, as the mind loses its calm anchor.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body with noticeably redder sides, dry yellow coating. Pulse: Wiry, rapid, and forceful, especially strong at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Liver Fire is a pattern of excess heat that rises. Emotional stress, especially anger, directly feeds the Liver’s fire, while spicy foods and alcohol introduce more heat into the body. Cooling foods and calming practices work by reducing that internal heat and giving the Liver a chance to settle, which naturally eases the pressure on the Heart-Spirit and calms the manic state.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Cool the body with foods like cucumber, celery, watermelon, and mung beans. Strictly avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy dishes, and fried foods, which add fuel to the fire. Regular gentle exercise like walking or swimming helps move stagnant Liver Qi before it ignites.

### Phlegm Misting the Heart
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-misting-the-heart)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Mental confusion or dullness, Gurgling or rattling sound in the throat, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating, Excessive saliva or drooling, Depression alternating with agitation
- **Worse with**: Damp, greasy foods, Cold, raw foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Damp weather, Emotional stress or anger
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle exercise or movement, Quiet, dark environment, Dry, airy living space
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Phlegm is a thick, turbid substance that forms when the body's fluids aren't transformed properly. In this pattern, phlegm accumulates and drifts upward to cloud the Heart, which houses the mind and spirit. This creates a heavy mental fog, confusion, and emotional flatness. However, when the obstruction becomes severe, the trapped spirit can erupt in sudden, disorganized manic agitation-often with muttering, staring, and a gurgling throat. Unlike fiery mania, these outbursts lack intense heat and are more chaotic and confused.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with a thick, white, greasy coating; may appear stiff. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), smooth and rolling, with good strength.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm arises when the Spleen's ability to transform fluids is weakened, often by a diet rich in damp, greasy, or cold foods and a sedentary lifestyle. Warm, cooked meals and light exercise strengthen the Spleen and move Qi, helping to transform and drain phlegm. A calm, dry environment prevents further dampness from accumulating. Emotional stress disrupts Qi flow and can congeal fluids into phlegm, so avoiding stress helps keep the mind clear.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid damp-producing foods like dairy, greasy or fried foods, and cold raw items. Eat warm, cooked meals with aromatic spices like ginger and cardamom to help transform phlegm. Gentle daily movement such as walking supports Qi flow and prevents stagnation. Keep living spaces dry and airy.

### Blood Stagnation with Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation-with-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing pain in head or body, Dark or purplish complexion, Lower abdominal distension and constipation, Irritability and restlessness, worse at night, Thirst with no desire to swallow
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress or anger, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Sedentary lifestyle, Hot weather or stuffy rooms
- **Better with**: Cooling foods, Gentle exercise or movement, Quiet, dark environment, Deep breathing or meditation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Blood stasis acts like a traffic jam in the vessels, preventing the Heart-Spirit from being nourished and settled. When heat gets trapped in this stagnation, it flares upward, agitating the mind and causing manic outbursts. The combination of stuck blood and internal heat often produces fixed, stabbing pains, a dark complexion, and a restless feeling that worsens at night.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: reddish-purple body with stasis spots, thin dry yellow coating. Pulse: choppy (se) and rapid (shu), often wiry at the left Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger stir up Liver Qi, which can turn into fire and worsen blood stasis. Spicy, greasy foods and alcohol add heat to the body, while a sedentary lifestyle allows blood to stagnate further. In contrast, cooling foods and gentle movement help clear heat and invigorate blood, restoring a smooth flow that calms the mind.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Choose cooling, blood-moving foods like celery, hawthorn berries, peach, and eggplant. Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods that add heat and thicken the blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or qi gong helps keep blood flowing and prevents stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture help calm a manic episode?
Yes, acupuncture can be a valuable tool during a manic episode. Points like Shenmen (HT-7) and Neiguan (PC-6) directly calm the Heart-Spirit and settle agitation. The treatment is gentle and often produces a noticeable sense of relaxation within minutes. However, for severe mania, acupuncture is best used alongside herbal medicine and should never replace emergency psychiatric care if safety is a concern.

### Will Chinese herbs interact with my mood stabilizers?
Some herbs, particularly those that clear heat or move blood, can enhance the sedative or blood-thinning effects of medications. It is essential to inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor about all treatments you are receiving. Never stop or adjust your psychiatric medication without medical supervision. A qualified TCM practitioner can select formulas that minimize interaction risk.

### How quickly can TCM help with mania?
Many patients experience a reduction in irritability and better sleep within the first week of herbal treatment. More stable mood and clearer thinking typically develop over 4-6 weeks. The timeline depends on the underlying pattern: fire-type mania often responds faster, while phlegm or blood stasis patterns take longer to clear. Consistency with herbs and sessions is key.

### Is TCM safe during a severe manic episode?
TCM can be safe and supportive, but severe mania with psychosis, aggression, or risk of harm requires immediate conventional medical care. Once the acute danger is managed, TCM can be integrated to support recovery and prevent relapse. Always consult a healthcare professional for severe symptoms.

### What dietary changes support treatment?
In general, avoid spicy, greasy, and overly rich foods that generate heat and phlegm. Favor cooling, light foods like leafy greens, cucumber, pear, and mung beans. Reduce caffeine, alcohol, and stimulants. Eating regular, moderate meals supports the Spleen and helps prevent phlegm accumulation, which is a common driver of manic agitation.

### Can TCM prevent future manic episodes?
By addressing the root imbalances-whether fire, phlegm, or stasis-TCM aims to reduce the constitutional tendency that leads to mania. Long-term herbal maintenance and periodic acupuncture can help stabilize mood and prevent relapse, especially when combined with lifestyle and dietary adjustments. Many patients find that the frequency and intensity of episodes decrease over time.
