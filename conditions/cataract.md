# Cataract (圆翳内障)
**Pinyin**: yuán yì nèi zhàng | **Kind**: Condition | **Category**: Eye & Vision Problems  
**Synonyms**: Cataracts, Cataracts (Early Stage) | **Body Regions**: Face — Periorbital

## Preview Summary
A cataract is never just an eye problem in TCM - it's a sign of deeper organ imbalance, and early treatment with herbs and acupuncture may slow its progression while easing dry eye...

---

## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
A cataract is a clouding of the eye's normally clear lens, causing blurry or dim vision, glare sensitivity, and faded colors. It usually develops slowly with age, though injury, certain medications, or diseases like diabetes can accelerate it. An eye doctor diagnoses it through a dilated eye exam and checks how much it interferes with daily life.

### Treatments
In the early stages, stronger glasses, brighter lighting, and anti-glare sunglasses help. When vision loss interferes with daily activities, surgery to remove the cloudy lens and replace it with an artificial one is the only definitive treatment. There are no eye drops or medications proven to reverse cataracts.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the lens is considered an extension of the Kidney essence and is nourished by Liver blood. The eye is the orifice of the Liver, and the pupil specifically belongs to the Kidney. When these organ systems weaken - through aging, overwork, or chronic stress - the lens loses its moisture and clarity, much like a pond that grows murky when its spring dries up.
But deficiency isn't the only culprit. If the Spleen is weak, the clear Yang Qi it should send upward to the eyes never arrives, and the lens clouds from lack of nourishment. Emotional strain can cause Liver Qi to turn into Fire that rises and disturbs the eye, creating glare and blur with heat signs. Even a past head injury can leave stagnant Blood blocking the channels that feed the lens.
This is why a TCM practitioner doesn't just look at the cataract itself. They ask about dryness or grittiness, energy levels, digestion, body temperature, and emotional state. The same cloudy lens can stem from a dried-up Yin pond, a cold Yang fog, a stagnant Blood barrier, or a Fire that's burning too bright - and each needs a fundamentally different treatment.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what your vision actually feels like and how it has changed over time. Cataracts (圆翳内障, yuán yì nèi zhàng) are rarely a single-pattern problem; the gradual clouding of the lens reflects a deeper imbalance. The speed of onset, accompanying discomfort, and overall energy level are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the blurring crept in slowly alongside dry eyes, dizziness, and a low back ache, the practitioner suspects Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency. The tongue is often red with little coating and the pulse feels thin or deep-thin. This is the classic picture of age-related lens decline, where the nourishing Yin fluids that should bathe the eye have run low.
When the sight is dim but the person also looks pale, feels chronically tired, has a poor appetite, and tends toward loose stools, the focus shifts to Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency. Here the tongue is pale with a thin white coat and the pulse is weak. The lens clouds because the clear Qi that the Spleen should send upward simply never reaches the eyes.
A different kind of dimness appears with Kidney Yang Deficiency. Vision is dull, the face is pale, the hands and feet feel cold, and the lower back and knees ache. The tongue looks pale and puffy, and the pulse is deep and slow. In this pattern the warming, brightening fire of the Kidneys is too weak to keep the lens clear.
If the vision problem feels more sudden or is accompanied by a gritty, dry sensation, glare sensitivity, a bitter taste in the mouth, and irritability, Liver Fire Blazing is likely. The tongue is red with a yellow coat and the pulse is wiry and rapid. Here heat rises along the Liver channel and disturbs the clear orifices of the head, creating a sense of smoky or fiery blur.
When dark spots drift across the field of vision or sight declines abruptly after an injury or a long period of emotional stress, Qi and Blood Stagnation is the leading suspect. The tongue may have purple spots or a dusky body, and the pulse feels wiry and choppy. Stagnation means the lens is literally starved of fresh blood flow, so the cloudiness develops unevenly.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Gradual, painless blurring of vision like looking through a fog, Dry, gritty eyes that worsen with reading or screen use, Dizziness and ringing in the ears, Night sweats and heat in the palms, soles, and chest, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Flushed cheekbones in the afternoon
- **Worse with**: Overwork and exhaustion, Spicy, fried, or greasy foods, Prolonged screen time, Anger, frustration, or emotional stress, Hot weather or dry environments
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Cool, dark environments, Goji berry and chrysanthemum tea, Gentle eye exercises and breaks from screens
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The lens of the eye is nourished by the essence of the Liver and Kidney. As we age, or through overwork and stress, Liver blood and Kidney Yin gradually decline. This leaves the lens without the moisture and nourishment it needs, so it slowly becomes cloudy and rigid. The resulting deficiency heat further dries the eyes, making vision foggy and uncomfortable.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, and a dry surface. Pulse: Fine, rapid, and often wiry; weak at the left Chi (Kidney) and Guan (Liver) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver Yin are the deep reserves that moisten and nourish the eyes. Overwork, late nights, and emotional stress burn through Yin, while spicy foods and dry environments add heat that further depletes it. This leaves the lens dry and cloudy. Rest, sleep, and cooling, moistening foods like goji berries and chrysanthemum tea replenish Yin, helping to clear the vision.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat moistening, Yin-nourishing foods: goji berries, chrysanthemum tea, black sesame, walnuts, and dark leafy greens. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods that consume Yin. Rest your eyes regularly, especially from screens, and prioritize deep sleep to replenish essence.

### Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-stomach-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Gradual blurring of vision, Poor appetite, Bloating and fullness after eating, Loose or unformed stools, Fatigue and physical weakness, Sallow or pale complexion
- **Worse with**: Overwork and exhaustion, Raw and cold foods and drinks, Heavy, greasy meals, Excessive worry and mental strain
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Small, frequent meals, Rest and adequate sleep, Gentle walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen and Stomach are the engines that turn food into clear, nourishing Qi. When they weaken, that clear Qi cannot rise to the head to feed the lens of the eye. Over time, the lens loses its transparency and becomes cloudy, just as a pond grows murky when fresh springs stop flowing. This is why the blurry vision comes with digestive signs like poor appetite and loose stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy body with tooth marks on the edges and a thin white coating. Pulse: Weak and soft overall, especially empty at the right middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: A weak Spleen and Stomach produce insufficient clear Qi to rise to the eyes, so anything that drains digestive energy-like raw foods, overwork, or worry-deepens the fog. Warm, cooked meals and rest conserve the Spleen's fire, while gentle movement encourages the upward flow of clear Yang without overtaxing the system.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, and easily digestible foods like congee, stews, and steamed vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen's digestive fire. Eat smaller, regular meals and chew thoroughly. Gentle daily movement such as walking or Tai Chi helps lift Qi without exhausting it.

### Kidney Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vision is dim and misty, like looking through a fog, Cold and aching lower back and knees, Feeling cold, especially in the lower body, Frequent clear urination, especially at night, Fatigue and low energy, Pale complexion
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Overwork and exhaustion, Raw and cold foods and drinks, Excessive sexual activity, Standing for long periods
- **Better with**: Warmth on the lower back, Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle walking, Sunlight exposure
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys' Yang is the body's vital fire, responsible for warming and brightening the entire system. When this fire weakens with age or overwork, the clear Yang fails to ascend to the eyes. The lens, a crystalline manifestation of Kidney essence, loses its warmth and becomes clouded, like a window fogging over from cold.
Fluid metabolism also slows without sufficient Yang, allowing dampness to accumulate in the eye. The result is vision that feels dim and misty, as if looking through a cold fog, often worse in the morning or after exposure to cold.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, and tender with teeth marks; wet white coating. Pulse: deep, slow, and weak, especially at the Chi position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Yang is the body's internal fire. Cold and overwork drain this fire, allowing cold to dominate and further cloud vision. Warmth, rest, and warm foods replenish the fire, helping clear Yang rise to the eyes. Gentle movement circulates Qi without depleting it, while excessive activity and standing strain the lower back and Kidneys, weakening the root.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warming foods like lamb, walnuts, ginger, and cinnamon. Avoid raw, cold, and icy foods that further chill the body. Keep the lower back and feet warm. Gentle daily movement like walking helps circulate Yang Qi.

### Liver Fire Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of glare or halos around lights, Dry, gritty, burning sensation in eyes, Throbbing headache at temples, Bitter taste in mouth, Irritability and short temper
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or emotional stress, Spicy, fried, or greasy foods, Alcohol, Hot weather or dry environments, Prolonged screen time
- **Better with**: Cool compress over eyes, Goji berry and chrysanthemum tea, Quiet, dark room, Gentle eye rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When intense anger or frustration is held in, Liver Qi transforms into Fire that surges upward along the Liver channel to the head. This rising Fire disturbs the clear orifices of the eyes, clouding the lens and creating a sensation of glare and visual blurring. The heat also dries out the eye fluids, causing gritty discomfort and redness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, especially at the sides, with a dry yellow coating; possible red prickles. Pulse: Wiry, rapid, and forceful, especially at the left middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and spicy foods ignite Liver Fire, which rushes upward to the head and eyes, worsening glare and irritation. Cooling foods and rest help drain this heat and calm the Liver, while darkness reduces eye strain and light sensitivity.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat cooling foods like chrysanthemum tea, mung beans, and celery. Avoid alcohol, spicy foods, and fried dishes that fuel internal heat. Practice gentle stress management such as meditation or walking to prevent emotional flare-ups.

### Qi And Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Floating dark spots or cobweb-like shadows, Sudden or rapid decline in visual clarity, Fixed, stabbing pain in or around the eye, History of eye injury or long-standing emotional stress, Dark purplish tongue with stasis spots
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or emotional stress, Raw and cold foods and drinks, Prolonged sitting, Eye trauma
- **Better with**: Warm compress, Gentle walking, Emotional release, Warm, cooked meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi stagnates and Blood congeals, the clear Yang that should rise to nourish the eye lens gets blocked. Over time, this lack of nourishment causes the lens to lose its transparency, leading to cloudy vision. This pattern often appears after a head injury or from long-held emotional tension, and it can cause a sudden worsening of sight with dark spots floating across your field of vision.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦) and choppy (涩), like a knife scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional frustration and physical injury both cause Qi to stagnate and Blood to congeal, which is the root of this pattern. Cold constricts the vessels, making stasis worse, while warmth and gentle movement encourage free flow. Releasing pent-up emotions and eating warming foods help dissolve the blockages that starve the lens of nourishment, allowing clarity to return.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warming, blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, black fungus, and small amounts of red meat. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks that congeal Blood. Gentle daily exercise like walking or tai chi helps keep Qi and Blood flowing. Protect your eyes from further injury and practice stress-reducing activities to prevent emotional stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture or herbs reverse a cataract?
No, TCM cannot dissolve an already-formed dense cataract. However, in the early stages it may slow or halt further clouding by correcting the underlying deficiency or stagnation that allowed the lens to lose its clarity. Many people also find their accompanying symptoms - dry eyes, glare sensitivity, and fatigue - improve noticeably.

### How long will I need to take herbs?
Herbal treatment for cataracts is not a quick fix. Most practitioners recommend a course of 3-6 months, with adjustments to the formula every few weeks as your pattern shifts. Once vision stabilizes, you might move to a maintenance dose or seasonal tune-ups to support long-term eye health.

### Can I use TCM alongside my regular eye drops or after surgery?
Yes, TCM can complement conventional care. If you use prescription eye drops, continue them as directed. After cataract surgery, herbs and acupuncture can help reduce inflammation and support healing - but always tell your surgeon and TCM practitioner about everything you're taking, as some herbs may thin the blood and need to be paused before the procedure.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a big supportive role. In general, eat foods that nourish the Liver and Kidneys: goji berries, chrysanthemum tea, dark leafy greens, black sesame, walnuts, and small amounts of high-quality animal protein. Avoid very spicy, greasy, or deep-fried foods that create internal heat. If your pattern involves Spleen weakness, warm cooked meals and easy-to-digest soups are especially helpful.

### Is TCM safe if my cataract is from diabetes?
Yes, but blood sugar control remains the priority. The TCM practitioner will choose herbs and points that support both the eye condition and your underlying diabetic pattern, often focusing on Yin deficiency and Blood stasis. Monitor your glucose levels carefully and keep your endocrinologist informed of any herbal additions.

### What about children with cataracts?
Congenital or early childhood cataracts are rare and usually require prompt surgical management. TCM can support a child's overall constitution and recovery after surgery, but it is not a substitute for specialist pediatric ophthalmology care. Gentle herbs and pediatric acupuncture (non-needle techniques) may be used under close supervision.
