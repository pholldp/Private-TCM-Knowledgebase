## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, purpura refers to purple-colored spots or patches on the skin caused by bleeding from small blood vessels. It can occur when platelets are low, when blood vessels are inflamed, or when clotting factors are deficient. Common causes include idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP), medication reactions, or infections. Diagnosis typically involves a physical exam, blood tests to check platelet counts and clotting function, and sometimes a skin biopsy.

### Treatments
Treatment depends on the underlying cause. For ITP, corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), or immunosuppressants may be used to raise platelet counts; in some cases the spleen is removed. For HSP, supportive care such as rest and hydration is often sufficient, though steroids may be prescribed for severe abdominal pain or kidney involvement. The goal is to control acute bleeding and prevent complications, but these treatments do not always address the tendency to recur.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the blood is held within the vessels by the Spleen's holding function and the integrity of the vessel walls. When Heat enters the blood, it makes the blood "reckless" - it moves chaotically and forces its way out of the vessels, causing bright red or dark purpura. This can happen after an external invasion of Wind-Heat, or from internal Heat generated by emotional stress, diet, or deep Yin deficiency.
But not all purpura comes from Heat. When the Spleen is weak and its Qi is deficient, it can no longer hold the blood in place. The blood oozes out passively, creating pale, lingering spots that come with fatigue and a poor appetite. This is a deficiency pattern, and it needs to be treated by strengthening the Spleen and tonifying Qi - not by cooling the blood.
Dampness also plays a role. When Damp-Heat settles in the lower body, it obstructs the channels and damages the blood vessels, leading to dark purplish spots often accompanied by urinary symptoms. This pattern is especially common in children with HSP. Each of these patterns points to a different root cause, and that is why a single Western diagnosis can have several TCM treatments - the right one depends on the full picture of your symptoms, tongue, and pulse.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When someone presents with purpura, a TCM practitioner first asks about the onset, color, and any accompanying symptoms. The appearance of the spots-bright red, pale, or dark-and the presence of fever, fatigue, or bleeding elsewhere are crucial clues. A look at the tongue and a feel of the pulse then confirm which pattern is driving the condition.
A very common pattern is Wind-Heat invading the collaterals. This typically causes an acute outbreak of bright red purpura, often with fever, itching, and a scratchy throat. The tongue is red with a thin yellow coating, and the pulse feels floating and rapid. These signs point to an external pathogen that needs to be cleared and cooled.
When Heat enters the Blood level, the purpura appears bright or dark red and may be widespread. The person often experiences nosebleeds, gum bleeding, or blood in the urine, together with intense thirst and restlessness. The tongue is deep red with a yellow coat, and the pulse is rapid and forceful-signs of reckless blood that must be cooled and calmed.
Chronic, recurring purpura with pale or light-colored spots suggests Qi not controlling Blood. This pattern comes with persistent fatigue, a sallow complexion, dizziness, and poor appetite. The tongue looks pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse is weak and thready. Here the focus shifts from clearing heat to strengthening the Spleen and holding blood back inside the vessels.
Less common is a deep Yin deficiency pattern, where empty fire disturbs the blood. Purpura appears intermittently, often accompanied by low-grade fever, night sweats, a dry mouth, and a feeling of heat in the palms and soles. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. This calls for nourishing Yin and gently cooling the blood.
When Damp-Heat settles in the lower burner, the purpura tends to be dark and is frequently accompanied by blood in the urine (hematuria) or stool. The tongue shows a sticky yellow coat, and the pulse is slippery and rapid. This pattern is more common in children and points to the need to drain dampness and clear heat from the deeper parts of the body.
A rarer picture is Spleen Deficiency with Dampness, where the purpura is dull and the person feels heavy, fatigued, and has little appetite. The tongue is pale with a white, greasy coating, and the pulse is weak. This indicates that the digestive system is too weak to transform fluids, allowing dampness to clog circulation and cause bleeding under the skin.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bright red purpura that appears suddenly, Fever with mild chills, Itching of the skin, Sore throat, Acute onset, often after a respiratory infection
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Hot weather or overheating, Wind exposure, Overexertion or overwork
- **Better with**: Rest and calm, Cooling foods and drinks, Cool environment, Light, breathable clothing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Heat is an external pathogen that invades the body through the skin and nose, often after a cold or flu. The Heat component enters the blood collaterals, making the blood 'reckless' and causing it to leak out of the vessels into the skin. This produces the sudden appearance of bright red purpura, usually with fever, sore throat, and itching.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red tip and edges with a thin yellow (or thin white-to-yellow) coating. Pulse: floating and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Heat is an external pathogen that thrives on heat and wind. Spicy, greasy, and hot foods create internal heat, which combines with the external pathogen and worsens purpura. Wind exposure invites more external Wind into the body, aggravating the condition. Rest and cooling foods help clear heat and support the body's defensive Qi to expel the pathogen, allowing the blood to calm and the spots to fade.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, or fried foods that generate internal heat. Eat cooling foods like mung beans, cucumber, and drink chrysanthemum tea. Rest well and avoid wind exposure. Keep the skin clean and avoid scratching.

### Heat in the Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heat-in-the-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bright or dark red purpura, often widespread, Nosebleeds or gum bleeding, Blood in urine or stool, Feeling of internal heat, worse at night, Thirst with desire for cold drinks, Restlessness and irritability, Flushed red face
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress or anger, Hot weather or overheating
- **Better with**: Cool environment, Cooling foods and drinks, Rest and calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When excessive Heat enters the blood level, it agitates the blood and makes it reckless. The blood then moves frenetically, bursting out of the vessels and pooling under the skin as bright or dark red purpura. This same Heat can also scorch the nose, gums, or urinary passages, causing nosebleeds, gum bleeding, or blood in the urine.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: deep red body with red spots or prickles, thin yellow dry coating. Pulse: rapid (Shu), wiry (Xian), and slippery (Hua), with force.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heat in the Blood is a true excess-Heat pattern. Anything that adds more Heat-spicy food, alcohol, anger, hot environments-will further agitate the blood and worsen bleeding. Cooling foods, rest, and a calm mind help settle the reckless blood and allow the vessels to heal.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid all warming and spicy foods, alcohol, and coffee. Eat plenty of cooling, hydrating foods like cucumber, watermelon, and mung beans. Keep the body cool and calm; avoid overexertion and emotional upset that can stir up internal fire.

### Qi not controlling Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-not-controlling-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale purple spots that appear gradually, Chronic, recurrent bruising, Fatigue and exhaustion, Poor appetite and loose stools, Pale complexion and lips, Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, Raw, cold foods and drinks, Excessive worry and mental strain
- **Better with**: Rest and calm, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Spleen Qi is responsible for holding blood within the vessels. When Spleen Qi becomes weak, it can no longer perform this holding function, allowing blood to seep out under the skin and form purpura. Because the underlying problem is a deficiency, the spots tend to be pale purple, appear gradually, and are accompanied by fatigue and a washed-out complexion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and puffy with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine and weak, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overwork and mental strain drain Spleen Qi, the very energy needed to hold blood in the vessels, so resting and eating warm, nourishing foods directly rebuild that Qi. Raw and cold foods introduce cold and dampness that further weaken the Spleen's digestive and holding functions, making purpura more likely.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digested meals like congee, soups, and stews. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Gentle daily movement such as walking supports Qi circulation, but rest when you feel tired.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency causing Heat in the Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency-causing-heat-in-the-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Purpura comes and goes, not acutely explosive, Night sweats or afternoon tidal heat sensation, Dry mouth and throat, worse at night, Low back and knee soreness or weakness, Dizziness, tinnitus, or blurred vision, Five-centre heat (palms, soles, chest)
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress or anger, Overexertion or overwork, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, dry weather
- **Better with**: Rest and calm, Cool, quiet environment, Yin-nourishing foods (pear, black sesame), Staying well hydrated, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Kidney and Liver Yin are deeply depleted over time, they can no longer anchor the body's Yang. This creates a state of empty heat - a smoldering, low-grade fire - that rises and stirs the blood. The heat makes the blood restless and reckless, pushing it out of the vessels and into the skin, where it appears as intermittent purpura. Unlike acute heat patterns, the spots here come and go, flaring when Yin is further drained by overwork, late nights, or emotional strain.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin, and dry with little or no coating; redder sides and possible cracks. Pulse: fine and rapid, wiry at the left Guan position, weak at the left Chi.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is rooted in Yin deficiency generating empty heat. Anything that further consumes Yin - like staying up late, overworking, or emotional frustration - fans the inner fire and makes the blood more restless, triggering purpura. Spicy foods, alcohol, and hot environments add external heat to an already overheated system. Rest, cooling foods, and hydration replenish Yin and help the body anchor its Yang, calming the blood and allowing the vessels to hold. Gentle movement keeps Qi flowing without draining Yin, while stillness in a cool space gives the body a chance to rebuild its deep reserves.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize deep rest and a regular sleep schedule - being in bed before 11 p.m. is especially protective of Yin. Eat cooling, moistening foods such as pear, black sesame, tofu, and lightly cooked leafy greens. Avoid spicy, fried, or grilled foods, alcohol, and coffee, all of which add heat and consume Yin. Gentle practices like restorative yoga, tai chi, or quiet walking help settle the mind and preserve Yin without overheating the body.

### Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-lower-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark purplish spots, often on lower limbs, Blood in urine or stool, Heavy, dragging sensation in the legs, Foul-smelling, sticky stools or diarrhoea, Yellow, greasy tongue coating thickest at the back
- **Worse with**: Hot, humid weather, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overexertion or overwork
- **Better with**: Cool, dry environment, Light, bland diet, Staying well hydrated, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat sink to the lower body, where they obstruct the flow of Qi and blood. The trapped Heat scorches the blood vessels, making them fragile, while the Dampness creates stagnation that forces blood to seep out under the skin. This produces the dark, purplish spots, often alongside urinary bleeding and a heavy, dragging sensation in the legs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, slightly swollen, with a yellow greasy coating that is thickest at the root. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò), often soggy (濡 rú), more pronounced at the rear position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat thrives in humid, hot conditions and on a diet that is heavy or greasy, which adds more Dampness and Heat to the body. A light, cooling diet and a dry environment drain away the excess, while gentle movement helps Qi move the Dampness out. Hydration supports urination, which is the natural exit route for Damp-Heat in the lower burner.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and overly sweet foods, as well as alcohol and dairy, which all generate Dampness and Heat. Eat cooling, light foods like barley, mung beans, cucumber, and bitter gourd. Drink plenty of water to help flush the urinary system. Keep the living environment dry and avoid prolonged sitting or standing in humid conditions.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull or dark red purpura, Fatigue and exhaustion, Poor appetite and loose stools, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and white greasy coating
- **Worse with**: Greasy, sweet, or raw foods, Damp or rainy weather, Overexertion or overwork, Overeating large meals
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement or walking, Dry, warm climate, Rest and calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform and transport fluids properly, allowing Dampness to accumulate. This Dampness clogs the channels and further impairs the Spleen's ability to hold blood within the vessels. As a result, blood seeps out under the skin, creating dull, dark purpura that often appears together with fatigue, heaviness, and poor digestion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), soft and lacking force.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, sweet, and raw foods create Dampness and further weaken the Spleen, while overeating overwhelms its digestive capacity, making purpura worse. Damp weather adds external Dampness to an already waterlogged system. Warmth, rest, and gentle movement support Spleen function, help transform Dampness, and restore the Spleen's grip on blood.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, greasy, and sweet foods that create Dampness. Eat smaller meals and chew thoroughly. Gentle daily walking helps Qi move and Dampness disperse.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does TCM stop the bleeding under the skin?
TCM uses herbs and acupuncture to address the specific cause of the bleeding. If Heat is making the blood reckless, we cool the blood and clear Heat. If the Spleen is too weak to hold blood, we strengthen Qi. The formula is chosen to match your pattern, and it works by restoring the body's normal control over the blood vessels.

### Can I take Chinese herbs while I'm on steroids or other medications?
Yes, in many cases TCM can be safely combined with conventional treatment, but it must be done under the guidance of both your doctor and your TCM practitioner. Some herbs that cool the blood or move blood can interact with anticoagulants or affect platelet function. Never stop or reduce your prescribed medication on your own - if the herbs are working, your doctor can supervise a gradual taper.

### Will the spots come back after I stop the herbs?
The goal of TCM is to correct the underlying imbalance, not just suppress the spots. When treatment is complete - meaning your tongue, pulse, and energy have returned to normal - the tendency to develop purpura should be greatly reduced. Some patients may need a maintenance formula or seasonal tune-ups if they have a constitutional weakness, but relapse is not the norm when the root is properly treated.

### Is TCM safe for children with purpura?
Yes, TCM is commonly used for children with Henoch-Schönlein purpura and other types of purpura. The herbal dosages and acupuncture techniques are adjusted for a child's age and constitution. Pediatric purpura often involves Damp-Heat or Wind-Heat patterns, which respond well to gentle, cooling herbs. Always work with a practitioner experienced in treating children.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays an important supporting role. In general, you'll want to avoid spicy, greasy, and deep-fried foods that create Heat in the body, as well as alcohol. Focus on cooling, easy-to-digest foods like pears, cucumbers, and mung beans. If your pattern is one of Qi deficiency, warm, nourishing foods like congee and stews are helpful. Your practitioner will give you specific guidance based on your pattern.

### Can acupuncture help with purpura directly on the spots?
Acupuncture points are chosen on the body's meridians, not directly on the purpura spots themselves. The needles help regulate blood flow, cool Heat, or strengthen Qi depending on your pattern. Many patients find that acupuncture sessions reduce the frequency and intensity of new outbreaks and improve their overall energy.
