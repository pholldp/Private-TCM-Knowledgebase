## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Scrofula is tuberculosis of the lymph nodes, most often affecting the cervical nodes in the neck. It is caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis and typically presents as a firm, painless swelling that may become matted and eventually form a draining sinus. Diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy, culture, or molecular testing of the affected node, along with chest imaging to check for pulmonary involvement.
While less common today, it remains a significant health concern in many parts of the world, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Standard medical treatment is a prolonged course of multiple antibiotics, and surgery may be needed to drain abscesses or remove persistently enlarged nodes.

### Treatments
The cornerstone of conventional treatment is a multi-drug antibiotic regimen - typically isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide - taken for six to nine months. If the lymph node forms a large abscess, needle aspiration or surgical drainage may be performed to relieve pressure and obtain a sample for testing. In rare cases where nodes remain stubbornly enlarged or cause compression, surgical excision is considered.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands scrofula primarily as a disorder of Phlegm and Heat. Emotional stress, frustration, or a diet rich in greasy, sweet foods can cause the Liver's Qi to stagnate. Over time, this stagnation generates Heat, and the body's fluids, improperly transformed, condense into Phlegm. When Phlegm and Heat combine, they become a sticky, toxic substance that follows the channels upward and lodges in the neck, forming the firm, bead-like nodules characteristic of scrofula. This is the classic Phlegm-Heat pattern, often seen with a red tongue and a slippery, rapid pulse.
But the Spleen is equally important. If Damp-Heat invades the Spleen, its ability to transform and transport fluids breaks down. Dampness accumulates, thickens into Phlegm, and congeals in the lymph nodes, sometimes causing them to ooze or feel heavy. A person with this pattern will often have digestive complaints - bloating, poor appetite, sticky stools - alongside a greasy tongue coating.
In chronic cases, the root is often a deep depletion of Kidney Yin. Long illness, overwork, or emotional strain can dry up the body's cooling, nourishing reserves, allowing Empty-Heat to flare upward. This heat condenses fluids into Phlegm, but the nodules tend to be painless or only mildly tender, accompanied by night sweats, hot flushes, and a red, peeled tongue. This pattern explains why some scrofula patients feel hot and exhausted at the same time. A less common but important pattern is Qi and Blood Deficiency, where the body simply lacks the strength to clear metabolic waste, leading to small, hard, recurrent lumps with a sallow complexion and deep fatigue. And when Phlegm-Heat intensifies, it can transform into Toxic-Heat, causing the nodes to become acutely inflamed, red, hot, and throbbing - a sign that the body is in a more aggressive struggle.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first examines the nodules themselves - their size, shape, hardness, and whether they are tender or painless. Firm, movable lumps that feel like a string of beads and cause little pain often point toward a Phlegm-Heat pattern, especially when the tongue is red with a greasy yellow coating and the pulse feels rapid and slippery.
If the swellings are accompanied by digestive complaints - a heavy feeling in the stomach, poor appetite, and sticky bowel movements - the practitioner suspects Damp-Heat invading the Spleen. Here the tongue may be red or just slightly pink with a greasy yellow coat, and the pulse tends to be rapid and slippery, reflecting internal dampness as much as heat.
When the nodules have been present for a long time and are painless or only mildly tender, the practitioner looks for signs of Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty-Heat Blazing. Night sweats, hot flushes in the palms and soles, a dry mouth, and a tongue that is red with very little coating all suggest that yin is depleted and deficiency fire is stirring.
A person who develops small, hard, recurrent lumps alongside a sallow complexion, persistent fatigue, and a pale tongue with a thin, weak pulse fits the picture of Qi and Blood Deficiency. Here the body lacks the resources to clear the stagnation, so nodules linger and easily return, without the redness or heat seen in other patterns.
In a more acute flare-up, the lumps become red, hot, and intensely painful, sometimes progressing to pus formation. This signals that heat has intensified into Toxic-Heat. The tongue is red with a dry yellow coating and the pulse is rapid, marking a shift from chronic stagnation to an active inflammatory process that demands urgent cooling and detoxifying care.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Phlegm-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Firm, movable, bead-like lumps in the neck, Red tongue with a thick, yellow, greasy coating, Rapid, slippery pulse, Feeling of heat, thirst for cool drinks, Restlessness and irritability
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or dairy foods, Spicy, heating foods and alcohol, Emotional stress, Hot, humid weather
- **Better with**: Cool, light meals, Gentle movement, Emotional calm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress, frustration, or dietary excess-especially rich, greasy foods-can disrupt the smooth flow of Qi. This stagnation allows fluids to collect and thicken into Phlegm. When Phlegm combines with internal Heat, it becomes a sticky, toxic substance that congeals along the channels, most often forming the firm, bead-like lumps in the neck known as scrofula.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen, with a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑, hua) and rapid (数, shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Heat forms when Qi stagnation and dietary excess create dampness that thickens and heats up. Greasy and spicy foods directly feed the Phlegm and Heat, while emotional stress knots the Qi, preventing the body from clearing the congestion. Cooling, light foods and calm movement help disperse Qi and drain the sticky Phlegm-Heat, allowing the nodules to soften and resolve.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and dairy-rich foods that create more Phlegm. Skip spicy, heating foods and alcohol that fan the flames of Heat. Instead, eat cooling, mucus-clearing foods like radish, pear, and seaweed. Gentle exercise like walking helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation.

### Damp-Heat invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Epigastric fullness and bloating, Poor appetite with nausea, Loose, sticky stools, Heavy sensation in the body and limbs, Swollen nodules that may ooze sticky fluid, Thirst with little desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Heavy, greasy foods, Dairy and cheese, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Hot, humid weather, Overwork and fatigue, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Light, warm meals, Bitter greens (e.g., dandelion), Gentle movement, Dry, ventilated environment, Small, frequent meals, Rest after eating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming and transporting fluids. When damp-heat invades and weakens the Spleen, this function breaks down, allowing dampness to accumulate and thicken into phlegm. This phlegm-damp can then congeal in the neck, forming the swollen, sometimes oozing nodules of scrofula. The underlying Spleen disharmony also produces the telltale digestive signs like epigastric fullness, poor appetite, and sticky stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating thickest at the centre and root, may be swollen with teeth marks. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), or slippery (Hua) and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness and heat are produced by improper diet and environment. Heavy, greasy, and cold foods overwhelm the Spleen, creating more dampness. Humid weather adds external dampness, while stress and overwork weaken the Spleen further. Light, warm meals and gentle movement support the Spleen's function, helping to transform and transport fluids. Bitter foods directly clear heat and dry dampness, reducing the internal conditions that form phlegm nodules.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid heavy, greasy, sweet, and raw-cold foods that create dampness. Eat warm, light meals like congee with barley (Yi Yi Ren) and a pinch of ginger. Gentle daily movement like walking helps the Spleen move Qi and drain dampness. Stay in a dry, well-ventilated environment and avoid excessive humidity.

### Kidney Yin Deficiency With Empty-Heat Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-yin-deficiency-with-empty-heat-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Painless or only mildly painful neck lumps, Night sweats and five-palm heat, Malar flush (red cheekbones), Low-grade afternoon or evening fever, Mental restlessness and irritability, Dry throat at night, thirst for small sips, Lower back soreness and weakness
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Emotional stress, Spicy, fried, or heating foods, Alcohol and coffee, Excessive sexual activity
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Cool, quiet environment, Moistening, cooling foods, Gentle movement, Acupuncture and moxibustion
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a long illness, overwork, or emotional strain depletes the body's deep Yin reserves, the cooling, nourishing foundation of the Kidneys runs dry. This allows empty heat to flare upward, much like a kettle boiling dry. The heat condenses normal body fluids into phlegm, which then accumulates in the neck as painless or only mildly tender lumps. Because the root is a deficiency, the nodes tend to develop slowly and persist, flaring when Yin is further taxed.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin, dry body with little or no coating, possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò), may feel floating and empty; Chi (rear) position weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Yin is like the body's cooling reserve. Overwork, late nights, and stress drain this reserve, causing empty heat to flare upward and congeal fluids into nodules. Rest and cooling habits replenish Yin, while spicy and heating foods add more heat. Gentle movement supports Qi flow without depleting resources.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid staying up late. Eat cooling, moistening foods like pear, black sesame, goji berries, and bone broths. Avoid spicy, fried, and overly heating foods. Gentle exercise like Tai Chi or walking supports Qi flow without draining Yin.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Small, hard, recurrent nodules that are not red or hot, Pale or sallow yellowish complexion, Persistent fatigue and lack of energy, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Poor appetite, Heart palpitations
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Emotional stress, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Skipping meals
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Warm, nourishing meals, Gentle movement, Consistent daily routine
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body is chronically depleted of Qi and Blood, it loses the strength to fully process and clear out metabolic waste. This stagnation allows phlegm and static blood to accumulate in the channels, especially in the neck, where they slowly harden into small, bead-like lumps. Because the root deficiency is never corrected, the nodules tend to recur even after they temporarily shrink.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or show teeth marks on the edges, with a thin white coating. Pulse: fine and weak (细弱, xì ruò), lacking force.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is driven by a deep deficit of Qi and Blood, which are the body's raw materials for healing and clearing blockages. Rest and nourishing food directly replenish these resources, while overwork and stress burn through them, leaving nothing left to dissolve the phlegm and stasis that form the lumps. Cold and raw foods further weaken the Spleen's ability to produce Qi and Blood, making the cycle harder to break.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, bone broth, and steamed greens. Add small amounts of iron-rich foods such as dark leafy greens and a little red meat if appropriate. Prioritize rest and avoid pushing through fatigue; gentle movement like walking or Tai Chi is enough until energy returns.

### Toxic-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/toxic-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Red, hot, painful swollen lymph nodes, May suppurate and form pus, High fever or burning sensation, Intense thirst with desire for cold drinks, Sore, swollen throat
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Emotional stress, Overwork and fatigue, Hot, humid weather
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and drinks, Adequate rest and sleep, Cool compresses on the neck
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In lymphatic tuberculosis, when phlegm-heat lingers and intensifies, it can transform into a more aggressive toxic-heat. This toxic-heat scorches the local lymph channels, causing the nodules to become acutely inflamed, red, hot, and throbbing with pain. If the heat continues to brew, it may liquefy the tissues and form pus, leading to suppuration and potential rupture.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with prickly thorns, dry yellow coating. Pulse: rapid (数 shuò) and forceful, often slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Toxic-heat is an intense, aggressive hot pathogen. Anything that adds heat to the body - like spicy foods, anger, or overwork - fans the flames, worsening inflammation and pain. Cooling foods, rest, and cold applications help drain the heat and soothe the tissues, allowing the body to resolve the swelling and prevent pus formation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid all spicy, greasy, and fried foods, which add heat. Drink plenty of water and herbal teas like chrysanthemum or dandelion. Eat cooling foods such as mung beans, cucumber, and watermelon. Rest is essential to allow the body's defenses to fight the infection.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM cure scrofula without antibiotics?
TCM can significantly reduce the size and tenderness of the lymph nodes and strengthen the body's resistance, but scrofula is a tuberculous infection that requires antibiotic therapy to eradicate the bacteria. TCM is best used as a complementary treatment alongside conventional TB drugs - never in place of them. The herbs and acupuncture help manage symptoms, speed healing, and support the body during the long antibiotic course.

### How long until the neck lumps disappear?
Most people notice that the lumps begin to soften and feel less tender within a few weeks of starting herbs and acupuncture. Visible shrinkage often takes 1-2 months for excess patterns like Phlegm-Heat, and longer - up to 3-6 months - for deficiency patterns. Complete resolution depends on the depth of the imbalance and how consistently you follow the treatment plan.

### Is it safe to combine TCM herbs with my TB medication?
Yes, in most cases TCM herbs can be safely combined with anti-tuberculosis drugs, and they may even help reduce side effects like nausea, fatigue, or liver stress. However, you must inform both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor about everything you are taking. Some herbs that clear Heat can influence liver enzyme levels, so your doctor may want to monitor your liver function more closely. Never stop or adjust your TB medication without your doctor's approval.

### What foods should I avoid if I have scrofula?
Foods that are greasy, deep-fried, very spicy, or excessively sweet encourage the production of Phlegm and Heat, which can worsen the nodules. Dairy products, rich meats, and alcohol can also be problematic. Instead, favour light, cooling foods like pear, radish, cucumber, seaweed, and bitter greens. Warm, easily digested meals support the Spleen and help clear Dampness.

### Can children get scrofula, and is TCM safe for them?
Children can develop lymph node tuberculosis, and TCM can be a gentle, supportive therapy for them. Herbal formulas are adjusted for age and weight, and acupuncture may be replaced with acupressure or very shallow needling. Always work with a pediatric TCM specialist and coordinate with the child's medical team to ensure antibiotics are taken as prescribed.

### Will the lumps come back after treatment?
If the underlying constitutional imbalance is fully corrected, recurrence is less likely. TCM aims to not only resolve the current nodules but also strengthen the body's Qi, clear lingering Phlegm and Dampness, and nourish any deficiencies. A healthy diet, stress management, and adequate rest after treatment help maintain the results.

### Does acupuncture on the neck hurt?
Acupuncture needles are extremely thin, and most people feel only a slight sensation - not pain - when they are inserted. Points on the neck are needled with great care, and the area around the lumps is usually treated with surrounding needles rather than directly puncturing the nodule. Many patients find the sessions deeply relaxing.
