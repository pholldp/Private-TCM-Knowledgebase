## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Hyperthyroidism occurs when the thyroid gland produces too much thyroid hormone, speeding up the body's metabolism. Common symptoms include unexplained weight loss, rapid or irregular heartbeat, sweating, heat intolerance, nervousness, tremor, and sometimes a visible swelling at the front of the neck called a goiter. The most common cause is Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition, but other causes include thyroid nodules or inflammation.
Diagnosis is usually confirmed with blood tests measuring thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which is typically low in hyperthyroidism. Conventional treatment aims to reduce hormone production or block its effects.

### Treatments
Standard treatments include antithyroid medications like methimazole or propylthiouracil (PTU) that slow hormone production, radioactive iodine therapy that destroys overactive thyroid cells, and beta-blockers to manage rapid heart rate and tremors. In some cases, surgery to remove part or all of the thyroid gland is recommended. Each option carries its own benefits and risks, and the choice depends on the cause, severity, and patient preference.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, hyperthyroidism is understood as a form of "goiter qi" (瘿气, yǐng qì) that begins with a disturbance in the Liver. The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body, and when emotional stress, frustration, or anger block this flow, Qi stagnates - often felt as a lump in the throat, chest tightness, or sighing. Over time, stagnant Qi fails to manage body fluids, which condense into a sticky Phlegm that can lodge in the neck, forming a soft goiter. This is the Qi-Phlegm pattern, often the earliest stage.
If the stagnation persists, the pent-up Qi can generate Heat and eventually blaze into Liver Fire. This Fire surges upward, causing the classic hyperthyroid symptoms of irritability, heat intolerance, sweating, a red face, and a racing pulse. The eyes may bulge as the Fire pushes upward. This pattern is more intense and acute, and it's what many people picture when they think of a "thyroid storm" - though in TCM, it's a distinct treatable stage.
When the Fire burns for too long, or when overwork and stress drain the body's reserves, the cooling, nourishing Yin of the Liver, Heart, and eventually the Kidneys becomes depleted. Without enough Yin to anchor the mind and body, symptoms shift toward restlessness, palpitations, insomnia, night sweats, and a deep, exhausting heat. The goiter may still be present, but the feeling is more one of emptiness and overstimulation rather than pure excess.
This is why the same Western diagnosis can present as an angry, fiery person or a thin, anxious, depleted one - TCM distinguishes these as different patterns with different treatments.
The Heart and Kidneys are deeply involved because the Heart houses the mind (Shen), and the Kidneys store the body's foundational Yin. When Liver Fire disturbs the Heart, anxiety and palpitations dominate; when Kidney Yin is consumed, lower back soreness, tinnitus, and profound fatigue appear. So a single diagnosis of hyperthyroidism can unfold along a spectrum from stagnation to fire to deficiency, and a TCM practitioner identifies where you are on that spectrum to choose the right herbs and points.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner typically begins by asking about the emotional landscape and the physical sensations in the neck and chest. Hyperthyroidism (瘿气, yǐng qì) almost always starts with a disruption in the Liver’s ability to keep Qi flowing smoothly, so questions about stress, frustration, and sighing are the first clues that point toward the underlying pattern.
If the neck swelling feels soft and mobile, and the person sighs often with a sensation of something stuck in the throat, the picture suggests Qi-Phlegm. The tongue may look slightly puffy with a thin white or slightly greasy coating, and the pulse feels wiry and slippery. This pattern is common early on, when Qi stagnation has started to condense fluids into phlegm but heat has not yet taken hold.
When irritability, a feeling of heat, sweating, and a racing pulse dominate, the diagnosis shifts toward Liver Fire Blazing. Here the tongue is red with a thin yellow coat, and the pulse is wiry and rapid. The person may also have protruding eyes and a bitter taste in the mouth. The practitioner will ask about anger and impatience to confirm that stagnation has transformed into fire.
If the person complains of dizziness, dry eyes, and fine tremors, and the pulse feels wiry, thin, and rapid, Liver Yin Deficiency is likely. The tongue often appears red with little coating. The fire has now damaged the Liver’s cooling, nourishing fluids, and the practitioner will check for accompanying signs like blurred vision or a dull headache to distinguish it from pure heat patterns.
When palpitations, insomnia, and a restless anxiety are the main complaints, the focus is on Heart Yin Deficiency. The tongue tip is especially red and the coat is scanty. This pattern often develops alongside Liver Yin Deficiency, because the Liver and Heart share a close relationship. The practitioner will ask about night sweats and dream-disturbed sleep to confirm that the mind is not being properly anchored by Yin.
In longer-standing cases, the practitioner will look for signs that the Yin deficiency has deepened into the Kidneys. Soreness in the low back and knees, tinnitus, and night sweats point to Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is fine and rapid. This pattern reflects a more profound depletion of the body’s foundational reserves, and the symptoms tend to be less fiery and more about weakness and dryness.
A simpler pattern of Liver Qi Stagnation may be present on its own before phlegm or heat develop. The person feels distension in the chest and rib sides, sighs frequently, and has a wiry pulse, but the tongue may still look relatively normal. The practitioner will ask whether the neck swelling is absent or minimal, and whether heat signs are missing, to separate this early picture from the more advanced patterns.

### Qi-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Soft, mobile neck swelling, Sensation of a lump in the throat (globus), Chest tightness and frequent sighing, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress, Moodiness and irritability
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Greasy, heavy, or sweet foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold, raw foods
- **Better with**: Stress relief and relaxation, Light, non-greasy meals, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi), Deep breathing and sighing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress and frustration cause the Liver Qi to stagnate. When Qi cannot move freely, it fails to manage body fluids, which then condense into a sticky phlegm. This phlegm rises and lodges in the neck, forming a soft, mobile goiter-the hallmark of early hyperthyroidism. The sensation of a lump in the throat, chest tightness, and frequent sighing are all signs of this Qi and phlegm blockage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal pink-red body, possibly slightly swollen, with a white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly tightens the Liver Qi, creating more stagnation and phlegm. Greasy and sweet foods generate dampness that thickens into phlegm, while cold raw foods impair digestion and fluid metabolism. Relaxation, light diet, and gentle movement all help to smooth the Liver Qi and prevent phlegm from accumulating.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid heavy, greasy, and sweet foods that create dampness and phlegm. Eat light, cooked meals with plenty of vegetables. Regular gentle exercise like walking or yoga helps keep Qi moving. Practice stress management through meditation or deep breathing, as emotional tension directly worsens this pattern.

### Liver Fire Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Throbbing headache at the temples or crown, Red, burning or painful eyes, Bitter taste in the mouth and dry throat, Intense irritability and short temper, Sensation of heat, easy sweating, Protruding eyes (exophthalmos)
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Spicy, fried, or heating foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overwork and lack of sleep
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and drinks, Rest and quiet environment, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress, anger, or frustration persist, Liver Qi stagnates and eventually turns into Fire. This Fire surges upward, carrying heat to the head, eyes, and neck. In hyperthyroidism, the rising Liver Fire accelerates the body’s metabolism, producing the classic symptoms of heat, sweating, rapid pulse, and irritability, while also contributing to thyroid swelling and protruding eyes.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with noticeably redder sides, a dry yellow coating, and possibly small red prickles. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and rapid, full and forceful, especially at the left middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly fuels Liver Qi stagnation and its transformation into Fire, so calming the mind and reducing anger is essential. Spicy foods, alcohol, and coffee introduce additional heat into the body, worsening the upward flare of Liver Fire. Cooling foods, rest, and gentle movement help clear heat and soothe the Liver, reducing symptoms like irritability, headache, and sweating.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add heat. Eat cooling, bitter vegetables like celery, cucumber, and dandelion greens. Sip chrysanthemum tea and practice deep breathing or gentle movement like tai chi to keep anger and stress in check.

### Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness and light-headedness, Dry, gritty eyes or blurred vision, Night sweats, Heat in the palms, soles, and chest, Fine tremors in the hands, Irritability and restlessness
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Spicy, fried, or heating foods, Overwork and lack of sleep, Hot weather or heat exposure
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and regular sleep, Cool environment, Moistening foods (pears, congee), Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress, overwork, or prolonged Liver Fire gradually consumes the Liver's cooling Yin fluids, the body loses its ability to anchor and calm the Liver. This allows empty heat to rise, producing the restless tremors, irritability, and sensation of heat that are so common in hyperthyroidism. The neck swelling is often present but softer than in earlier phlegm-stasis patterns, because the root issue is now a deficiency rather than a blockage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with redder sides, dry surface, little or no coating. Pulse: wiry, fine, and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger directly consume Liver Yin, while spicy and hot foods generate internal heat that further depletes the body's cooling fluids. Overwork and insufficient sleep exhaust Yin, leaving the Liver unanchored and prone to empty heat. Rest, cooling foods, and gentle movement preserve and slowly rebuild Yin, allowing the Liver to settle and the tremors and heat to subside.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid pushing through fatigue. Eat moistening, cooling foods like pears, tofu, millet, and black sesame. Strictly reduce spicy, fried, and caffeinated items that fan the flames of empty heat. Gentle, flowing movement such as tai chi or restorative yoga helps preserve Yin without overheating the body.

### Heart Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations, worse at rest or at night, Insomnia with vivid or disturbing dreams, Mental restlessness and anxiety, Night sweats, Heat in the palms, soles, and chest
- **Worse with**: Anxiety and overthinking, Spicy, fried, or heating foods, Overwork and lack of sleep, Hot weather or heat exposure
- **Better with**: Rest and quiet environment, Cool environment, Moistening foods (pears, congee), Avoiding overstimulation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In hyperthyroidism, prolonged Liver Qi stagnation and heat often consume the body's Yin, and the Liver Yin deficiency easily spreads to the Heart. The Heart houses the mind (Shen), and when its Yin becomes depleted, the mind loses its anchor. This leads to palpitations, anxiety, and a restless, overactive mind, especially at night when Yin should be dominant.
The empty-heat that arises from Yin deficiency rises to disturb the Heart, causing night sweats, a sensation of heat in the chest, and a rapid pulse. Because the Heart's Yin is insufficient to nourish the spirit, sleep becomes shallow and filled with vivid, disturbing dreams, and the person may feel easily startled or frightened.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially at the tip, with little or no coating and a dry surface; a midline crack may be present. Pulse: fine (thin) and rapid, weak at the deep level, particularly at the left cun (Heart) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Anxiety and overthinking consume Heart Yin and stir up empty-heat, so calming the mind and resting are essential. Spicy and fried foods generate internal heat that further depletes Yin fluids, while cooling, moistening foods help replenish them. Late nights and overwork drain Yin directly, so adequate rest and a regular sleep schedule are crucial for recovery.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat cooling, moistening foods like pears, lily bulb, black sesame, and goji berries. Avoid spicy, fried, and overly stimulating foods that create internal heat. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork, especially late nights, as these drain Yin. Gentle activities like walking or restorative yoga can help, but avoid intense exercise that generates heat.

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distension or bloating along the ribs and flanks, Chest tightness and frequent sighing, Irritability, mood swings, or emotional depression, Sensation of a lump in the throat (globus)
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Sedentary lifestyle, Alcohol, Greasy, heavy, or sweet foods
- **Better with**: Stress relief and relaxation, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi), Warm peppermint tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress, frustration, and unexpressed anger directly impair the Liver's role in keeping Qi flowing smoothly. When Liver Qi stagnates, the free flow of energy through the chest and throat becomes blocked, creating the sensation of a lump in the throat and distension under the ribs. This stuck energy can then disrupt fluid metabolism and generate heat, setting the stage for the more obvious phlegm and fire patterns that drive classic hyperthyroid symptoms.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body; red or slightly purple sides; thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a guitar string, especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly constrains Liver Qi, which is the very mechanism of this pattern, so calming and relaxing habits ease it. Gentle movement and aromatic, warming foods like peppermint help disperse stagnant Qi. Alcohol and greasy foods burden the Liver and worsen stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Engage in regular gentle movement like walking, yoga, or tai chi to keep Qi flowing. Avoid alcohol, spicy, and greasy foods that can aggravate stagnation. Practice stress management such as meditation or journaling. Sip warm peppermint tea and include plenty of green vegetables in your diet.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture and Chinese herbs really help with hyperthyroidism?
Yes. TCM doesn't replace thyroid hormone directly but works to calm the underlying patterns that drive the overactivity. Many patients find that herbs and acupuncture reduce symptoms like palpitations, anxiety, heat intolerance, and tremor, and some are able to lower their medication dose over time under their doctor's supervision. The goiter may also soften and shrink as Phlegm and stagnation resolve.

### Is it safe to combine Chinese herbs with my antithyroid medication?
Yes, when managed by a qualified practitioner. Herbs should be used carefully alongside drugs like methimazole to avoid excessive thyroid suppression. Your TCM practitioner will select formulas that support your pattern without interfering with your medication. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your endocrinologist about everything you're taking, and never stop or adjust your medication on your own.

### Will I have to take herbs forever?
Not usually. The goal is to rebalance your system so that it maintains stability on its own. Once symptoms have resolved and your pattern has shifted, your practitioner will taper the herbs and eventually stop them. Some people with chronic Yin deficiency may benefit from a maintenance formula during stressful periods, but long-term daily herbs are rarely needed.

### What foods should I avoid if I have hyperthyroidism?
In general, favor cooling, moistening foods like pears, apples, cucumber, and mung beans, and avoid spicy, greasy, or fried foods, alcohol, and excessive caffeine, which can aggravate Heat. While seaweed (kelp) is used in specific TCM formulas for goiter, dietary excess of high-iodine foods may worsen hyperthyroidism, so use them only under guidance. Light cooking methods like steaming and boiling are best.

### How long until I feel better?
Most people notice a shift within 2-4 weeks: better sleep, less irritability, and a calmer heart. Physical symptoms like tremor and heat sensitivity often improve gradually over 4-8 weeks. Goiter reduction can take longer, sometimes 3-6 months, depending on its size and how long it has been present. Yin deficiency patterns are slower to rebuild, but steady progress is common.

### Can TCM help with the eye bulging (Graves' ophthalmopathy)?
Yes, especially when the bulging is related to Liver Fire or Phlegm-Heat rising to the eyes. Acupuncture points around the eyes and on the Liver and Gallbladder channels, combined with herbs that clear Heat and resolve Phlegm, can reduce swelling and discomfort. Severe or rapidly worsening eye symptoms should be evaluated by an ophthalmologist, but TCM can be a valuable supportive therapy.

### Will my symptoms come back after treatment?
If the underlying pattern has been fully resolved and you maintain a balanced lifestyle, recurrence is less likely. However, major emotional stress, overwork, or poor diet can trigger a relapse, just as they can trigger a flare of any chronic condition. Your practitioner will teach you to recognize early signs and may provide a simple herbal formula or dietary tips to use at the first hint of imbalance.
