## Preview Summary
Not all gastritis is the same. The stress-triggered pain that radiates to your ribs, the burning after spicy food, and the dull ache that improves with a hot water bottle are three...

## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining, which can be acute or chronic. It may cause burning pain in the upper abdomen, nausea, bloating, and a feeling of fullness. Common causes include infection with Helicobacter pylori, regular use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), excessive alcohol, and severe stress. Diagnosis is usually made through endoscopy with biopsy, which can identify H. pylori and assess the degree of damage.

### Treatments
Standard treatment depends on the cause. For H. pylori-related gastritis, a combination of antibiotics and acid-suppressing drugs (proton pump inhibitors or PPIs) is used. For NSAID-induced gastritis, stopping the offending medication and taking PPIs or H2 blockers is typical. Antacids may provide quick symptom relief. Dietary changes, such as avoiding spicy or acidic foods, are also recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Stomach is the 'sea of grain and water' - it receives food and drink, ripens them with its yang fire, and sends the purified essence upward to the Spleen while pushing waste downward. For this to work smoothly, Stomach Qi must descend. Pain arises when something disrupts that downward flow, whether an external invader like Cold, an internal buildup like Damp-Heat, or a weakness in the Stomach's own energy.
The Liver plays a crucial role because it governs the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. Emotional stress, frustration, or anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate and then 'invade' the Stomach, reversing its normal downward direction and causing pain, belching, and distension that radiates to the ribs. This is why stress is such a common trigger for gastritis flares.
The Spleen is the Stomach's partner in digestion, transforming food into Qi and Blood. When the Spleen is weak - often from irregular eating or too many cold, raw foods - it fails to manage fluids, and dampness accumulates. This dampness can combine with heat from rich or spicy foods to create Damp-Heat, a sticky, hot condition that clogs the Stomach and causes a heavy, burning pain with nausea.
Not all gastritis is excess. Chronic gastritis often reflects a deficiency: either the warming Yang of the Stomach is depleted, leaving it cold and slow, or the cooling Yin fluids have dried up, leaving it dry and irritated. The first feels like a dull ache that loves warmth; the second like a gnawing, burning sensation with a dry mouth. These deficiency patterns take longer to develop and longer to heal.
Because one person's gastritis may be driven by Cold while another's by Damp-Heat or Liver Qi stagnation, TCM doesn't have a single 'gastritis formula.' Instead, treatment is tailored to the specific pattern. The tongue and pulse are essential tools for telling these patterns apart, which is why a professional diagnosis is so valuable.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what the pain actually feels like, what brings it on, and what makes it better or worse. The quality, location, and timing of the discomfort are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another. They then look at the tongue and feel the pulse to confirm which internal imbalance is driving the symptoms.
If the pain is sharp and cramping and clearly improves with warmth, Cold invading the Stomach is likely. This pattern often follows a sudden chill or cold drinks, and the tongue usually looks pale with a thin white coating, while the pulse feels tight and tense.
A stuffy, burning sensation in the upper abdomen, a bitter taste in the mouth, and a thick yellow greasy tongue coating point to Damp-Heat in the Stomach. This pattern is common after a period of rich, greasy, or spicy food and alcohol, and the pulse often feels rapid and slippery.
When the pain is linked to emotional stress and radiates toward the ribs, Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach is the likely picture. Frequent belching, sighing, and a feeling of distension are typical, and the tongue may look normal or have a thin white coating while the pulse feels wiry, like a guitar string.
Epigastric fullness, sour regurgitation, and belching with a foul smell after overeating suggest Food Stagnation in the Stomach. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse feels slippery. This pattern is usually acute and directly tied to a recent dietary indiscretion.
A dull, nagging ache that feels better with pressure and warmth, accompanied by fatigue and loose stools, indicates a deeper deficiency: Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold. The tongue is pale and puffy with a thin white coating, and the pulse is deep and weak, reflecting the lack of warming energy.
When the pain is more of a burning sensation, with a feeling of hunger but no real desire to eat, dry mouth, and a red tongue with little or no coating, Stomach Yin Deficiency is the pattern. The pulse is often thin and rapid, signaling that the stomach’s moistening fluids have been depleted over time.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain that radiates to the ribs, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Irritability, moodiness, or frequent sighing
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Anger or frustration, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Skipping meals, Irregular eating schedule
- **Better with**: Stress reduction, Gentle walking, Light, warm, easy-to-digest meals, Deep breathing or meditation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger build up, that flow stalls, and the stagnant Liver Qi can surge sideways to attack the Stomach. This disrupts the Stomach's ability to receive and send food downward, causing Qi to rebel upward as belching, acid reflux, and distending pain that often radiates toward the ribs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, sides may be redder, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), most pronounced at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and anger are the primary drivers of Liver Qi stagnation, which invades the Stomach. Spicy, greasy, or irregular eating further disrupts the Stomach's downward-moving Qi, compounding the problem. Warm, regular, and simple meals support the Stomach's natural rhythm, while relaxation and gentle movement help unblock the Liver and allow Qi to flow smoothly again.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Protect your Stomach from emotional stress by building short relaxation breaks into your day-deep breathing, a walk, or gentle stretching. Eat at regular times and avoid rushing through meals. Choose cooked, warm, and mildly seasoned foods; raw, cold, spicy, or greasy items can aggravate Liver Qi and upset the Stomach.

### Damp-Heat in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning, stuffy epigastric pain, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Feeling of heaviness in the body, Nausea with sticky or bitter taste in mouth, Loose, sticky stools and dark scanty urine, Thirst with little desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Damp, humid weather, Overeating, Stress and frustration
- **Better with**: Light, warm, easy-to-digest meals, Warm water or barley tea, Gentle walking, Cool, dry environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you regularly eat rich, greasy, spicy, or sweet foods and drink alcohol, it creates a sticky, hot environment in the stomach. This Damp-Heat clogs the stomach, preventing Qi from descending, leading to a stuffy, burning pain and nausea. The heat causes inflammation, while the dampness makes you feel heavy and sluggish.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with red prickles in the center; thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò), especially at the right middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat in the Stomach arises from overconsumption of rich, greasy, and spicy foods and alcohol, which create a sticky, hot obstruction. These triggers add more Dampness and Heat, worsening the burning, full sensation. Light, bland foods and barley tea help drain Dampness and clear Heat, while gentle movement keeps Qi flowing so the Stomach can descend properly. Stress can also aggravate by causing Qi to stagnate, which further traps Damp-Heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and small portions of lean protein. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which create more Damp-Heat. Drink warm water or barley tea. Gentle exercise like walking helps move Qi and dry Dampness.

### Cold invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden sharp or cramping epigastric pain, Pain improves with warmth and worsens with cold, No thirst, bland taste in mouth, Vomiting clear watery fluid, Cold sensation in the stomach area
- **Worse with**: Cold foods and icy drinks, Raw salads and chilled fruit, Cold weather or draughts, Overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Warm compress on stomach, Ginger tea, Resting in a warm place, Light, warm, easy-to-digest meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Sudden exposure to cold-like eating icy foods or getting chilled-allows an external Cold pathogen to invade the Stomach. Cold congeals Qi and Blood, blocking the Stomach's normal downward flow and causing sudden, sharp cramping pain. The pain feels better with warmth because warmth dispels the Cold and helps Qi move again.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, moist, with a thin white slippery coating. Pulse: tight (Jin), often wiry and slow.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold directly congeals Stomach Qi, causing it to seize up and spasm, which is why cold foods and weather trigger the pain. Warmth, on the other hand, disperses the Cold, relaxes the Stomach, and restores the smooth downward flow of Qi. Resting allows the body to conserve Qi to fight the pathogen, while overwork depletes Qi and makes the Stomach more vulnerable to cold invasion.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold drinks, raw foods, and icy treats entirely. Sip warm water or ginger tea throughout the day. Keep your belly covered and warm, especially in cold weather or air-conditioned rooms.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Epigastric fullness and distension that worsens after eating, Sour, rotten-smelling belching, Acid regurgitation or vomiting of undigested food, Pain worse with pressure, relieved after vomiting, Thick, greasy, curd-like tongue coating, Loose stools with a sour, foul smell
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Eating late at night, Pressure on the abdomen, Stress or rushing while eating
- **Better with**: Vomiting, Light, warm, easy-to-digest meals, Gentle walking, Fasting or skipping a meal
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you overeat or eat too quickly, the Stomach cannot process the food properly. The undigested food sits and ferments, blocking the normal downward movement of Stomach Qi. This obstruction creates a sensation of fullness, distending pain that feels worse with pressure, and the sour, rotten-smelling belching characteristic of food stagnation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy, curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑, huá) and full, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating directly creates the food stagnation that blocks Stomach Qi, so smaller portions and lighter foods prevent the overload. Vomiting forcibly expels the accumulated food, providing immediate relief, just as a short walk gently stimulates the downward movement of Qi. Pressure on the abdomen aggravates the obstruction, making the distension feel worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and chew thoroughly. Avoid greasy, fried, and sweet foods that are hard to digest. A short walk after eating can help Stomach Qi descend, but avoid lying down immediately.

### Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yang-deficient-and-cold)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, aching pain in the upper belly, Pain improves with warmth and pressure, Vomiting clear watery fluid, Loose stools, Cold hands and feet, fatigue
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Iced drinks, Cold, damp weather, Overwork and fatigue, Skipping meals
- **Better with**: Warm compress on stomach, Light, warm, easy-to-digest meals, Ginger tea, Gentle abdominal pressure, Rest after eating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the warming Yang of the Stomach is deficient, the digestive fire weakens and cold accumulates in the middle burner. This cold congeals Qi, causing a dull, persistent ache that feels better with heat and gentle pressure. The Stomach also fails to properly transform food and fluids, leading to bloating, vomiting of clear fluid, and loose stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender with teeth marks; white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, weak; especially weak at the right guan (middle) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Warmth and warm foods directly supplement the Stomach's deficient Yang, helping to dispel internal cold and ease pain. Cold foods and environments further deplete Yang, causing the Stomach to contract and pain to worsen. Rest and gentle pressure conserve Qi, while overwork and irregular eating strain the digestive system, aggravating the deficiency.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and stews, and avoid raw, cold, or iced foods and drinks. Sip ginger tea throughout the day to gently warm the Stomach. Keep your abdomen warm with a hot water bottle or a belly band. Rest after meals and avoid overexertion.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, gnawing, burning pain in the upper abdomen, Hunger but no real desire to eat, Dry mouth and throat, thirst for small sips, Dry stools or constipation, Red tongue with little or no coating, especially in the centre
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Late nights and overwork, Emotional stress, Dry, roasted foods
- **Better with**: Small, frequent, moist meals, Steamed pears or congee, Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, not hot, drinks
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Stomach relies on Yin fluids to stay cool, moist, and protected. When those fluids run low-often after chronic inflammation, irregular eating, or a draining illness-the Stomach lining loses its cushion. This leaves it dry and overheated, producing a dull, gnawing, burning ache. Because the Stomach is undernourished, you may feel hungry yet have no real desire to eat, and the lack of moisture shows up as a dry mouth, thirst for small sips, and dry stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating, possibly cracked. Pulse: fine (細 xì) and rapid (數 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin is like the cooling, moist cushion that protects the stomach lining. Spicy, fried, and drying foods act like a heat source that evaporates this cushion, making the burning pain worse. Alcohol, coffee, late nights, and emotional stress all generate internal heat and consume fluids, deepening the Yin deficit. Conversely, moist, bland, and easy-to-digest foods, along with rest, help rebuild that protective layer and calm the empty heat. Small, frequent meals prevent overloading the weakened stomach, giving it time to recover its moisture and function.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals that are moistening and easy to digest-think congee, steamed pears, tofu, and well-cooked vegetables. Avoid spicy, fried, roasted, or greasy foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which all dry out the Stomach. Sip warm water or mild herbal teas throughout the day. Prioritise rest and a regular sleep schedule; late nights and overwork consume Yin. Gentle, calming activities like walking or restorative yoga help without overheating.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### What does TCM treatment for gastritis involve?
Treatment typically includes acupuncture, a customized herbal formula, and dietary guidance. Acupuncture points on the abdomen, legs, and arms help regulate Stomach Qi and calm the Liver. Herbal formulas are taken daily, usually as a tea or granules, to address the underlying pattern. Your practitioner will also advise on foods to avoid and those that support healing.

### Can I keep taking my acid reducer while doing TCM?
Yes, TCM can be safely combined with proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers. Many patients begin TCM while still on these medications. As your symptoms improve, you can work with your prescribing doctor to slowly reduce the dose if appropriate. Never stop acid-suppressing drugs abruptly, as this can cause rebound acid production.

### How long until I feel better?
For acute gastritis, many people notice improvement within the first week of herbs and acupuncture. Chronic gastritis often takes longer - you may see gradual relief over 4-8 weeks. The timeline depends on the pattern: excess conditions like Food Stagnation clear quickly, while deficiency patterns require time to rebuild the stomach lining and digestive fire.

### Do I have to change my diet completely?
Dietary adjustments are an important part of TCM treatment, but they don't have to be extreme. The general rule is to eat warm, cooked foods, avoid raw and cold items, and steer clear of greasy, spicy, or overly sweet foods that create dampness. You'll also receive pattern-specific advice - for example, ginger tea for cold patterns or pear for dryness. Most patients find that once they feel better, they can gradually reintroduce a wider range of foods.

### Is acupuncture safe for gastritis?
Yes, acupuncture is very safe when performed by a trained professional. The needles are hair-thin and cause minimal discomfort. For gastritis, points are often chosen on the abdomen, lower legs, and wrists. Some people feel immediate relaxation and a reduction in pain after a session.

### Will the pain come back after treatment?
TCM aims to resolve the underlying imbalance, not just mask the pain. When the pattern is fully corrected and dietary habits are maintained, recurrence is less likely. However, if you return to the same triggers - chronic stress, poor diet, overwork - the condition can reappear. Your practitioner will help you identify and manage your personal triggers.
