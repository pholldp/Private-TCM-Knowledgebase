## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, upper abdominal pain is often diagnosed as gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), or functional dyspepsia. Diagnosis may involve endoscopy, imaging, and tests for H. pylori infection.
Symptoms can include burning, aching, bloating, or sharp pain, often related to meals or stress. Treatment focuses on reducing stomach acid, protecting the lining, or eradicating bacteria.

### Treatments
Standard treatments include antacids, H2 blockers, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and antibiotics for H. pylori. Dietary changes such as avoiding trigger foods and eating smaller meals are also recommended. For functional dyspepsia, prokinetic agents may be used to improve stomach emptying.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees the Stomach as the 'sea of food and water,' responsible for receiving and ripening what we eat. For it to function smoothly, its Qi must descend. Pain occurs when this downward movement is obstructed - by stress, cold, or undigested food - or when the Stomach lacks the warmth or moisture it needs to work properly. This is summed up as 'blockage causes pain' or 'malnourishment causes pain.'
The Liver plays a surprisingly central role. Emotional tension, frustration, and chronic stress cause Liver Qi to stagnate. That stuck energy then invades the Stomach, disrupting its rhythm and creating a characteristic distending pain that often spreads to the ribs, along with belching and a feeling of fullness. This pattern is extremely common in modern life.
Dietary factors are another major cause. Overeating, rich greasy food, or ice-cold drinks can directly create Food Stagnation or Cold Invasion, leading to sudden bloating or sharp cramping. Over time, a diet heavy in spicy, fried, or damp-producing foods can brew Damp-Heat in the Stomach, causing a burning pain with a bitter, sticky taste.
When the Stomach's own reserves run low, deficiency patterns emerge. Weak Yang fails to warm the digestive fire, so pain feels dull, cold, and relieved by a hot water bottle. Depleted Yin leaves the stomach lining dry and irritated, creating a gnawing, burning ache with thirst and a red tongue. These chronic patterns develop over years and require patient rebuilding.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking you to describe the pain: is it dull, burning, distending, or sharp? When does it strike - after eating, on an empty stomach, or with stress? The quality and timing of your discomfort are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the pain feels bloating and distending, radiates to the ribcage, and worsens with emotional stress, the diagnosis often leans toward Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. Belching, sighing, and a thin white tongue coating with a wiry pulse confirm that the Liver’s energy is stuck and disrupting the Stomach.
When the pain is a dull ache that feels better with warmth and gentle pressure, and worse when the stomach is empty, Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold is likely. You may feel cold, tired, and have loose stools. A pale tongue and a deep, slow pulse support this picture of internal cold and weakness.
A burning sensation with a dry mouth, thirst, and a desire for cool drinks points to Stomach Yin Deficiency. The tongue appears red with little or no coating, and the pulse feels thin and rapid. This pattern reflects a lack of nourishing fluids, leaving the stomach lining irritated and overheated.
Acute fullness, bloating, and pain after a heavy meal, along with sour regurgitation or relief after vomiting, suggests Food Stagnation in the Stomach. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery - signs that undigested food is clogging the stomach’s normal downward movement.
Sudden, severe pain triggered by exposure to cold or eating cold foods, and relieved by warmth, indicates Cold invading the Stomach. The tongue is pale with a white coating, and the pulse feels tight. This is an acute external attack that freezes the stomach’s function.
If the pain burns, you have a bitter taste, nausea, and a heavy sensation, Damp-Heat in the Stomach may be the cause. The tongue coating is thick, yellow, and greasy, and the pulse is slippery and rapid. This pattern mixes heat with fluid stagnation, creating inflammation and discomfort.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain radiating to the rib area, Worse with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Irritability and mood swings, Feeling of a lump in the throat
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Premenstrual phase
- **Better with**: Stress relief and relaxation, Gentle exercise or movement, Deep breathing or sighing, Warm compress on the abdomen, Small, light meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress and frustration build up, the Liver's smooth flow of Qi becomes stuck. This stagnant Qi then pushes sideways into the Stomach, disrupting its natural downward movement. The result is a trapped, distending pain in the upper abdomen that often spreads to the ribs, along with frequent belching and acid reflux as the Stomach rebels upward.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, may have redder sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Emotional stress directly aggravates Liver Qi stagnation, which then invades the Stomach, so calming the mind and releasing tension are the most direct ways to break the pain cycle. Gentle movement and deep breathing help Qi flow smoothly, preventing the stuck sensation. Heavy or greasy foods burden the Stomach and further obstruct Qi, while small, light meals avoid adding to the stagnation.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize stress management through meditation, deep breathing, or gentle yoga. Eat regular, light meals and avoid eating when upset. Sip warm peppermint or chamomile tea to help relax the Stomach and move Qi.

### Stomach Yang Deficient and Cold
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yang-deficient-and-cold)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, cold aching pain in the upper belly, Pain relieved by warmth, Vomiting of clear watery fluid, Cold hands and feet, sensitivity to cold, Loose stools
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Cold weather or drafts, Overwork and fatigue, Skipping meals
- **Better with**: Warm compress on the abdomen, Eating warm, cooked meals, Rest after eating, Warm drinks like ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Stomach's warming Yang becomes deficient, it can no longer generate enough heat to properly "cook" and move food. This internal cold slows Qi flow and causes the dull, aching pain that is so typical of this pattern. The pain feels cold and is naturally relieved by warmth and pressure, which temporarily restore some Yang and get Qi moving again. Because the Stomach lacks the energy to handle food efficiently, eating even small amounts can bring temporary relief, but the underlying weakness remains, and the pain often returns on an empty stomach.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks; white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, slow, weak, especially at the right Guan (middle) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods, as well as a cold environment, further drain the Stomach's already deficient Yang, leading to more severe contraction and pain. Overwork and fasting deplete the Qi and Yang that the Stomach needs to function, making the empty ache worse. Warmth, whether from food, drink, or external heat, temporarily restores the Yang and encourages Qi to move, which is why heat and eating provide such reliable relief. Rest conserves the body's limited energy, allowing it to be used for digestion and warming.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congees. Add gentle warming spices such as ginger, cinnamon, and a little black pepper. Avoid raw, cold, and icy foods and drinks. Eat small, regular meals and never skip breakfast. Keep your abdomen warm with a hot water bottle or a belly band, especially in cold weather.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull burning pain in the upper abdomen, Dry mouth and throat, thirst for small sips, Feeling hungry but not wanting to eat, Gnawing discomfort in the stomach, Dry stools or constipation
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Overwork and fatigue, Dry, hot weather, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Cool, moistening foods, Small, light meals, Rest and adequate sleep, Avoiding spicy and fried foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Stomach relies on a moist, nourishing environment to function smoothly. When its Yin-the cooling, lubricating fluids-runs low, the lining becomes dry and irritated. This generates a subtle empty heat that produces the characteristic dull, burning ache and gnawing discomfort in the upper abdomen.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating, possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin deficiency means the organ lacks cooling, lubricating fluids, so any food or habit that is heating or drying-like spicy meals, alcohol, or staying up late-further consumes those precious fluids and intensifies the empty heat that causes the burning pain. Conversely, moistening, cool-natured foods and adequate rest help restore Yin and soothe the irritated stomach lining. Emotional stress also generates internal heat and consumes Yin, so keeping a calm mind protects what little moisture remains.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on moistening, easy-to-digest foods like congee, steamed pears, and honey water. Avoid spicy, fried, or dry foods that further deplete fluids. Eat small, frequent meals rather than large portions, and limit alcohol and coffee. Gentle evening routines and adequate sleep help the body replenish Yin.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain with fullness, Worse after eating, better after vomiting, Sour, rotten-smelling belching and acid regurgitation, Thick, greasy, curd-like tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Pressure on the abdomen, Eating late at night
- **Better with**: Vomiting, Passing foul-smelling gas, Skipping a meal, Gentle abdominal massage, Warm drinks like ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you eat too much, too quickly, or consume rich, greasy foods, your Stomach cannot process the load. The undigested food sits and ferments, blocking the normal downward movement of Stomach Qi. This stagnation creates pressure, bloating, and a distending pain that feels worse with pressure and better after vomiting.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy, curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and full, especially at the right middle (Stomach) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: When you eat too much or consume heavy foods, the Stomach becomes physically overloaded. The undigested mass blocks the normal downward movement of Qi, leading to painful distension. Vomiting or passing gas clears the obstruction, allowing Qi to descend and pain to ease. Skipping a meal and gentle massage give the Stomach a chance to recover and move the stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, lighter meals and avoid overeating. Chew food thoroughly and stop eating before you feel full. Avoid greasy, fried, and rich foods until digestion recovers. Gentle walking after meals can help move Qi downward.

### Cold invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden sharp or cramping pain after consuming cold food or drink, Pain relieved by warmth, Vomiting of clear watery fluid, No thirst, preference for warm drinks
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Cold weather or drafts
- **Better with**: Warm compress on the abdomen, Warm drinks like ginger tea, Rest and staying warm, Moxibustion or heat therapy
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When cold invades the Stomach directly-from eating icy foods, drinking cold beverages, or exposure to cold weather-it constricts the Stomach's Qi and blood vessels. This sudden constriction blocks the flow and causes sharp, cramping pain that feels better with warmth. The cold also impairs the Stomach's ability to digest, leading to nausea and watery stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body with a moist, thin white slippery coating. Pulse: tight (Jin) and slow, especially in the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold directly invades the Stomach, constricting Qi and blood, causing pain. Warmth relaxes the constriction and restores flow, while cold food or environment reinforces the pathogenic Cold. Ginger and moxibustion introduce Yang energy to expel Cold.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold and raw foods and drinks. Sip warm ginger tea or cinnamon tea to warm the Stomach. Keep the abdomen warm with a scarf or hot water bottle. Eat small, warm, easily digestible meals like congee or soups.

### Damp-Heat in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning epigastric pain, Bitter or sticky taste in the mouth, Nausea or vomiting, Feeling of heaviness in the body, Thirst with little desire to drink, Dark scanty urine
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overeating, Rich, sweet foods
- **Better with**: Light, cooling meals, Rest in a cool place, Drinking plenty of water, Gentle exercise or movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat combine in the Stomach, creating a sticky obstruction that blocks the normal downward flow of Stomach Qi. This stagnation leads to a sensation of fullness, stuffiness, and burning pain in the upper abdomen. The Heat component gives the pain a burning quality, while the Dampness creates heaviness and a greasy taste.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with prickles in centre, thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑, Huá) and rapid (数, Shuò), especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat is a combination of heavy, sticky Dampness and hot, active Heat. Greasy, fried, and sweet foods directly create more Dampness and Heat, while spicy foods and alcohol add Heat, aggravating the burning pain. Light, cooling foods and adequate hydration help clear Heat and drain Dampness, while rest and a cool environment reduce internal Heat. Gentle movement prevents Qi stagnation, which helps the Stomach descend properly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on light, easily digestible meals with plenty of cooling vegetables like cucumber, celery, and bitter greens. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which all generate Dampness and Heat. Gentle exercise like walking helps move Qi and prevent stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does TCM diagnose the cause of my stomach pain?
Your practitioner will ask about the pain's quality (dull, burning, sharp, distending), timing (before or after meals, with stress), and what makes it better or worse. They'll also examine your tongue for signs like coating thickness and color, and feel your pulse for qualities like wiry, slippery, or weak. This information reveals the underlying pattern.

### Can acupuncture really help with upper abdominal pain?
Yes. Acupuncture points like Zhongwan (REN-12), Zusanli (ST-36), and Neiguan (PC-6) directly regulate Stomach Qi, ease pain, and harmonize digestion. Many patients feel relief during or after the first session, though lasting change comes with a course of treatment tailored to your specific pattern.

### How long until I feel better?
Many notice reduced pain and bloating within the first 2-4 weeks of herbal therapy and weekly acupuncture. Complete resolution depends on the pattern: acute issues like Food Stagnation clear quickly, while chronic Yin or Yang deficiency may take several months. Consistency is key.

### Can I take TCM herbs with my stomach medication?
Generally yes, but always inform both your TCM practitioner and doctor. Some herbs like Gan Cao (licorice) can interact with blood pressure medications or diuretics. Acupuncture is safe alongside most medications, but a full disclosure of your regimen ensures no unexpected interactions.

### Will I have to change my diet?
Yes, dietary guidance is a cornerstone of TCM treatment. You'll receive pattern-specific advice, but universally we recommend eating warm, cooked foods, avoiding raw, cold, and greasy items, and eating at regular times. Small, frequent meals are easier on the Stomach than large ones.

### Is TCM safe during pregnancy for stomach pain?
Acupuncture from a qualified practitioner is generally safe during pregnancy and can relieve digestive discomfort. However, certain herbs and acupuncture points are contraindicated in pregnancy. Always tell your practitioner you are pregnant so they can modify treatment accordingly.

### Will the pain come back after treatment?
If the underlying imbalance is fully corrected and you maintain healthy dietary and lifestyle habits, recurrence is unlikely. However, returning to old patterns - chronic stress, irregular eating, or a diet heavy in cold or greasy foods - can retrigger the condition. TCM aims to build resilience, not just suppress symptoms.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1155/2016/4083690))
   *Journal/Publication: Pang B, Jiang T, Du YH, et al. Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2016;2016:4083690.*
   This meta-analysis pooled data from 16 RCTs and found that acupuncture significantly improved overall symptoms of functional dyspepsia compared with sham acupuncture or conventional medication. The effect was particularly notable for epigastric pain and postprandial fullness.

2. **Chinese herbal medicine for functional dyspepsia: systematic review of randomised controlled trials** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgh.12244))
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang Y, Han M, Liu Z, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for functional dyspepsia: systematic review of randomised controlled trials. J Gastroenterol Hepatol. 2013;28(4):577-585.*
   A review of 27 RCTs evaluating various Chinese herbal formulas for functional dyspepsia. The analysis concluded that Chinese herbal medicine was more effective than placebo and comparable to prokinetic drugs in relieving symptoms including epigastric pain, with fewer side effects.

3. **Chaihu Shugan San for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis** ([Link](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000008407))
   *Journal/Publication: Wang C, Zhu M, Xia W, et al. Chaihu Shugan San for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Medicine (Baltimore). 2017;96(50):e8407.*
   This study focused specifically on Chaihu Shugan San, a key formula for Liver Qi stagnation invading the Stomach. Meta-analysis of 14 RCTs showed it significantly improved epigastric pain, belching, and rib-side distention compared with conventional prokinetics.
