## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, chronic bleeding is defined as recurrent or persistent blood loss from the gastrointestinal tract, urinary tract, uterus, or skin. Common causes include peptic ulcers, hemorrhoids, inflammatory bowel disease, menstrual disorders, and clotting abnormalities. Diagnosis typically involves blood tests, endoscopy, colonoscopy, or imaging studies to locate the source of bleeding and rule out serious conditions like cancer.

### Treatments
Treatment depends on the source and cause. Options include iron supplements to address anemia, acid-suppressing drugs for ulcers, cauterization or banding for hemorrhoids, hormonal therapies for uterine bleeding, and surgery for structural problems. Antifibrinolytic medications may be used for heavy menstruation, while clotting factor replacement is given for bleeding disorders.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, blood is held in the vessels by the Spleen and by the body's Qi. The Spleen has a special function called 'governing Blood' - it creates the Qi that keeps blood from leaking out. When the Spleen is weak from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness, it can no longer hold blood in place, and slow, pale bleeding can occur from the gut, uterus, or under the skin. A similar mechanism happens when overall Qi is depleted: the invisible net that contains blood slackens, allowing it to ooze out.
Heat is another major cause. Excess heat in the Stomach or Liver can agitate the blood, making it move recklessly and burst out of vessels. This produces bright or dark red bleeding, often with burning sensations, thirst, and a rapid pulse. Damp-Heat that settles in the lower body can cause recurrent blood in the stool or urine with a heavy, foul quality. In these cases, the bleeding is like water boiling over - the heat must be cleared to calm it.
Blood Stagnation, where circulation is blocked, also leads to chronic bleeding. When vessels are obstructed, blood can't flow smoothly and may seep out, producing dark clots and fixed, stabbing pain. This pattern is common in conditions like endometriosis or chronic pelvic congestion. TCM sees each of these mechanisms as a distinct pattern requiring its own treatment, which is why two people with the same Western diagnosis might receive completely different herbal formulas.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking where the bleeding occurs and what the blood looks like. The color, consistency, and location offer the first major clues. Pale, thin blood that oozes slowly points toward deficiency patterns, while bright or dark red blood with heat signs suggests excess heat. The accompanying symptoms - such as pain, thirst, or fatigue - help narrow the field further.
If chronic bleeding is accompanied by persistent tiredness, poor appetite, loose stools, and a pale, slightly swollen tongue, the pattern is likely Spleen not controlling Blood. The pulse will feel weak and thready, reflecting the Spleen’s inability to produce and hold blood. This pattern is especially common in prolonged bleeding from the digestive tract or uterus.
When the person feels generally drained, short of breath on mild exertion, and sweats easily without heat, but lacks the specific digestive complaints of Spleen deficiency, Qi not controlling Blood is considered. The blood is thin and light-colored, the tongue is pale, and the pulse is weak and forceless. The overall lack of holding power is the key feature.
Vomiting of bright or dark red blood with a burning sensation in the stomach, intense thirst, bad breath, and a red tongue with a thick yellow coat points to Stomach Fire. The pulse is rapid and forceful. This excess heat pattern drives blood recklessly upward, so the practitioner asks about diet, alcohol, and spicy food habits that fuel the fire.
Lower-body bleeding - blood in the stool or urine with a heavy, burning sensation, a feeling of incomplete evacuation, and a greasy yellow tongue coat - suggests Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner. The pulse feels slippery and rapid. The practitioner will inquire about urinary urgency, stool mucus, and dietary triggers to confirm this pattern.
Emotional stress preceding vomiting of blood, together with a bitter taste, rib-side distension, and a wiry, rapid pulse, indicates Liver Qi Stagnation that has transformed into Heat. The tongue may be red with a thin yellow coat. The practitioner looks for irritability and premenstrual tension as supporting signs of Liver involvement.
When the bleeding produces dark purple clots and is accompanied by fixed, stabbing pain that feels like a needle, Blood Stagnation is the underlying pattern. The tongue appears dusky purple with possible stasis spots, and the pulse may feel rough or wiry. This pattern often complicates other patterns and is suspected whenever pain is sharp and unchanging.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen not controlling Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-not-controlling-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, oozing bleeding (stool, urine, skin, or heavy periods), General fatigue and weakness, Poor appetite, Loose stools, Pale complexion, Dizziness or light-headedness
- **Worse with**: Overwork and physical exhaustion, Stress and excessive worry, Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Prolonged standing or heavy lifting
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warmth and warm drinks, Small, frequent, cooked meals, Gentle walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for keeping blood inside the vessels. When Spleen Qi is weak, it can no longer hold the blood in place, so blood leaks out slowly over time. This leads to pale, oozing bleeding from various sites, along with fatigue and poor appetite because the Spleen is too depleted to transform food into energy and blood.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: Fine and weak, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overwork and prolonged standing drain Spleen Qi, while cold, raw foods directly damage the Spleen’s warming function, both making it harder to hold blood inside the vessels. Worry and mental strain knot the Liver Qi, which then overacts on the Spleen, further weakening it. Rest, warmth, and small cooked meals all support the Spleen’s ability to transform food into Qi and blood, gradually restoring its holding power.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easy-to-digest foods like congee, soups, and stewed vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork or prolonged standing. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi circulate without depleting it.

### Qi not controlling Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-not-controlling-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chronic bleeding of pale, thin blood, Profound fatigue and shortness of breath, Reluctance to speak, Pale, puffy tongue with teeth marks, Loose stools and poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overwork and physical strain, Prolonged standing or heavy lifting, Raw, cold, or greasy foods, Emotional stress and worry, Excessive talking or singing
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Warm, easily digested meals, Gentle exercise (walking, Tai Chi), Moxibustion on lower abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi acts as an invisible net that holds blood within the vessels. When Qi becomes weak-often from chronic illness, overwork, or poor nutrition-it can no longer keep blood in place, leading to slow, persistent bleeding. The blood is typically pale and thin because Qi is also needed to produce vibrant blood. This generalized Qi deficiency also leaves you feeling profoundly tired and short of breath, with a pale complexion and tongue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy body with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: Fine, weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi is the body's energy that holds blood in place. Overwork, prolonged standing, and emotional strain all consume Qi, making it harder to contain blood. Raw and cold foods weaken the Spleen, which is the source of Qi. Rest, warmth, and nourishing foods restore Qi, while gentle movement circulates it without depletion. Moxibustion directly warms and tonifies Qi to stop bleeding.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like congee, soups, and stews with Qi-nourishing ingredients such as rice, oats, sweet potato, and a little ginger. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and gentle movement like Tai Chi; overexertion depletes Qi further.

### Stomach Fire (Stomach Heat)
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-fire-stomach-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning pain in the upper stomach, Vomiting bright red or dark red blood, Bleeding gums, Foul breath, Constant hunger, Thirst with desire for cold drinks
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Stress and anger, Hot weather, Overeating
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and drinks, Cool environment, Gentle exercise, Managing anger and stress
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When excessive heat builds up in the Stomach, it acts like a fire that agitates the blood, pushing it recklessly out of the vessels. This can cause bleeding from the gums, vomiting of bright or dark red blood, or blood in the stool. The heat also burns the Stomach lining, leading to a burning sensation, thirst, and foul breath.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, possible central prickles, thick dry yellow coat. Pulse: Rapid, slippery, and full, especially strong in the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy and greasy foods, alcohol, and coffee add heat to the Stomach, fueling the fire that drives blood recklessly out of the vessels. Stress and anger stir up Liver Fire, which can invade the Stomach and worsen the heat. Cooling foods and a calm environment help clear the excess heat, while gentle exercise promotes the smooth flow of Qi and prevents stagnation from generating more heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add heat to the Stomach. Focus on cooling, light foods such as cucumber, watermelon, mung bean soup, and steamed vegetables. Eat smaller, more frequent meals to avoid overloading the Stomach. Manage emotional stress, as anger and frustration can fuel Liver Fire that invades the Stomach.

### Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-lower-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning sensation during urination or defecation, Heavy, dragging feeling in the lower abdomen or legs, Foul-smelling, sticky stools or cloudy, dark urine, Yellow greasy tongue coating, thickest at the back, Thirst with no real desire to drink, Afternoon low-grade fever or feeling of heat
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Prolonged sitting, Emotional frustration
- **Better with**: Cool environment, Light, bland diet, Drinking plenty of water, Avoiding alcohol and spicy food, Gentle walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat combine and sink to the lower body, where they settle into the intestines or bladder. The Heat agitates the blood and damages the tiny vessels, while the Dampness makes the bleeding sticky, foul, and difficult to stop. This causes recurrent blood-streaked stools or cloudy, reddish urine, often with a dragging heaviness and a sense of incomplete emptying.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, slightly swollen, with a yellow greasy coating that is thickest at the root. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò), more pronounced at the rear (Chi) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, greasy, and fried foods directly create dampness and heat in the body, pouring fuel on the lower burner fire and making bleeding worse. A bland, cooling diet and plenty of water help drain dampness and cool the blood. Gentle movement keeps Qi and fluids circulating, preventing stagnation, while rest and a cool environment calm the heat. Emotional frustration can stir liver Qi, which transforms into heat and aggravates the damp-heat below.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and sweet foods that brew dampness and heat. Drink plenty of room-temperature water, and eat cooling, dampness-resolving foods like mung beans, celery, and barley. Keep the genital and anal area clean and dry, and avoid sitting for long periods.

### Liver Qi Stagnation that transforms into Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-that-transforms-into-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vomiting of dark red blood or blood-tinged vomit, Bitter taste in the mouth and dry throat, Distending or burning pain along the ribs, Irritability and explosive anger, Red face and eyes, headache at the temples
- **Worse with**: Anger and frustration, Alcohol and spicy food, Overwork and late nights
- **Better with**: Emotional calm and stress reduction, Cooling foods and drinks, Gentle, regular movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Longstanding emotional stress and frustration cause Liver Qi to stagnate. Over time this stagnation generates Heat, which can flare upward as Liver Fire. When Liver Fire invades the Stomach, it scorches the Stomach collaterals and forces blood to move recklessly, leading to vomiting of blood that is often dark red or contains clots.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with redder sides and a yellow coating. Pulse: wiry and rapid, especially forceful at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Anger and emotional stress directly fuel Liver Qi stagnation and Heat, which is the engine of this bleeding pattern. Cooling, calming habits soothe the Liver and clear Heat, while rest and gentle movement help Qi flow smoothly, preventing stagnation from flaring into Fire and forcing blood out of the vessels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy and greasy foods-they add Heat and fuel Liver Fire. Prioritize stress management through gentle movement like tai chi or walking, and wind down before bed. Cooling foods like chrysanthemum tea, celery, and cucumber can help settle the Liver.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark purple blood with clots, Fixed stabbing pain, Pain worse at night or with pressure, Dark or dusky complexion, Purple tongue with stasis spots
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or cold foods, Prolonged inactivity, Stress and emotional suppression, Injury or trauma
- **Better with**: Gentle movement like walking, Warmth applied to painful area, Warming spices like turmeric, Avoiding prolonged sitting
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi and Blood stagnate, the vessels become blocked and fragile. The obstructed flow forces blood to seep out of the vessels, producing chronic bleeding that is typically dark purple with clots. Because stasis is a fixed obstruction, the bleeding is often accompanied by a distinct stabbing pain that feels worse with pressure and at night.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots and distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (Se) or wiry (Xian).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold congeals and slows blood, making stasis worse; warmth and gentle movement help keep blood flowing. Emotional stress tightens the Liver Qi, which can further obstruct blood flow, so relaxation and stress management are key. Prolonged sitting or standing still allows blood to pool, while regular gentle activity promotes circulation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and add gentle blood-moving spices like turmeric. Avoid icy drinks and raw foods that congeal blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or stretching helps keep blood flowing; avoid prolonged sitting or standing still.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can Chinese herbs stop bleeding as quickly as Western medications?
For some patterns, especially those involving heat, herbs can slow or stop bleeding within days to a week. However, TCM aims to correct the root cause, so the full course of treatment takes longer than a quick fix. If you have active, heavy bleeding, always seek emergency care first; TCM is best suited for chronic, recurrent, or low-grade bleeding.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs if I'm on blood thinners?
Some herbs used for bleeding, such as San Qi (notoginseng), can affect blood clotting. If you are taking warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, or other anticoagulants, you must inform both your TCM practitioner and prescribing doctor. They can adjust your formula to avoid interactions and monitor your clotting times closely.

### Will I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a big role in TCM. In general, you'll want to avoid spicy, greasy, and raw-cold foods, which can worsen heat or damage the Spleen. Your practitioner will give you specific guidance - for example, cooling foods like cucumber and watermelon for heat patterns, or warm, cooked soups and stews for deficiency patterns.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
A typical course is weekly sessions for 8-12 weeks, then reassessment. Many people notice less bleeding and more energy after 4-6 sessions. Chronic or complex patterns may need longer treatment, and your practitioner may recommend monthly maintenance sessions after the initial course to prevent relapse.

### Can acupuncture help if I have a diagnosed bleeding disorder?
Acupuncture can support overall Qi and blood health, but it must be done with caution in conditions like hemophilia or severe thrombocytopenia. Always inform your acupuncturist about any clotting disorder. They will use gentle techniques and avoid points that strongly move blood.

### Can I combine TCM with my conventional treatment?
Yes, in most cases TCM works well alongside Western treatments. For example, herbs can complement iron supplements for anemia. Just make sure both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor know everything you are taking to avoid herb-drug interactions. Never stop prescribed medication without your doctor's approval.
