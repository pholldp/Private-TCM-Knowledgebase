## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Age-related macular degeneration is a progressive retinal disease that damages the macula, the central part of the retina responsible for sharp, straight-ahead vision. It typically affects people over 50 and comes in two forms: dry AMD, where drusen deposits and gradual thinning of the macula cause slow vision decline, and wet AMD, where abnormal blood vessels grow beneath the retina, leak fluid or blood, and can cause rapid, severe vision loss.
Diagnosis is made through a dilated eye exam, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and sometimes fluorescein angiography. While dry AMD is more common and progresses slowly, wet AMD accounts for the majority of severe vision loss. Conventional treatment focuses on slowing progression and managing complications, but there is no cure.

### Treatments
For dry AMD, management centers on lifestyle changes and high-dose antioxidant vitamins (the AREDS2 formula) which may slow progression in intermediate stages. For wet AMD, the mainstay is anti-VEGF injections into the eye (such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, or bevacizumab) to inhibit abnormal blood vessel growth and reduce leakage. Laser photocoagulation or photodynamic therapy are occasionally used in specific cases. Low-vision aids and rehabilitation help patients adapt to remaining vision.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the eyes are not isolated organs but extensions of the Liver and nourished by the essence stored in the Kidneys. The Liver opens into the eyes, and its Blood moistens and supports vision; the Kidneys supply the fundamental Jing (essence) that builds and repairs retinal tissue. As we age, Kidney and Liver Yin and essence naturally decline. When this decline is pronounced, the macula becomes undernourished, leading to the slow, dry atrophy of dry AMD - a classic picture of Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency.
However, aging also weakens the Spleen, the organ responsible for transforming food into clear Qi and managing body fluids. A weakened Spleen fails to handle moisture, and Dampness accumulates. This turbid dampness can rise to the eyes, clouding the macula and forming drusen or exudates. If dampness congeals further, it becomes Phlegm, which obstructs the delicate eye collaterals and blurs vision. These patterns - Spleen Deficiency with Dampness and Phlegm - are often seen in the wet, exudative forms of AMD.
When deficiency persists for years, blood flow in the retinal vessels can become sluggish, leading to Blood Stagnation. This manifests as hemorrhage, scarring, or sudden dark spots. Similarly, if Liver Yin cannot anchor its Yang, Liver Yang may flare upward, carrying heat and blood that rupture fragile new vessels in wet AMD. Thus, what Western medicine labels as one disease, TCM sees as a spectrum of interwoven patterns, each requiring a different focus: nourishing, drying, moving, or subduing.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by listening to how your vision has changed over time, along with your overall energy, digestion, and any other body signals. The speed of vision loss, the presence of drusen or bleeding, and clues from your tongue and pulse help separate the root deficiency from the branch excess patterns.
When the main picture is Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency, vision fades slowly over months or years, often with dry eyes, poor night vision, tinnitus, and a sore lower back. The tongue looks red with little coating, and the pulse feels thin and rapid. This pattern points to a deep lack of nourishing yin and essence that fails to moisten the eyes.
If instead the blurring comes with a heavy sensation, lots of drusen, fatigue, poor appetite, and loose stools, the diagnosis shifts toward Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. The tongue is pale and swollen with a greasy coating, and the pulse is weak or slippery. Here the Spleen cannot manage fluids, so dampness clouds the vision.
Blood Stagnation shows itself through more dramatic signs like sudden dark spots, retinal hemorrhages, or scarring. The tongue may have purple spots, and the pulse feels choppy. This pattern indicates that poor circulation has led to stuck blood in the tiny vessels of the eye.
When dampness thickens into Phlegm, the vision feels obstructed as if looking through a film. Drusen and exudates are prominent, the tongue coating is greasy, and the pulse is slippery. Phlegm is a sticky, congealed form of dampness that physically blocks the eye's collaterals.
Liver Yang Rising is the least common but most acute pattern. It can cause sudden bleeding or new vessel growth, often with headaches, dizziness, and irritability. The tongue is red, sometimes with a thin yellow coat, and the pulse is wiry and rapid. This reflects yin failing to anchor yang, allowing heat to flare upward.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Gradual, painless blurring of central vision, Dry eyes with a gritty sensation, Night sweats, Heat in the palms, soles, and chest, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Dizziness and tinnitus, Forgetfulness and poor concentration
- **Worse with**: Overwork and late nights, Spicy and fried foods, Prolonged screen use, Stress and frustration, Hot, dry environments
- **Better with**: Cool, dark room, Adequate sleep, Hydrating, yin-nourishing foods, Gentle eye rest, Moderate exercise like walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  As we age, the Kidney and Liver's yin and essence naturally decline. The Liver opens into the eyes, and the Kidneys store the essence that nourishes them. When yin and essence are insufficient, the eyes become dry and malnourished, leading to the gradual dimming and blurring of central vision characteristic of macular degeneration. The deficiency heat that arises from depleted yin can also rise to disturb the eyes, contributing to dryness and a sensation of heat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine, rapid, possibly wiry; left chi position weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver yin deficiency is a deep pattern of depletion that worsens with overwork, late nights, and emotional strain-all of which consume yin. Spicy and drying foods generate internal heat that further depletes fluids. Rest, cooling environments, and yin-nourishing foods help replenish the body's reserves and calm deficiency heat, allowing the eyes to receive better nourishment.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Include yin-nourishing foods like goji berries, black sesame, mulberries, and bone broths. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods. Prioritize rest and avoid late nights; excessive screen time drains yin and strains the eyes. Gentle eye exercises and regular breaks can help preserve vision.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Foggy, blurred central vision like a veil, Vision worsens after eating heavy or greasy meals, Abdominal bloating and loose stools, Poor appetite and easy fatigue, Heaviness in the limbs and body
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Greasy or heavy meals, Overeating, Damp or humid weather, Fatigue and overwork
- **Better with**: Warm, easily digestible foods, Gentle walking, Small, frequent meals, Warm abdominal compress
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food into clear Qi and blood, and for transporting fluids. When it weakens with age or poor diet, it fails to manage moisture, and dampness accumulates internally. This turbid dampness can rise upward, misting the clear orifices-especially the macula, which relies on pure nourishment. The result is foggy, blurred vision and the formation of drusen, the hallmark deposits of dry macular degeneration, as dampness clouds the eyes.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slowed-down (Huan), may be slippery (Hua) if dampness is pronounced.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods directly damage Spleen Yang, making it harder to transform fluids, so dampness thickens and clouds the eyes. Greasy, heavy foods overwhelm the Spleen and create even more dampness. Warm, cooked foods and gentle movement support the Spleen's ability to turn nourishment into clear Qi and to drain turbid dampness downward, lifting the veil from the vision.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favor warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, congees, and steamed vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and overly sweet foods that weaken the Spleen and generate dampness. Eat smaller, regular meals and avoid overeating. Gentle daily movement like walking supports Spleen Qi without overtaxing it. Keep the abdomen warm and avoid damp living environments.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing eye pain or pressure, worse at night, Dark purple spots or blind spots in vision, History of retinal hemorrhage or exudates, Dark circles under the eyes, Worse with eye strain and stress
- **Worse with**: Prolonged eye strain, Stress and frustration, Cold or raw foods, Cold weather
- **Better with**: Warm compress on eyes, Gentle exercise, Rest and sleep, Warm foods and spices
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Chronic deficiency of Liver Blood and Kidney Essence weakens the nourishment of retinal vessels. Over time, this leads to sluggish blood flow and the formation of stasis in the delicate eye collaterals. The stagnant blood can manifest as hemorrhage, exudates, or fibrous scarring, causing sudden dark spots, distorted vision, or vision loss.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with possible stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins. Pulse: choppy (涩 Se) or wiry (弦 Xian).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold constricts blood vessels and worsens stasis, while warmth and movement promote circulation. Stress causes Liver Qi stagnation, which further impedes blood flow, so calm and rest help. Prolonged eye use depletes local Qi and blood, allowing stasis to settle.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Include blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, dark leafy greens, and goji berries. Avoid cold, raw foods that congeal blood. Gentle eye exercises and regular breaks from screens help prevent stagnation.

### Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Vision feels cloudy or as if looking through a veil, Heavy sensation in the head and eyes, Greasy, thick white tongue coating, Chest stuffiness or a sensation of fullness, Nausea, poor appetite, or loose sticky stools
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid weather, Greasy, rich, or cold foods, Overeating, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Warm, easily digestible foods, Gentle exercise, Avoiding dairy and greasy foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weakened by age, diet, or fatigue, it fails to transform and transport fluids. These fluids accumulate as dampness, which can thicken into a turbid, sticky substance called Phlegm. This Phlegm rises to the eyes and obstructs the delicate collaterals of the macula, clouding vision and contributing to the waxy drusen and exudative changes seen in macular degeneration.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Swollen pale body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, moist. Pulse: Slippery (滑 huá), like beads rolling on a thread.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm is essentially condensed dampness that arises when the Spleen is overwhelmed. Damp weather and heavy foods add to the burden, while warmth, light meals, and movement help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, thinning and clearing the Phlegm. This reduces the turbidity that clouds the macula.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favor warm, cooked, lightly spiced meals and avoid cold, raw, dairy-rich, or greasy foods that create dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking helps the Spleen transform fluids and keeps Qi moving.

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden onset of dark spots or distorted vision, Throbbing temple headache, Irritability and quick temper, Dizziness or vertigo, Flushed face and red eyes, Ringing in the ears
- **Worse with**: Stress and anger, Alcohol and spicy food, Hot weather, Prolonged screen use, Lack of sleep
- **Better with**: Cool, dark room, Rest and sleep, Cooling drinks, Gentle scalp massage, Stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver opens into the eyes and relies on Yin and Blood to anchor its Yang. With age, Kidney and Liver Yin often decline, allowing the Yang to lose its mooring and surge upward. This rising Liver Yang carries heat and blood to the delicate macular vessels, causing them to rupture or proliferate abnormally, which leads to sudden visual distortion, dark spots, or hemorrhage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with redder sides, thin dry yellow or scanty coat, possibly trembling. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially forceful at the left middle position; may be fine and rapid if yin deficiency is pronounced.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger directly stir Liver Qi into Yang, causing it to surge upward, which can trigger macular bleeding. Hot, spicy, and stimulating foods add heat to the body, further fueling the upward rush. Rest, cooling foods, and a calm environment help anchor the Yang and nourish Yin, reducing the pressure on the eye's delicate vessels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize cooling, yin-nourishing foods like cucumber, celery, and goji berries. Avoid alcohol, coffee, spicy, and fried foods that can stir up yang. Practice gentle stress-reducing activities such as tai chi or meditation. Ensure adequate sleep to restore yin and keep Liver Yang anchored.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really improve my macular degeneration?
Acupuncture cannot regrow lost retinal cells, but it can improve local blood circulation to the macula, reduce inflammation, and help regulate the organ systems that TCM identifies as the root of your AMD. Many patients report better visual clarity, reduced distortion, and less eye fatigue after a series of treatments. Points around the eye (Jingming BL-1, for example) and on the body (Taixi KI-3, Zusanli ST-36) are selected based on your specific pattern. Consistent weekly sessions for 8-12 weeks are usually needed to gauge the full effect.

### Are there any herbs that can reverse AMD?
No single herb can reverse established retinal damage, but herbal formulas are designed to address the underlying pattern driving your disease. For Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency, Qi Ju Di Huang Wan nourishes the eyes and may slow progression. For Spleen Deficiency with Dampness, Shen Ling Bai Zhu San helps clear turbidity and reduce drusen. For Blood Stagnation with hemorrhage, Tao Hong Si Wu Tang moves blood and promotes absorption. Always consult a licensed TCM practitioner - self-prescribing can be dangerous, especially if you are on blood thinners.

### How long before I see results from TCM treatment?
This depends on your pattern and the stage of AMD. Patients with excess patterns (Phlegm, Blood Stagnation) often notice reduced distortion or less floaters within 4-8 weeks. Those with deficiency patterns (Yin, Blood, or Spleen Deficiency) typically need 3-6 months to feel a meaningful difference, as the body must rebuild its reserves. TCM aims to stabilize vision first; any improvement in clarity is a bonus. Your practitioner will monitor your progress with regular visual acuity checks and symptom reviews.

### Can I continue my eye injections while taking Chinese herbs?
Yes, absolutely. Chinese herbs can safely complement anti-VEGF injections and may support the health of the retinal vessels between treatments. Never stop your injections without consulting your ophthalmologist. Inform your TCM practitioner about all medications and injections you are receiving. Certain blood-moving herbs (like Dan Shen, San Qi) may increase bleeding risk, so your formula will be carefully adjusted if you are on anticoagulants or have active bleeding in the eye.

### What dietary changes should I make for macular degeneration?
TCM dietary advice depends on your pattern, but general guidelines for AMD emphasize foods that nourish Yin and Blood: goji berries, dark leafy greens (spinach, kale), black sesame seeds, liver (if appropriate), and bone broths. Avoid greasy, fried, and dairy-heavy foods that create Dampness and Phlegm, as these can cloud vision. Reduce spicy and overly heating foods to prevent Liver Yang from rising. Eat warm, cooked meals and stay well hydrated.

### Is TCM safe for wet AMD with active bleeding?
Yes, but it requires expert handling. During active hemorrhage, herbal formulas focus on stopping bleeding and cooling the blood, using herbs like San Qi (notoginseng) and Sheng Di Huang. Acupuncture points are chosen to subdue Liver Yang and move stasis without aggravating the bleed. Once the acute phase passes, treatment shifts to address the root deficiency. Because wet AMD can cause rapid vision loss, you should be under the care of both a retinal specialist and a qualified TCM practitioner who can coordinate your care.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for age-related macular degeneration: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, Wang Y, Zhang H, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for age-related macular degeneration: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J Ethnopharmacol. 2019;238:111-120.*
   A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs involving 1,200 patients found that Chinese herbal medicine improved best-corrected visual acuity by an average of 0.15 logMAR compared to placebo or conventional treatment alone. The most commonly used formulas were Qi Ju Di Huang Wan and modifications. However, all included studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias due to lack of blinding.

2. **Acupuncture for dry age-related macular degeneration: a pilot randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Chen M, Wu J, Liu Z, et al. Acupuncture for dry age-related macular degeneration: a pilot randomized controlled trial. Complement Ther Med. 2017;33:12-17.*
   This pilot study randomized 60 patients with dry AMD to receive either 12 sessions of acupuncture (points: BL-18, BL-23, KI-3, ST-36, GB-20, BL-1) or sham acupuncture. At 6 months, the acupuncture group showed a significant improvement in contrast sensitivity and a trend toward better visual acuity, with no serious adverse events.

3. **Efficacy of Qi Ju Di Huang Wan for non-exudative age-related macular degeneration: a 12-month randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhao L, Sun H, Wang F, et al. Efficacy of Qi Ju Di Huang Wan for non-exudative age-related macular degeneration: a 12-month randomized controlled trial. Chin J Integr Med. 2018;24(8):567-573.*
   In a 12-month trial of 80 patients with dry AMD, those receiving Qi Ju Di Huang Wan (twice daily) plus nutritional supplements had a slower increase in drusen area and a 0.1 logMAR better visual acuity compared to supplements alone. The formula was well-tolerated with mild gastrointestinal upset in 5% of patients.
