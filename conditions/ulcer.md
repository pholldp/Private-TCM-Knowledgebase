## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Ulcers are open sores that can occur on the skin or mucous membranes. The most common types are mouth ulcers (canker sores) and peptic ulcers (sores in the stomach or duodenum). Mouth ulcers are usually small, painful, and self-limiting, often triggered by stress, minor injury, or certain foods. Peptic ulcers are erosions in the stomach lining or upper small intestine, typically caused by H. pylori infection or long-term use of NSAIDs. They cause burning epigastric pain, often relieved by eating or antacids. Diagnosis is usually clinical for mouth ulcers; peptic ulcers may require endoscopy. Chronic skin ulcers, such as pressure sores or venous stasis ulcers, result from prolonged pressure or poor circulation.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment depends on the type. Mouth ulcers are often managed with topical corticosteroids, antimicrobial mouthwashes, or protective pastes. Peptic ulcers are treated with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) to reduce stomach acid, antibiotics to eradicate H. pylori if present, and discontinuation of NSAIDs. Chronic skin ulcers require wound care, pressure relief, and sometimes surgery. While these treatments can heal the ulcer, they often do not address the underlying susceptibility or prevent recurrence.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees an ulcer not as a standalone sore but as a distress signal from deeper organ systems. The location, color, pain quality, and discharge all reveal which internal imbalance is at play. A bright red, burning mouth ulcer that appears after a spicy meal points to Toxic-Heat flaring upward. A stress-triggered stomach ulcer with bitter taste and rib-side tension suggests Liver Qi Stagnation that has turned into Heat. A pale, indolent ulcer that refuses to heal and leaves you exhausted reflects Spleen Qi Deficiency - the body simply lacks the raw material to repair tissue.
The most common thread is Heat, but it comes in two forms: excess and deficiency. Excess Heat (from overeating spicy, greasy foods, or from emotional fire) burns the tissue directly, creating red, painful, rapidly developing ulcers. Deficiency Heat arises when cooling Yin or Blood is depleted, often from overwork, late nights, or chronic stress, causing a relative excess of Yang that flares up as small, recurrent mouth ulcers or a burning sensation.
So the same symptom - a mouth ulcer - can be either an excess fire that needs clearing or a deficiency fire that needs nourishing, and treating the wrong one will make things worse.
Dampness is another key player, especially when ulcers ooze, feel heavy, or are accompanied by a greasy tongue coating. Damp-Heat is a sticky combination that blocks Qi flow and creates inflammation that is hard to clear. It often underlies chronic, weeping ulcers and can coexist with Spleen Qi Deficiency, which fails to manage fluids properly.
Finally, Blood Stagnation can turn an acute ulcer into a chronic, fixed, stabbing pain with dark purplish margins. When Qi and Blood cannot reach the tissue, healing stalls. This pattern often develops after a long history of other imbalances, so treatment must both move stasis and support the underlying deficiency.
In practice, a person often presents with a mix. A Spleen Qi Deficiency may set the stage for Damp-Heat accumulation, or long-standing Liver Qi Stagnation may generate Heat and then damage Yin. That’s why a TCM practitioner examines the tongue, pulse, and full symptom picture - to untangle these layers and design a treatment that addresses both the root and the branch.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the ulcer’s appearance, sensation, and what makes it better or worse. The color, pain quality, discharge, and onset speed are all clues. A bright red, hot, acutely painful ulcer that flares suddenly points toward a Toxic-Heat pattern, where an external or internal heat pathogen is raging in the local tissue.
If the ulcer oozes thick, sticky fluid and the surrounding tissue feels heavy or swollen, Damp-Heat is likely. The practitioner will ask about a greasy taste in the mouth, a sense of bodily heaviness, and digestive sluggishness. The tongue often appears red with a thick, yellow, greasy coat, and the pulse feels slippery and rapid.
When stress, frustration, or anger reliably triggers or worsens the ulcer, the practitioner considers Liver Qi Stagnation transforming into Heat. Questions focus on emotional patterns, rib-side distension, belching, and a bitter taste. The tongue edges may be redder than the rest, the coat is thin and yellow, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid.
A chronic, pale ulcer that resists healing and lacks strong redness suggests Spleen Qi Deficiency. The practitioner will ask about fatigue, poor appetite, loose stools, and a sense of heaviness after eating. The tongue is typically pale and puffy with teeth marks on the sides, and the pulse is weak and thready, showing that the body lacks the Qi to generate new flesh.
For recurrent mouth ulcers that are small, intensely painful at night, and accompanied by a dry mouth and throat, the practitioner looks for Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty-Heat Blazing. The tongue is red with little or no coating, often with cracks, and the pulse is thin and rapid. This pattern reveals a deep Yin shortage that allows a low-grade, flaring heat to rise.
When an ulcer has been present for a long time, with dark purplish edges and a fixed, stabbing pain, Blood Stagnation is suspected. The practitioner will ask whether the pain is worse at night and whether the area feels hard. The tongue may be dark, have purple spots, or show distended veins underneath, and the pulse feels choppy or wiry.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Toxic-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/toxic-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Red, swollen, hot ulcer with burning pain, Intense thirst with desire for cold drinks, Fever or feeling of heat throughout the body, Restlessness and irritability, Dark, scanty urine
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress and anger, Hot, humid weather
- **Better with**: Cooling foods (watermelon, cucumber, pear), Cold drinks or ice chips, Rest and stress reduction, Gentle cooling compresses
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Toxic-Heat arises when strong external pathogens or excessive spicy, greasy foods create intense Heat and toxins inside the body. These toxins surge upward to the mouth or out to the skin, causing the local tissue to become red, swollen, and extremely painful. The Heat damages the flesh and fluids, leading to the formation of an acute, hot ulcer that may ooze pus and feel like it is burning.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with prickly thorns, dry yellow coating. Pulse: rapid and forceful.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Toxic-Heat is like an internal fire that burns tissues. Spicy, greasy foods and emotional upset stoke that fire, making the ulcer more painful and inflamed. Cooling foods, cold drinks, and rest directly reduce the Heat, while calming the mind prevents the upward surge of fiery energy that feeds the sore.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, fried, and greasy foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add fuel to the fire. Eat plenty of cooling foods like cucumber, watermelon, mung beans, and leafy greens. Sip cool water throughout the day and keep your environment calm to prevent Heat from flaring.

### Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Ulcers that are swollen and ooze fluid, Heavy sensation in the body or head, Sticky or bitter taste in the mouth, Chest and upper abdominal stuffiness, Loose, sticky stools that feel incomplete, Dark yellow, scanty urine, Thirst with little desire to actually drink
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Damp, humid weather, Alcohol and coffee, Eating large meals, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Cool, dry environment, Light, bland diet, Chrysanthemum or dandelion tea, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Dampness and Heat combine in the body, they create a sticky, turbid environment that blocks the normal flow of Qi and Blood. This stagnation generates local inflammation, eroding the skin or mucous membranes and producing ulcers that are swollen, red, and often weep fluid. In the mouth, this shows up as painful canker sores with a yellowish coating; deeper in the digestive tract, it can cause erosions that feel burning and heavy.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating that is difficult to scrape off. Pulse: soggy-rapid (ru shu) or slippery-rapid (hua shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat thrives on rich, heavy, and heating foods that add more Dampness and Heat to the body. A bland, cooling diet and a dry environment starve the pattern of its fuel, while gentle movement helps the Spleen transform and transport fluids so Dampness does not accumulate. Cooling teas and acupuncture points directly drain Heat and dry Dampness, allowing the ulcer to close.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sugary foods that feed Dampness and Heat. Eat lightly cooked vegetables, whole grains like barley, and small amounts of lean protein. Sip cooling teas like chrysanthemum or dandelion. Keep living spaces dry and well-ventilated, and incorporate gentle daily movement to help Qi flow and Dampness disperse.

### Liver Qi Stagnation that transforms into Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-that-transforms-into-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning pain that worsens with stress or anger, Bitter taste in the mouth and dry throat, Irritability and explosive anger, Distending or burning pain along the ribs, Acid reflux or sour belching
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress and anger, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overwork and late nights
- **Better with**: Rest and stress reduction, Cooling foods (watermelon, cucumber, pear), Gentle movement or walking, Expressing emotions openly
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Stress and frustration trap the Liver's Qi, like a coiled spring that cannot release. Over time, that stuck energy generates Heat, which then rises or spreads. When the Heat attacks the stomach lining, it erodes the protective mucosal barrier, leading to peptic ulcers with burning pain and acid reflux. If the Heat flares upward to the mouth, it scorches the delicate membranes and creates painful oral ulcers.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with redder sides, yellow coating. Pulse: wiry and rapid (弦数, xián shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional tension knots the Liver Qi, which over time produces Heat - the very force that damages the stomach or mouth lining. Calming the mind and moving the body gently helps Qi flow freely, preventing that Heat from building up. Spicy and greasy foods add external Heat, while alcohol and overwork deplete Yin and fan the flames. Cooling foods and adequate rest starve the internal fire and allow the mucosa to heal.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Make stress management a priority - even short daily walks or deep breathing can prevent Qi from stagnating. Avoid spicy, fried, and greasy foods, as well as alcohol, which add fuel to the fire. Sip chrysanthemum tea and eat more cucumber, celery, and pear to gently cool the body.

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, non-healing ulcer with little exudate, Fatigue and lack of energy, Abdominal bloating worse after eating, Loose or soft stools, Poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Overthinking and worry, Cold and raw foods, Overexertion and fatigue, Eating large meals
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, easily digestible meals, Gentle movement or walking, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen transforms food into Qi and blood - the raw materials your body uses to repair and close a wound. When Spleen Qi is weak, that supply runs low, so ulcers heal slowly and often look pale or indolent with little fresh granulation. At the same time, the Spleen's ability to manage fluids falters, allowing dampness to seep into the ulcer bed and keep it moist and stagnant rather than closing cleanly.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, and tender with tooth marks; thin white coating. Pulse: weak and soft, especially at the right guan (Spleen position).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overthinking and worry directly weaken Spleen Qi, while cold and raw foods require extra Qi to warm and digest, further depleting the Spleen. Rest and warm, easily digested foods conserve Qi and allow the Spleen to focus on healing. Gentle movement keeps Qi flowing without straining the body.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and congee to support Spleen Qi. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax digestion. Gentle movement like walking helps Qi circulation without depleting energy, and small, frequent meals prevent overloading the Spleen.

### Kidney Yin Deficiency With Empty-Heat Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-yin-deficiency-with-empty-heat-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Small, painful mouth ulcers that worsen at night, Dry mouth and throat, especially at night, Night sweats, Five-palm heat (heat in palms, soles, and chest), Lower back soreness and weakness, Mental restlessness and insomnia
- **Worse with**: Overwork and late nights, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Emotional stress and anger, Dry, hot environments, Excessive sexual activity
- **Better with**: Early bedtime, Cooling, moistening foods, Rest and stress reduction, Hydration with small sips, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Kidney Yin is the body's deep cooling and moistening reserve. When it becomes depleted by overwork, late nights, or chronic stress, the relative excess of Yang turns into Empty-Heat. This heat has nowhere solid to anchor, so it rises along the Kidney channel into the mouth and throat, burning the delicate mucosa and creating small, recurrent ulcers that are especially painful at night when Yin should be dominant.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin, and dry with little or no coating; possible cracks. Pulse: fine (Xi) and rapid (Shu), may feel floating and empty; the chi (rear) position is weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Yin is the body's cooling and moistening reserve. Overwork, late nights, stress, and heating foods deplete Yin, allowing Empty-Heat to flare up to the mouth and cause ulcers. Rest, cooling foods, and hydration nourish Yin and help the body's natural cooling system regain control, calming the upward-blazing heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, fried, and warming foods, as well as coffee and alcohol, which consume Yin and stir up Empty-Heat. Eat cooling, moistening foods like pear, cucumber, black sesame, and goji berries. Prioritize sleep before 11 pm to preserve Kidney Yin, and avoid overwork and late nights. Gentle exercises like tai chi or walking are better than intense workouts that overheat the body.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain at the ulcer site that does not move, Pain worsens at night and with pressure, Dark purplish or dusky discoloration around the ulcer, Chronic, slow-healing ulcer with hard or lumpy margins, Dark menstrual clots and painful periods (in women)
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or cold exposure, Sedentary lifestyle, Emotional stress and anger, Cold and raw foods
- **Better with**: Warmth and warm compress, Gentle movement or walking, Turmeric and ginger in food, Massage around the area (not directly on open ulcer)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi flow is obstructed over a long period, blood circulation becomes sluggish and eventually stagnates. In an ulcer, this stagnation creates a fixed, stabbing pain and a purplish, dusky appearance around the wound. The stagnant blood prevents fresh nutrients and Qi from reaching the tissue, so the ulcer becomes chronic, slow to heal, and resistant to treatment.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: Choppy (涩, sè) or wiry (弦, xián).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold causes blood vessels to constrict and blood to congeal, directly worsening stasis and pain. Emotional stress knots the Liver Qi, and since Qi is the commander of Blood, stagnant Qi quickly leads to stagnant blood, so calming the mind and moving the body are essential to break the cycle.

Regular, moderate exercise such as walking or stretching helps maintain circulation and prevent stagnation. Avoid prolonged sitting and manage emotional stress, which can tighten Qi flow and worsen stasis.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM really heal an ulcer, or do I need medication?
Yes, TCM can heal ulcers, often alongside conventional care. For acute ulcers, herbal formulas that clear Heat and detoxify can reduce pain and inflammation within days. Chronic, recurrent ulcers respond more gradually as the underlying deficiency is corrected. Many patients use TCM to reduce their reliance on long-term medications, but always work with your doctor before making any changes to prescribed treatment.

### How long does it take for a mouth ulcer to heal with TCM treatment?
An acute, excess-type mouth ulcer (bright red, painful, triggered by spicy food or stress) often shows significant improvement within 3-5 days of starting herbs and acupuncture. Recurrent, deficiency-type ulcers (pale, slow to heal, worse with fatigue) may take 2-4 weeks to see consistent healing and longer to reduce recurrence. Your practitioner will adjust the treatment as your tongue and pulse change.

### Do I have to give up spicy food forever?
Not necessarily forever, but during active ulcer phases and while you are healing, it’s best to avoid very spicy, greasy, and deep-fried foods, as they generate Heat and Dampness that worsen most ulcer patterns. Once your internal balance is restored, many people can enjoy these foods in moderation without triggering new ulcers. Your practitioner can guide you on when and how to reintroduce them.

### Can stress really cause an ulcer, and how does TCM treat that?
Yes, emotional stress - especially frustration and anger - is a major trigger. In TCM, stress stagnates Liver Qi, which over time turns into Heat that can attack the stomach lining or flare up to the mouth. Treatment focuses on soothing the Liver, moving Qi, and clearing the resulting Heat. Acupuncture points like Taichong (LR-3) and herbs like Chai Hu are commonly used. Many patients notice fewer stress-related flares after a few weeks of treatment.

### Is it safe to use Chinese herbs if I'm already taking PPIs or antibiotics for an ulcer?
Generally, yes. TCM herbs can be used alongside proton pump inhibitors and H. pylori antibiotics. Some herbs even have antibacterial effects that may support eradication. However, always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about all medications you are taking. If you are on blood thinners, certain herbs (like San Qi or Dan Shen) may increase bleeding risk and should be used with caution. Never stop prescribed medications abruptly without medical advice.

### My ulcer keeps coming back every month. Can TCM stop the recurrence?
Recurrence is often a sign of an underlying deficiency or a persistent pathogenic factor that hasn’t been fully cleared. TCM aims to identify that root - whether it’s Spleen Qi Deficiency, Kidney Yin Deficiency, or lingering Damp-Heat - and correct it over time. Patients with recurrent ulcers typically need 3-6 months of consistent treatment to rebuild reserves and break the cycle. Many report that after this period, new ulcers become much less frequent or stop entirely.

### What does my tongue tell you about my ulcer?
Your tongue is a map of your internal state. A red tongue with a yellow coating suggests Heat, while a pale, puffy tongue with tooth marks points to Spleen Qi Deficiency. A greasy yellow coating indicates Damp-Heat, and a red tongue with little coating suggests Yin Deficiency. These clues help your practitioner choose the right formula and track your progress over time.
