## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Atherosclerosis is a condition where arteries become narrowed and hardened due to a buildup of plaque - fat, cholesterol, calcium, and other substances - on the inner walls. This buildup restricts blood flow and can lead to serious complications like heart attack, stroke, or peripheral artery disease. Diagnosis typically involves blood tests to check cholesterol and inflammation markers, imaging such as ultrasound or CT scans, and sometimes angiography to see the blockages directly.

### Treatments
Standard treatment focuses on slowing plaque progression and preventing clots. Lifestyle changes - a heart-healthy diet, exercise, and quitting smoking - are foundational. Medications commonly include statins to lower cholesterol, antihypertensives to control blood pressure, and antiplatelet drugs like aspirin to reduce clotting risk. In advanced cases, procedures such as angioplasty with stenting or bypass surgery may be needed to restore blood flow.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM sees atherosclerosis as a condition rooted in two main pathological accumulations: Phlegm and Blood Stagnation. When the Spleen is weak - often from poor diet, stress, or aging - it fails to transform fluids properly, and dampness condenses into thick, turbid Phlegm. This Phlegm can seep into the blood vessels and stick to the walls, forming the fatty deposits that narrow the arteries.
At the same time, if Qi flow is obstructed or deficient, blood circulation slows and congeals, creating Blood Stagnation that hardens and thickens the vessel walls.
The underlying driver is almost always a deficiency - most commonly of the Spleen and Kidney. The Spleen provides the energy to move and transform, while the Kidneys store the body's foundational Yin and Yang. As these reserves decline with age or chronic illness, the vessels lose their nourishment and elasticity, becoming dry, brittle, and more susceptible to damage. This is why atherosclerosis is more common in older adults and those with long-standing metabolic imbalance.
Because the root is deficiency and the branch is excess (Phlegm, Stagnation, sometimes Heat), the same Western diagnosis can present very differently in different people. One person may have a heavy, sluggish feeling with a greasy tongue coating (Phlegm dominant), while another has sharp, fixed chest pain and a purple tongue (Blood Stagnation dominant), and a third may feel profoundly fatigued with a weak pulse (Qi Deficiency). Each requires a fundamentally different treatment strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When a TCM practitioner sees atherosclerosis, they look beyond the plaque itself to the underlying imbalances that allowed it to form. They ask about energy, digestion, mood, and the exact nature of any pain, and they pay close attention to the tongue and pulse. These details help them identify which pattern is driving the vessel changes.
If the person tends to be overweight, feels chest oppression, produces a lot of phlegm, and has a greasy tongue coating with a slippery pulse, the pattern is likely Phlegm. This turbid, damp accumulation is considered the main building material of the plaque.
When sharp, fixed, stabbing pain is the dominant complaint, and the tongue looks dark purple with stasis spots while the pulse feels choppy or wiry, Blood Stagnation is the key pattern. The blood is not moving smoothly, and that stagnation hardens and thickens the vessel walls.
If fatigue and weakness are prominent, the complexion is pale or sallow, and the pain is dull rather than sharp, the problem may be Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation. The tongue may be pale with dark spots, and the pulse is weak and deep. Here the blood stagnates because the Qi is too weak to push it along.
When the phlegm pattern turns hot, you get Phlegm-Heat. The person may feel chest fullness with a burning sensation, have a dry mouth, bad breath, and a yellow greasy tongue coating with a rapid, slippery pulse. The heat makes the plaque more inflammatory and unstable.
If the person is thin, tired, has a dry mouth but doesn’t want to drink much, and the tongue is red or pale with little coating, the pattern is Qi and Yin Deficiency. The vessels lack proper nourishment, and the blood becomes sluggish due to the dryness and weakness.
In older adults, Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency often underlies atherosclerosis. They may complain of dizziness, tinnitus, sore lower back, and dry eyes. The tongue is red with scant coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. The Yin deficiency leaves the vessels dry and brittle, making plaque more likely.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chest heaviness and oppression, Productive cough with white sticky sputum, Heavy, sluggish feeling in the body and limbs, Loose, sticky stools, Mental fogginess or excessive sleepiness
- **Worse with**: Damp, humid weather, Greasy, fried, or fatty foods, Dairy products, Overeating, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold, raw foods
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Light, cooked meals, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and fails to transform fluids, dampness accumulates and condenses into thick, turbid phlegm. This phlegm can seep into the blood vessels, where it congeals and sticks to the vessel walls, forming the fatty plaques of atherosclerosis. The result is a feeling of heaviness, chest oppression, and sluggish circulation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: swollen pale body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), like beads rolling under the fingers.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm is formed when the Spleen fails to transform fluids, often due to a diet heavy in greasy, dairy, and cold foods, or a damp living environment. These factors overwhelm the Spleen, creating more dampness that congeals into phlegm. Warmth, light meals, and movement all help the Spleen function better, promoting the transformation and excretion of dampness. Avoiding phlegm-producing foods and staying active are essential to prevent phlegm from accumulating and clogging the vessels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and dairy-rich foods, as well as cold and raw items, which all promote dampness and phlegm. Eat warm, cooked meals with plenty of vegetables and moderate amounts of lean protein. Gentle daily exercise like walking or tai chi helps move Qi and transform phlegm. Keep living spaces dry and well-ventilated.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain that worsens with pressure, Pain worse at night and with pressure, Dark, dusky, or purplish complexion and lips, Dark purple tongue with stasis spots, Distended dark veins under the tongue
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold environment or icy foods, Greasy, fried, or fatty foods
- **Better with**: Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.), Warmth (warm drinks, heating pad), Stress reduction, Massage or acupressure
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi flow is obstructed, blood cannot circulate smoothly. Over time, the sluggish blood congeals and deposits in the vessel walls, much like silt settling in a slow-moving river. This leads to the hardening and narrowing of arteries, producing the fixed, stabbing pain and dark complexion characteristic of blood stasis in atherosclerosis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè) like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦 xián) when Qi stagnation is present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis arises when Qi stagnates or cold congeals the blood. Stress tightens the Liver Qi, which then fails to propel blood, while cold physically thickens blood and slows its flow. Gentle movement, warmth, and relaxation all encourage Qi to move and blood to circulate, preventing the stasis that leads to plaque buildup.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Warm the body and move the blood. Include warming spices like turmeric, ginger, and garlic in meals. Eat moderate amounts of onion, chives, and eggplant. Avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which congeal blood. Gentle daily exercise-walking, tai chi, or cycling-keeps circulation active. Do not sit for long periods; take short movement breaks.

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain that worsens with pressure, Chest or ribcage area, Numbness or tingling in the limbs, Dark, dusky, or purplish complexion and lips, Fatigue and lack of strength, Shortness of breath, possibly with reluctance to speak
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, including late nights, Cold, raw foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, cooked meals or soups, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.), Stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi is deficient, it lacks the strength to push blood through the vessels. This sluggish flow allows blood to congeal and form stasis, which over time deposits as plaque on artery walls. The result is a combination of deep fatigue and fixed, stabbing pain - the hallmark of atherosclerosis driven by Qi deficiency with blood stasis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body, possible stasis spots, teeth marks, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: deep, fine or weak, and choppy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi deficiency means the body lacks the energy to push blood; overexertion further drains Qi, allowing stasis to worsen. Cold and raw foods chill the Spleen, impairing Qi production, while a sedentary lifestyle slows Qi and blood circulation, encouraging plaque buildup. Rest, gentle movement, and warm nourishing meals restore Qi and keep blood moving, directly countering the root deficiency and its stasis consequence.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, and easily digestible foods like congee, stews, and steamed vegetables with small amounts of lean meat. Avoid cold, raw, or greasy foods that further weaken digestion. Gentle daily movement such as walking, tai chi, or qigong helps Qi circulate and prevents blood from stagnating. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion.

### Phlegm-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Chest oppression with a burning or heavy sensation, Thick, sticky, yellow phlegm (if coughing), Dry mouth and throat, possibly with sticky taste or little desire to drink, Restlessness, irritability, or a flushed face, Feeling of heat or low-grade fever, Yellow, greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or fatty foods, Alcohol and coffee, Spicy foods, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Hot, humid weather
- **Better with**: Cooling, light or moist foods, Peppermint or chrysanthemum tea, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.), Calm, cool environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body fails to transform fluids properly, they accumulate as phlegm. Over time, this phlegm can become stagnant and generate heat, creating a sticky, inflammatory mixture that deposits inside the arteries. This Phlegm-Heat makes the vessel walls thick and inflamed, contributing directly to plaque buildup and a sense of chest oppression or burning.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, may be slightly swollen, with a thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Heat arises from excess Dampness and Heat in the body. Greasy, spicy, and alcoholic foods add more Dampness and Heat, directly thickening the phlegm and inflaming the vessels. Cooling, light foods and herbs like peppermint clear Heat, while gentle movement keeps Qi and fluids circulating so phlegm cannot settle. Stress and anger stir up internal Heat, so staying calm helps prevent the pattern from flaring.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and spicy foods, as well as alcohol, which all generate more Phlegm and Heat. Favor cooling, light foods like cucumber, pear, and mung beans. Gentle daily movement such as walking helps Qi flow and keeps phlegm from stagnating.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fatigue and lack of strength, Dry mouth and throat, possibly with sticky taste or little desire to drink, Night sweats, Heat sensation in palms, soles, and chest, Shortness of breath, possibly with reluctance to speak, Heart palpitations and dizziness
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, including late nights, Spicy foods, Hot, dry weather or environments, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Warm, cooked meals or soups, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.), Cool, humid environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi is the engine that moves blood and nourishes the vessel walls. When Qi is weak, blood flow slows and becomes stagnant. Yin is the cooling, moisturizing force; when it is depleted, the vessel walls become dry, brittle, and prone to damage. This combination of sluggish flow and fragile vessels allows fatty deposits to accumulate, forming atherosclerotic plaques. Over time, the body’s lack of energy and moisture deepens the damage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red or pale-red body with thin or no coating, possibly cracked and dry. Pulse: fine (thin), weak, and often rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin are depleted, so anything that further drains energy or dries fluids worsens the condition. Rest and gentle movement conserve Qi, while moistening foods and cool environments replenish Yin and soothe the Empty Heat that arises from Yin deficiency.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, moistening foods like congee, yams, pears, and black sesame. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly dry foods that deplete fluids. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork; gentle movement such as Tai Chi or walking can strengthen Qi without draining it. Keep the living environment cool and humidified to soothe Yin deficiency.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness and ringing in the ears, Dry eyes with blurred vision, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Heat sensation in palms, soles, and chest, Insomnia with restless, vivid dreams, Dry mouth and throat, possibly with sticky taste or little desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or overwork, including late nights, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, dry weather or environments
- **Better with**: Rest and adequate sleep, Cooling, light or moist foods, Acupuncture and herbal support, Gentle exercise (walking, tai chi, etc.), Hydration with warm water
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Kidney and Liver Yin are depleted, the body's nourishing fluids run dry. The blood vessels lose their moisture and elasticity, becoming stiff and brittle. This dryness, combined with the low-grade heat from Yin deficiency, damages the vessel walls and makes them more susceptible to plaque buildup and hardening.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin), rapid, and often wiry; the left Chi (Kidney) position is weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver Yin are easily consumed by overwork, stress, and heat. Rest and cooling, moist foods replenish Yin fluids, while acupuncture and herbs directly nourish the depleted essence. Avoiding stimulants and hot environments prevents further fluid loss, allowing the vessels to regain some elasticity and resilience.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Emphasize moist, cooling foods like black sesame, goji berries, mulberries, and pears. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods as well as alcohol and coffee, which deplete Yin. Gentle exercise like tai chi and adequate sleep help conserve Yin. Manage stress to prevent further Liver Yin depletion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture and herbs actually clear plaque from my arteries?
Research and clinical experience suggest that TCM can help stabilize plaques, reduce inflammation, and improve the body's ability to metabolize fats - which may slow or even partially reverse plaque buildup over time. Herbs that move Blood and transform Phlegm, combined with acupuncture to improve circulation, work to change the internal environment so that new plaque is less likely to form and existing deposits may gradually soften. Results vary, but many patients see improvements in blood flow and a reduction in related symptoms.

### Is TCM safe to use alongside my statins or blood thinners?
Yes, TCM can often be used safely alongside conventional medications, but it requires careful coordination. Some blood-moving herbs like Dang Gui, Chuan Xiong, and Tao Ren may enhance the effect of anticoagulants such as warfarin or aspirin, increasing bleeding risk. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your cardiologist about all medications and supplements you are taking. Never stop or adjust your prescribed medications without medical supervision.

### How long does it take to see results with TCM?
Most people notice improvements in energy, chest comfort, and overall well-being within 4-6 weeks. Lab markers like cholesterol and blood pressure may begin to shift after 2-3 months. Significant plaque stabilization or regression is a longer process, often taking 6-12 months of consistent treatment and lifestyle changes. Your TCM practitioner will monitor your progress through symptom changes and tongue/pulse assessment, adjusting your formula as your pattern evolves.

### What diet should I follow to support my TCM treatment?
A TCM-friendly diet for atherosclerosis emphasizes warm, cooked foods that support the Spleen and reduce Phlegm and Dampness. Favor whole grains, legumes, lightly cooked vegetables, and moderate amounts of lean protein. Avoid greasy, fried, and overly sweet foods, as well as excessive dairy and cold raw foods, which weaken digestion and promote Phlegm. Small amounts of onion, garlic, turmeric, and hawthorn berry can gently support circulation.

### Can TCM prevent a heart attack or stroke?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalances that lead to plaque formation and vessel damage, which may lower the long-term risk of heart attack and stroke. By improving blood flow, reducing inflammation, and strengthening the body's Qi and Yin, TCM works as a preventive strategy. However, it does not replace emergency medical care. If you have acute chest pain, sudden weakness, or other warning signs, seek immediate medical attention.

### Will I need acupuncture forever?
No. Acupuncture is typically most intensive at the beginning - often weekly for the first 2-3 months. As your condition stabilizes, sessions can be tapered to every other week, then monthly, and eventually to seasonal tune-ups. Herbal therapy may also be reduced or stopped once your pattern has shifted. The goal is to restore balance so that your body can maintain vascular health with minimal ongoing intervention.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Effect of Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction on carotid atherosclerosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang J, et al. Effect of Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction on carotid atherosclerosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine. 2018;24(8):567-575.*
   This meta-analysis pooled data from multiple RCTs and found that adding Xue Fu Zhu Yu Tang to standard care significantly reduced carotid intima-media thickness and improved blood lipid profiles compared to standard care alone. The study supports the formula's traditional use for Blood Stagnation-driven atherosclerosis.

2. **Huanglian Wendan Decoction for phlegm-heat type atherosclerosis: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, et al. Clinical study on Huanglian Wendan Decoction in treating phlegm-heat obstructing the lung type carotid atherosclerosis. Chinese Journal of Experimental Traditional Medical Formulae. 2020;26(15):112-118.*
   In this RCT, 120 patients with carotid atherosclerosis and a Phlegm-Heat pattern were treated with either Huang Lian Wen Dan Tang plus atorvastatin or atorvastatin alone. After 12 weeks, the combination group showed greater reductions in plaque area and inflammatory markers, with fewer gastrointestinal side effects.

3. **Acupuncture for stable angina pectoris: a systematic review**
   *Journal/Publication: Chen Y, et al. Acupuncture for stable angina pectoris: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine. 2019;2019:8567127.*
   This review evaluated 16 RCTs and concluded that acupuncture as an adjunct to conventional medication significantly reduced angina frequency and improved exercise tolerance in patients with coronary artery disease, which is closely linked to atherosclerosis. The most commonly used points were Neiguan PC-6 and Xinshu BL-15.
