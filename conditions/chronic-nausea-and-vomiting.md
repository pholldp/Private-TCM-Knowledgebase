## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, chronic nausea and vomiting is defined as persistent or recurrent symptoms lasting more than a month. Common causes include functional disorders like gastroparesis or cyclic vomiting syndrome, as well as structural, metabolic, or neurological conditions. Diagnosis often involves endoscopy, gastric emptying studies, and lab tests to rule out underlying disease. When no clear cause is found, it's often labeled as functional nausea or chronic idiopathic vomiting.

### Treatments
Standard treatment includes antiemetic medications (such as ondansetron and prochlorperazine), prokinetic agents (metoclopramide), and acid suppressors if reflux is present. Dietary modifications and stress management are often recommended, but many patients continue to experience symptoms despite these measures.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Stomach is meant to send its contents downward - like a pot that drains into the intestines. Nausea and vomiting happen when Stomach Qi rebels and moves upward instead. This rebellion rarely occurs in isolation. It is usually driven by a deeper imbalance in the Spleen, Liver, or the presence of pathogenic factors like Dampness or Phlegm that obstruct the Stomach's normal descent.
The Spleen plays a central role. It transforms food into Qi and sends the clear, nourishing part upward. When Spleen Qi is weak - from overwork, poor diet, or chronic illness - this transformation stalls. Undigested food and fluids accumulate, creating Dampness that weighs down the Stomach and forces Qi to rebel. This is why nausea from Spleen Qi Deficiency often worsens after meals and comes with bloating and fatigue.
The Liver is equally important. Its job is to keep Qi flowing smoothly throughout the body. Emotional stress, frustration, or unexpressed anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate. That stuck energy often 'attacks' the Stomach horizontally, disrupting its downward movement. This explains why stress-triggered nausea is so common - the Liver and Stomach are intimately connected, and emotional upset can directly churn the gut.
Other patterns add their own signature. Damp-Heat from rich, greasy foods creates a heavy, sticky nausea with a thick yellow tongue coating. Phlegm-Fluids, born from a weak Spleen that cannot manage fluids, produce a sloshing sensation and vomiting of watery fluid. When Liver Qi stagnation persists, it can generate Fire, leading to burning nausea and a bitter taste. In Stomach Yin Deficiency, the organ's cooling fluids are depleted, causing dry retching and a low-grade burning sensation.
Each pattern requires a different treatment strategy, which is why TCM looks beyond the symptom to the whole picture.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner first listens to the quality of the nausea and what brings it on. In Spleen Qi Deficiency (脾气虚, pí qì xū), the nausea is often mild but persistent, worse after eating, and accompanied by bloating, fatigue, and loose stools. The tongue looks pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels weak. The story usually includes a long period of poor appetite or overwork that drained the digestive fire.
When Liver Qi Stagnation invades the Stomach (肝气犯胃, gān qì fàn wèi), the nausea flares with emotional stress. There may be belching, a distended feeling under the ribs, and frequent sighing. The tongue coating stays thin and white, but the pulse becomes wiry - tight like a guitar string. Asking about recent frustrations or bottled-up anger often reveals the trigger.
Damp-Heat in the Stomach (湿热壅胃, shī rè yōng wèi) creates a heavy, sticky nausea with a sensation of fullness that does not ease after burping. The tongue coating is the giveaway: thick, greasy, and yellow. The pulse feels slippery and rapid. The person may feel worse in humid weather or after rich, fried foods, and may vomit sour or bitter fluid.
In Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach and Small Intestine (痰饮停胃, tán yǐn tíng wèi), the hallmark is a sloshing sound in the stomach and nausea with vomiting of clear, watery fluids. Dizziness and a heavy head often accompany it. The tongue is swollen with a white, slippery coating, and the pulse is slippery. A practitioner may gently tap the abdomen to detect retained fluids.
When prolonged Liver stagnation generates heat, Stagnant Liver Qi turning into Fire (肝郁化火, gān yù huà huǒ) rises to disturb the Stomach. The nausea comes with a burning sensation in the chest, acid reflux, and a bitter taste in the mouth. Irritability and a red face are common. The tongue is red with a yellow coating, and the pulse is wiry and rapid, reflecting the internal heat.
Stomach Yin Deficiency (胃阴虚, wèi yīn xū) appears as dry heaving and mild nausea with a sensation of heat, but little actual vomiting. The person may feel hungry yet have no desire to eat, and craves small sips of water. The tongue is red, dry, and may lack coating entirely. The pulse is thin and rapid. This pattern often follows a long illness or dehydration.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Nausea and vomiting worse after eating or drinking, Bloating and fullness in the abdomen, Poor appetite, Loose or soft stools, Fatigue and lack of energy
- **Worse with**: Cold and raw foods, Overeating, Stress and worry, Overwork or late nights, Damp or cold damp weather
- **Better with**: Warm, easily digestible meals, Ginger tea, Rest after eating, Small, warm meals, Gentle walking or movement after eating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food into Qi and sending it upward. When Spleen Qi is weak, that transformation stalls, leaving undigested food and fluids to accumulate. This stagnation generates Dampness and causes the Stomach's Qi to rebel upward instead of descending, which is what produces the chronic nausea and vomiting-often worse after eating.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with tooth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: weak and soft, especially at the right middle position (Spleen).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen thrives on warmth and regular, moderate nourishment. Cold, raw, and heavy foods tax its weak digestive fire, while overwork and worry deplete Qi further. Warm, easy-to-digest meals and rest allow the Spleen to rebuild its strength and keep Stomach Qi moving downward.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, greasy, and sweet foods that burden the Spleen. Eat small, frequent meals and rest briefly after eating. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow without exhausting the Spleen.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Nausea and belching that worsen with stress or anger, Distending pain in the upper abdomen that spreads to the ribs, Frequent sighing or a sensation of a lump in the throat, Irritability and emotional ups and downs, Acid reflux or sour regurgitation
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or irritability, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Eating while upset or rushed
- **Better with**: Deep breathing, meditation, or calming activities, Warm, easily digestible meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress, frustration, or unexpressed anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate. When the Liver's energy gets stuck, it often 'attacks' the Stomach horizontally, disrupting its normal downward movement. This upward rebellion of Stomach Qi is what produces the persistent nausea, belching, and feeling of fullness under the ribs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, often with redder sides, and a thin white coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, felt most clearly at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly causes Liver Qi to stagnate and then surge horizontally into the Stomach, which is the root mechanism of this pattern. Calming activities release that stuck energy, while warm, simple meals avoid overburdening the already disrupted digestive process. Conversely, anger and heavy foods further agitate the Liver and clog the Stomach's downward movement, making nausea and distension worse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, warm, cooked meals at regular times, and avoid eating when you are upset or rushed. Gentle daily movement like walking or yoga helps keep Liver Qi flowing. Incorporate calming practices such as deep breathing, journaling, or meditation to process emotions before they affect your stomach.

### Damp-Heat in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Feeling of heaviness and fullness in the upper belly, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Loose stools that feel incomplete or sticky, Thirst with little desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, humid weather, Overeating
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Barley or chrysanthemum tea, Cool, dry weather, Gentle walking or movement after eating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat accumulate in the Stomach when rich, greasy foods or hot, humid environments overwhelm the digestive system. Dampness creates a heavy, stagnant feeling that blocks the Stomach's normal downward movement, while Heat adds a burning, inflammatory quality. The result is rebellious Stomach Qi that rises upward as nausea and vomiting, often with a sticky taste and a sense of fullness that does not go away.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑, huá) and rapid (数, shuò), especially strong at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, fried, and spicy foods directly add Dampness and Heat to the body, overwhelming the Stomach and causing Qi to rebel upward. Alcohol and hot, humid environments further fuel Heat and Dampness. Light, bland meals and cooling teas help drain Dampness and clear Heat, allowing Stomach Qi to descend naturally. Gentle movement encourages Qi flow without exhausting the Spleen, while cold drinks and heavy meals create stagnation that worsens the nausea.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible meals such as congee, steamed vegetables, and small portions of lean protein. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sugar-laden foods that create more Dampness and Heat. Stay hydrated with warm water or barley tea, but avoid ice-cold drinks which can further stagnate digestion. Gentle exercise like walking can help Qi move, but avoid heavy exertion in hot, humid weather.

### Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach and Small intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fluids-in-the-stomach-and-small-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Splashing or gurgling sound in the stomach when tapped or moved, Vomiting of thin, clear, watery fluid, Feeling of fullness and hardness below the chest, No thirst, or thirst without desire to drink, Preference for warmth on the abdomen, Dizziness and heaviness in the body
- **Worse with**: Cold and raw foods, Dairy and greasy foods, Overeating, Damp or cold damp weather, Lying down immediately after eating
- **Better with**: Warm compresses on the abdomen, Ginger tea, Small, warm meals, Gentle walking or movement after eating, Resting with head elevated
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and fails to transform fluids, water accumulates in the Stomach and intestines as cold Phlegm-Fluids. These heavy fluids obstruct the Stomach's normal downward movement, causing Qi to rebel upward. The result is chronic nausea and the vomiting of thin, watery fluid, often with a characteristic splashing sound when the belly is moved.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white slippery coating, excessively moist. Pulse: deep (Chen), wiry (Xian), and slippery (Hua), especially slippery in the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods further weaken the Spleen Yang and promote fluid accumulation, while warm foods and warmth help transform fluids. Overeating overwhelms the already weak Spleen, leading to more fluid retention and upward Qi rebellion. Gentle movement encourages Qi circulation and helps the Spleen process fluids, reducing the sloshing sensation and nausea.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and avoid cold, raw, and iced items. Sip ginger tea to warm the Stomach. Limit dairy, sweets, and greasy foods that create Dampness. Have small, frequent meals and take a short walk after eating to help Qi move.

### Stagnant Liver Qi turning into Fire
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stagnant-liver-qi-turning-into-fire)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning sensation in the stomach or chest, Bitter taste in the mouth, Acid reflux or sour regurgitation, Irritability and angry outbursts, Rib-side distension or pain, Constipation
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or irritability, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, stuffy environments
- **Better with**: Cool drinks and foods, Calm, quiet environment, Gentle walking or movement after eating, Deep breathing, meditation, or calming activities
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress and frustration are held in for too long, Liver Qi gets stuck. That stuck energy can eventually generate Heat, which rises like a flame and attacks the Stomach. This rebellious Fire disrupts the Stomach's natural downward movement, causing Qi to surge upward as nausea, acid reflux, and vomiting with a bitter or burning sensation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially at the sides, with a dry yellow coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and rapid (数 shuò), taut like a guitar string.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress, especially anger and frustration, is the primary cause of Liver Qi stagnation that generates Fire. Spicy, greasy foods, alcohol, and coffee add external Heat to the body, further fueling the Fire that rises to the Stomach. Cooling foods and drinks help clear that internal Heat, while calming activities and gentle movement smooth the flow of Liver Qi, preventing it from stagnating and flaring up to cause nausea.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add Heat to the body. Favor cooling, easily digested foods like cucumber, pear, mung beans, and leafy greens. Regular gentle movement like walking or tai chi helps keep Liver Qi flowing smoothly, and finding healthy outlets for stress-such as breathing exercises or journaling-is essential to prevent the Fire from building up.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry retching or nausea without much vomiting, Burning sensation in the upper abdomen, Dry mouth and throat with a desire to sip water, Feeling hungry but no appetite, Dry stools or constipation, Red tongue with little or no coating
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overwork or late nights, Stress and worry, Dry, windy weather
- **Better with**: Sipping warm water or herbal tea, Eating congee or soups, Rest and relaxation, Cool, moist environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Stomach's Yin and fluids are depleted, the organ loses its natural moistening and cooling. This creates an environment of empty heat that irritates the Stomach, causing Qi to rebel upward instead of descending. The result is a persistent, low-grade nausea, often with dry retching, a burning sensation in the upper abdomen, and a strong aversion to dry or spicy foods.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating with possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò), weak at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, fried, and drying foods directly consume Stomach Yin and worsen the empty heat, while moistening foods like congee replenish fluids. Rest and sleep are essential for Yin restoration, and overwork or late nights deplete it further. Warm fluids gently support the Stomach without aggravating the deficiency.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on moist, easy-to-digest foods like congee, soups, steamed vegetables, tofu, and pears. Avoid spicy, fried, and dry foods that consume Yin. Eat smaller, frequent meals and sip warm water throughout the day. Prioritize rest and avoid staying up late, as nighttime is when Yin should be restored.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does TCM treat chronic nausea differently from Western medicine?
Western medicine typically uses medications to block the nausea signal, regardless of the cause. TCM looks for the root imbalance behind the symptom. For example, nausea that worsens after meals and comes with fatigue points to Spleen Qi Deficiency and is treated with strengthening herbs. Nausea triggered by stress and accompanied by belching points to Liver Qi invading the Stomach and is treated with soothing, regulating herbs. This means the treatment is tailored to you, not just the symptom.

### Can acupuncture really stop nausea?
Yes. Acupuncture points like Pericardium 6 (Neiguan, on the inner wrist) and Stomach 36 (Zusanli, below the knee) have a well-documented effect on regulating Stomach Qi and calming the vomiting reflex. Many patients feel relief during or shortly after a session. For chronic nausea, weekly acupuncture combined with herbal medicine provides the most lasting results by correcting the underlying pattern.

### Will I need to take herbs forever?
No. Herbal formulas are a tool to rebalance your system, not a lifelong prescription. For excess patterns, treatment may last 4 to 8 weeks. For deficiency patterns that require rebuilding digestive strength, it may take 3 to 6 months. Once the pattern resolves and symptoms stabilize, herbs are tapered and stopped. Many people keep a simple formula on hand for occasional flare-ups, but daily use is not typical.

### Can I combine TCM with my anti-nausea medication?
Yes, in most cases. TCM herbs and acupuncture can work alongside conventional antiemetics. However, you must inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about everything you are taking. Some herbs may interact with medications, so a qualified practitioner will adjust your formula accordingly. Never stop prescribed medication abruptly - work with your doctor to taper if your symptoms improve.

### What foods should I avoid if I have chronic nausea?
In general, avoid raw, cold, greasy, and spicy foods, which burden the Spleen and generate Dampness. Dairy, fried foods, and iced drinks are common triggers. Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and congee. Ginger tea is a universal digestive aid. Eating small, frequent meals and chewing thoroughly also helps the Stomach do its job with less effort.

### Why does my nausea get worse when I'm stressed?
In TCM, the Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi. When you are stressed or frustrated, Liver Qi stagnates. Because the Liver and Stomach are closely connected, that stuck energy can attack the Stomach, disrupting its downward movement and causing nausea, belching, and a distended feeling under the ribs. This is why calming the mind and soothing the Liver are central to treatment for stress-related nausea.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese Herbal Medicine Liu Jun Zi Tang and Xiang Sha Liu Jun Zi Tang for Functional Dyspepsia: Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials** ([Link](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3530827/))
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang S, et al. Chinese Herbal Medicine Liu Jun Zi Tang and Xiang Sha Liu Jun Zi Tang for Functional Dyspepsia: Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2012;2012:936459.*
   This meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of Liu Jun Zi Tang and Xiang Sha Liu Jun Zi Tang for functional dyspepsia, a condition in which chronic nausea is a common symptom. The analysis of multiple RCTs found that these formulas significantly improved overall dyspepsia symptoms, including nausea, bloating, and epigastric discomfort, compared to placebo or prokinetic drugs. The effect was attributed to the formulas' ability to strengthen the Spleen, transform Dampness, and descend rebellious Stomach Qi.

2. **Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Lan L, et al. Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis. World J Gastroenterol. 2014;20(7):1862-73.*
   This systematic review assessed acupuncture's effectiveness for functional dyspepsia, a common cause of chronic nausea. Acupuncture was found to be superior to sham acupuncture and as effective as conventional prokinetic medications in reducing dyspepsia symptoms, including nausea and postprandial fullness. The Neiguan PC-6 and Zusanli ST-36 points were most frequently used.
