## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, jaundice is a sign of elevated bilirubin in the blood, causing a yellow discoloration of the skin, whites of the eyes, and mucous membranes. It typically indicates an underlying problem with the liver, gallbladder, or red blood cells. The three main categories are pre-hepatic (excessive breakdown of red blood cells), hepatic (liver cell damage from hepatitis, cirrhosis, or medications), and post-hepatic (obstruction of the bile ducts, often from gallstones or tumors).
Diagnosis usually involves blood tests to measure bilirubin and liver enzymes, along with imaging such as ultrasound or CT scans to visualize the bile ducts. Treatment is directed at the root cause - antivirals for hepatitis, surgery for obstructions, or phototherapy for newborn jaundice. Jaundice itself is a symptom, not a disease, and its resolution depends on correcting the underlying issue.

### Treatments
Conventional management targets the specific cause. For viral hepatitis, antiviral medications or supportive care are used. Obstructive jaundice from gallstones often requires endoscopic removal or surgery. Autoimmune liver conditions may be treated with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. In cases of drug-induced liver injury, the offending medication is stopped. For newborn jaundice, phototherapy is the standard. While these interventions can be lifesaving, they focus on the structural or infectious trigger rather than the individual's overall constitutional terrain.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, jaundice is understood as a disorder of dampness. The root is always the accumulation of pathological dampness that obstructs the normal flow of bile, forcing it to seep into the bloodstream and stain the tissues. But dampness rarely acts alone - it combines with heat, cold, or stagnation to produce the distinct clinical pictures we call patterns. The Liver and Gallbladder are the organs most directly involved because they govern the free flow of Qi and the storage and excretion of bile. When dampness clogs this system, the result is jaundice.
The Spleen plays an equally critical role. It is responsible for transforming and transporting fluids; when the Spleen is weak - whether from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness - it fails to manage moisture, and dampness accumulates. This is why many people with jaundice also experience bloating, heavy limbs, and fatigue. The interplay between the Liver, Gallbladder, and Spleen explains why the same Western diagnosis can present so differently: a robust person with a rich diet might develop damp-heat jaundice (bright yellow, irritable, greasy tongue), while a constitutionally weak person might develop a spleen-deficiency dampness pattern (pale yellow, exhausted, poor appetite).
Clinically, TCM first distinguishes between yang jaundice and yin jaundice. Yang jaundice is acute, with a vivid orange-yellow hue, and is driven by damp-heat or toxic-heat - it is an excess condition. Yin jaundice is chronic, with a dull, sallow, or dark yellow color, and stems from cold-damp, spleen deficiency, or blood stasis - a mixed excess-deficiency picture. This distinction is the compass that guides all treatment decisions.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
In Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat, the most common cause of jaundice, the skin and eyes take on a bright, vivid yellow like a tangerine. The person usually feels hot, thirsty, and irritable. The tongue is red with a thick, greasy yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid. This pattern is a classic yang jaundice driven by damp-heat obstructing bile flow.
When Damp-Heat in the Gallbladder is dominant, the jaundice is also bright yellow, but the hallmark is pain or distension under the right ribs and a bitter taste in the mouth. The tongue is red with a yellow coat, and the pulse is wiry, slippery, and rapid. The right rib pain and bitter taste help distinguish this pattern from more general liver damp-heat.
Cold-Damp invading the Spleen produces a different picture: the yellow is dull, sallow, and lackluster, like a faded lemon. The person feels cold, especially in the limbs, and may have poor appetite and loose stools. The tongue is pale and swollen with a white, greasy coat, and the pulse is deep and slow. This is a yin jaundice pattern, where cold and dampness obstruct the spleen and liver.
Spleen Deficiency with Dampness also causes a pale, lusterless yellow hue, but the main feature is fatigue, weak digestion, and a general sense of heaviness. The tongue is pale with a thin white coating, and the pulse is weak and thready. Unlike cold-damp, there is less coldness and more signs of qi deficiency, pointing to the spleen’s failure to transform fluids and contain bile.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-and-gallbladder-damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bright yellow skin and eyes (yang jaundice), Pain or distension under the right ribs, Bitter taste in the mouth, Dark yellow, scanty urine, Fever or alternating chills and fever, Irritability and restlessness
- **Worse with**: Alcohol, Greasy, fried, or rich food, Spicy, hot foods, Stress and anger
- **Better with**: Rest in a cool environment, Light, cooling foods, Plenty of water
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat combine and lodge in the Liver and Gallbladder, choking the flow of Qi and bile. The bile, unable to drain properly, spills into the bloodstream and surfaces as the bright, orange-tinged yellow of yang jaundice. The stuck Liver Qi creates the characteristic right-side rib pain, distension, and bitter taste.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with redder sides and a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: wiry, slippery, and rapid (弦滑数 xián huá shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Alcohol, greasy food, and heat generate dampness and heat, which are the very substances that clog the Liver and Gallbladder in this pattern. Stress and anger cause Liver Qi to stagnate, fanning the heat and worsening bile stasis. Cooling, light foods and rest reduce the damp-heat load, allowing the Liver to regain its smooth flow and the bile to drain properly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, cooling foods like mung beans, bitter gourd, cucumber, and watermelon. Avoid alcohol, fried food, rich meats, and spicy dishes. Gentle daily movement helps Qi flow, but rest when feverish.

### Damp-Heat in the Gallbladder
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-gallbladder)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pain or distension under the right ribs, Bitter taste in the mouth, Bright yellow skin and eyes (like an orange peel), Aversion to greasy or oily food, Dark, scanty urine, Nausea and vomiting
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or rich food, Alcohol, Spicy, hot foods, Hot, humid weather, Stress and anger
- **Better with**: Cool, bitter foods (e.g., cucumber, dandelion greens), Light, easily digested meals, Rest in a cool environment, Gentle walking or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When dampness and heat combine and settle in the gallbladder, they obstruct the normal flow of bile. The bile then backs up and overflows into the blood, staining the skin and eyes a bright, fresh yellow. This is the classic “yang jaundice” - it appears relatively quickly and the yellow is vivid, like an orange peel.
The gallbladder sits under the right ribs, so the trapped damp-heat creates a characteristic pain or distension there. The heat rises into the mouth, leaving a bitter taste, while the dampness drags the body down, causing heaviness and sticky stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen, with redder sides; yellow, greasy (sticky) coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), slippery (滑 huá), and rapid (数 shuò); the wiry quality is most noticeable at the left middle (Guan) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-heat is a heavy, sticky, hot pathogen. Rich, greasy, and spicy foods add more dampness and heat to the body, directly feeding the problem. Alcohol is pure heat and dampness in a cup. Conversely, cool, bitter foods naturally clear heat and drain dampness. Rest in a cool environment prevents external heat from adding to the internal fire. Gentle movement keeps Qi and fluids circulating, preventing dampness from settling and stagnating in the gallbladder.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, cooling foods like cucumber, celery, mung beans, and bitter greens. Avoid alcohol, deep-fried foods, rich dairy, and spicy dishes completely while recovering. Gentle daily movement helps Qi and dampness move, but rest when fatigued.

### Cold-Damp invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-damp-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, sallow yellow skin and eyes, Heavy, sluggish feeling in the body and limbs, Bloating, fullness, and poor appetite, Loose or watery stools, Thick white greasy tongue coating, Pale, swollen tongue with tooth marks
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw, or iced foods, Damp, humid weather, Overeating or heavy, greasy meals, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged inactivity, Exposure to cold
- **Better with**: Warmth, Light, cooked meals, Gentle walking or exercise, Rest, Dry environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Cold and Dampness build up in the body, they smother the Spleen's ability to transform and transport fluids. This heavy, sluggish energy blocks the Liver's smooth flow, trapping bile and forcing it to seep into the bloodstream. Because the underlying force is cold and stagnant rather than hot and active, the resulting jaundice appears dull, sallow, and shadowy instead of bright yellow.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with tooth marks; thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: soggy (濡 ru) or slippery (滑 hua) and slow (迟 chi).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and Dampness are the root cause of this pattern, so anything that adds more cold or moisture-like raw foods, cold weather, or lack of movement-worsens the stagnation. Warmth and dryness act like a gentle fire that evaporates the dampness and thaws the cold, allowing the Spleen to resume its job of transforming fluids and the Liver to move bile smoothly again. Light, cooked foods and gentle activity support this process without overwhelming the weakened digestive system.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Gentle daily movement, such as walking, helps circulate Qi and dry dampness, but rest when tired. Keep the abdomen warm and avoid damp living environments.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, sallow yellow skin and eyes (not bright), Loose, unformed stools or diarrhea, Heavy, tired limbs and body, Bloating and fullness after eating, Poor appetite with no taste, Foggy head, drowsiness, especially after meals
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw, or iced foods, Overeating or heavy, greasy meals, Damp, humid weather, Overwork and exhaustion
- **Better with**: Warm, easy-to-digest meals, Rest and adequate sleep, Gentle walking or exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform and transport fluids properly. This allows dampness to accumulate inside the body. The stagnant dampness presses on the bile pathways, causing bile to seep outward into the skin and eyes, producing a pale, sallow, lusterless yellow discoloration typical of yin jaundice.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks, white greasy moist coating. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slowed-down (Huan), possibly slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods dampen the Spleen's digestive fire, directly worsening the root deficiency and allowing more dampness to form. Overeating overwhelms the already weak Spleen, creating more stagnation. Rest conserves Qi, allowing the Spleen to recover its transforming ability, while warm, cooked foods provide nourishment without adding dampness. Gentle movement prevents Qi stagnation but does not drain energy.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like congee, stews, and roasted vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and sweet foods that create more dampness. Small, frequent meals are easier on a weak Spleen. Gentle daily walking helps move Qi and dry dampness.

### Toxic-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/toxic-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden onset of deep golden-yellow skin and eyes, like fresh gold, High fever with intense thirst and a desire for cold drinks, Bleeding tendency: nosebleeds, bleeding gums, or small purple spots under the skin, Restlessness, irritability, or mental confusion, Dark, scanty urine and constipation
- **Worse with**: Alcohol and spicy, greasy food, Stress and anger, Overwork and exhaustion, Hot, stuffy environments
- **Better with**: Cool, quiet environment, Drinking cooling herbal teas (chrysanthemum, dandelion), Light, bland congee, Complete bed rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In Toxic-Heat jaundice, a sudden invasion of epidemic heat toxin strikes the body with overwhelming force. This fierce pathogen directly attacks the Liver and Gallbladder, forcing bile to spill into the blood rather than flowing its normal path. The result is a rapid, deep golden-yellow discoloration of the skin and eyes, often accompanied by high fever and systemic poisoning.
Because the toxin is so aggressive, it can also damage the blood vessels, causing bleeding under the skin or from the nose and gums, and cloud the mind, leading to confusion or drowsiness. This pattern is the most critical form of jaundice and requires urgent medical attention.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red or crimson body with prickly thorns, dry yellow coating (may become dry and black in severe cases). Pulse: Rapid (数 shuò) and forceful, often overflowing (洪 hóng) or slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Toxic heat is an extreme fire pathogen that consumes body fluids, damages the blood, and disturbs the mind. Cooling herbs and a calm environment directly counteract this fire, while rest preserves the body's defensive Qi so it can fight the toxin. Alcohol, spicy food, and emotional stress add more heat to an already overheated system, worsening the poisoning and potentially leading to life-threatening complications.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: This is a medical emergency - always seek immediate professional care. At home, sip cooling fluids like chrysanthemum tea, dandelion tea, or mung bean soup. Avoid all alcohol, fried foods, spicy dishes, and red meat. Rest completely in a cool, quiet room.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark, gloomy yellow discoloration of skin and eyes, Fixed, stabbing pain in the right upper abdomen, Pain that worsens at night, Dark purple tongue with stasis spots, Palpable hard mass in the upper abdomen
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged inactivity, Emotional stress, Greasy, fried, or rich food
- **Better with**: Warm compress on the abdomen, Gentle walking or exercise, Rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi and blood flow become chronically sluggish, blood can stagnate in the liver and bile ducts. This blockage traps bile, forcing it to seep into the bloodstream and tissues, producing a dark, gloomy jaundice. The fixed, stabbing pain is a direct result of blood stasis, which typically worsens at night when circulation slows further.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩, Sè), wiry (弦, Xián) or firm (牢, Láo).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis is a condition of sluggish circulation. Warmth and gentle movement help to disperse stasis, while cold and inactivity congeal it further. Stress causes Liver Qi stagnation, which is the root of blood stasis; fatty foods burden the liver and gallbladder, worsening the blockage.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, warm meals and avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that burden the liver. Gentle daily exercise like walking or tai chi helps move Qi and blood. Manage stress through relaxation techniques.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How does TCM treat jaundice differently from Western medicine?
Western medicine identifies the structural or infectious cause of jaundice - a gallstone, a virus, a medication - and treats that cause directly. TCM looks at the whole person and asks: what kind of dampness is blocking the bile flow, and which organs are weak or out of balance? Treatment is then customized to clear that specific type of dampness (heat, cold, or stagnation) while strengthening the Spleen and Liver. This means two people with the same bilirubin level might receive completely different herbal formulas and acupuncture points because their patterns are different.

### Can I take Chinese herbs while I'm on medication for my liver condition?
Yes, in most cases, but it must be done under professional supervision. Your TCM practitioner needs to know every medication you are taking, and you should inform your doctor about any herbs you start. Some herbs used for jaundice, like Yin Chen, are generally safe but could theoretically interact with drugs processed by the liver. A trained practitioner will select a formula that avoids known interactions and supports your liver function. Never stop or adjust your conventional medication without your doctor's approval.

### How long does it take for the yellow color to go away with TCM?
The timeline depends on the pattern. Acute damp-heat jaundice often begins to fade within two to four weeks of consistent treatment. Chronic yin jaundice from spleen deficiency or blood stasis may take two to three months or longer, because the goal is to rebuild the body's foundation, not just drain the yellow. You'll typically notice the urine becoming lighter first, then the eyes, and finally the skin. Regular blood tests will also show improvement in bilirubin levels.

### Is acupuncture safe for someone with jaundice?
Yes, acupuncture is very safe when performed by a licensed practitioner using sterile, single-use needles. It can help reduce stress on the liver, improve bile flow, and alleviate symptoms like fatigue, nausea, and rib pain. However, if your jaundice is caused by a condition that affects blood clotting (such as advanced cirrhosis), you should discuss this with both your doctor and acupuncturist, as certain points may need to be avoided.

### What foods should I avoid if I have jaundice?
The most important foods to avoid are those that create dampness and heat: alcohol, greasy or deep-fried foods, rich dairy products, and sugary desserts. Spicy foods can also aggravate damp-heat patterns. Cold and raw foods, like salads and iced drinks, can weaken the Spleen and worsen yin jaundice. Focus on simple, cooked meals - congee, soups, and steamed vegetables - that are easy to digest and don't burden your liver.

### Does TCM work for newborn jaundice?
Newborn jaundice is a specific condition that requires careful evaluation, and we have a dedicated page for it. In general, mild physiological jaundice in newborns is often managed with phototherapy in conventional medicine. TCM may offer gentle herbal baths or maternal dietary adjustments (if breastfeeding) under the guidance of a pediatric TCM specialist, but any jaundice in a newborn should be monitored by a pediatrician first to rule out serious causes.
