## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Chronic gastrointestinal bleeding refers to slow, often unnoticed blood loss from the digestive tract - anywhere from the stomach to the colon. It may show up as dark, tarry stools (melena), blood mixed with stool, or only as iron-deficiency anemia with fatigue and pallor. Common causes include peptic ulcers, gastritis, esophagitis, inflammatory bowel disease, or small growths in the gut. Diagnosis usually involves fecal occult blood tests, endoscopy, or colonoscopy to locate the source of the bleed.

### Treatments
Conventional management focuses on stopping the bleeding and healing the underlying lesion. This often includes acid-suppressing medications like proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, or antacids to protect the stomach lining. Endoscopic procedures can cauterize or clip bleeding vessels. Iron supplements address anemia. If bleeding is due to an ulcer, antibiotics may be used to eradicate H. pylori. In some cases, surgery is needed to remove a source of persistent bleeding.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Spleen is the organ most closely tied to chronic bleeding. It governs the transformation of food into Qi and Blood, and it also has the job of holding Blood inside the vessels. When Spleen Qi becomes weak - from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness - it loses this grip. Blood seeps out slowly into the digestive tract, producing dark, tarry stools and leaving the person deeply fatigued. This is the Spleen not controlling Blood pattern.
Heat is another major player. When the Stomach or Liver carries excessive Heat, it can agitate the blood and force it out of the vessels, much like boiling water overflows a pot. This tends to produce brighter red blood, often in vomit, accompanied by burning pain, thirst, and a bitter taste. Stomach Fire and Liver Fire Blazing are the two main Heat patterns, and they often flare up after spicy food, alcohol, or emotional stress.
Blood can also fail to move smoothly. In Stomach Blood Stagnation, sluggish circulation makes the vessels fragile and prone to rupture. The bleeding is dark and often clotted, with a fixed, stabbing pain that worsens after eating. Less commonly, Stomach Yin Deficiency - a drying out of the stomach's protective lining - leads to slow, scant oozing, while Damp-Heat brews turbid heat that erodes the gut lining from within. Because the same Western diagnosis can have such different roots, TCM treatment always starts with identifying the exact pattern behind the bleed.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first asks about the color, volume, and timing of the bleeding. Bright red blood that appears suddenly suggests Heat forcing blood out of the vessels, while dark, tarry, or slow oozing points to a deficiency pattern where the Spleen cannot hold blood in place. The practitioner also looks at the tongue and feels the pulse to confirm the underlying disharmony.
In the Spleen not controlling Blood pattern, the bleeding is chronic and the person feels deeply fatigued with a poor appetite. The stools are often dark and tarry, and the complexion is pale or sallow. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak and thready, reflecting Qi that is too depleted to govern the blood.
When Stomach Fire is the culprit, the bleeding is more active, often with bright or dark red vomited blood. There is burning epigastric pain, thirst, and a bitter taste. The tongue is red with a thick yellow coat, and the pulse is rapid and slippery. If Liver Fire Blazing is involved, similar heat signs appear but with added irritability, rib-side pain, and a wiry, rapid pulse.
In Stomach Blood Stagnation, the hallmark is a fixed, stabbing pain in the stomach area, and the blood may appear dark with clots. The tongue is purplish or shows stasis spots, and the pulse is choppy. This pattern often develops after long-standing heat or Qi stagnation damages the local collaterals.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen not controlling Blood
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-not-controlling-blood)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark or tarry blood in stool, Overwhelming fatigue and weakness, Poor appetite and loose stools, Pale, sallow complexion, Dizziness and shortness of breath
- **Worse with**: Overwork and exhaustion, Cold, raw foods and drinks, Irregular eating habits, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Warm, easy-to-digest meals, Adequate rest and sleep, Small, frequent meals, Gentle warmth on abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen holds blood inside the vessels. When Spleen Qi becomes weak, it loses this grip, and blood slowly seeps into the digestive tract. This is why the bleeding tends to be gradual and the blood often appears dark or mixed with stool, rather than bright red.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen's Qi is easily drained by overwork, irregular eating, and cold foods, which directly weaken its ability to hold blood. Rest and warm, cooked meals conserve and replenish Spleen Qi, allowing it to recover its governing function. Stress knots the Liver Qi, which then overacts on the Spleen, so calming the mind also protects the Spleen.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like congee, stews, and soups. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork, as fatigue drains Qi further.

### Stomach Fire (Stomach Heat)
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-fire-stomach-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning pain in the stomach area, Vomiting bright red blood or passing dark tarry stools, Thirst with desire for cold drinks, Red tongue with thick dry yellow coating, Rapid, slippery, forceful pulse
- **Worse with**: Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress and anger, Hot weather or overheating
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and drinks, Rest and quiet, Cold compresses on the stomach
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When excessive Heat accumulates in the Stomach, it scorches the blood vessels in the stomach lining, causing them to rupture and bleed. This heat also drives blood to move recklessly, leading to bright red or dark red blood in vomit or stool. The burning pain and thirst for cold drinks are signs of the internal fire.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly with central prickles, and a thick dry yellow coating. Pulse: rapid (数 shuò), slippery (滑 huá), and full or forceful, especially strong at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy, greasy, and fried foods, along with alcohol, directly generate Heat in the Stomach, which is the root cause of this pattern. Emotional stress and anger can cause Liver Qi stagnation that transforms into Fire, which then invades the Stomach. Cooling foods and drinks help to clear the Heat and soothe the burning sensation. Rest and quiet prevent further agitation of Qi and allow the body's cooling mechanisms to work. Cold compresses locally draw heat away from the Stomach area.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which all add heat to the Stomach. Eat cooling, moistening foods such as cucumber, watermelon, pear, mung beans, and tofu. Drink plenty of water at room temperature or cool. Manage stress through relaxation techniques, as emotional upset can stir up fire.

### Liver Fire Blazing
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-fire-blazing)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bitter taste in the mouth, Intense irritability and quick temper, Throbbing headache at the temples, Red, painful, or burning eyes, Dark or bright red blood in vomit or stool, Burning pain along the ribs
- **Worse with**: Anger, frustration, or emotional stress, Spicy or greasy food, Alcohol and coffee, Overwork and late nights, Hot, stuffy environments
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and bitter greens, Stress reduction and meditation, Gentle outdoor walks, Chrysanthemum or peppermint tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Liver Fire blazes, it can invade the Stomach and disrupt the normal downward flow of Qi, forcing blood to move recklessly upward or into the stool. The intense heat damages the blood vessels in the digestive tract, causing bleeding that may appear as dark stools or vomiting of blood. This pattern often arises from prolonged anger, frustration, or stress that turns into internal fire.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, redder at the sides, dry yellow coating, possibly with red prickles. Pulse: wiry, rapid, and forceful, especially at the left middle (Liver) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Anger and emotional stress directly fan Liver fire, causing it to surge upward and invade the Stomach, which forces blood out of the vessels. Spicy, greasy, and heating foods add fuel to the internal fire, while alcohol and coffee overstimulate and dry out the system. Cooling foods and bitter herbs help drain excess heat, and calming practices like meditation soothe the Liver, allowing Qi to descend and blood to stay within its pathways. Adequate rest gives the Liver time to store blood and recover, preventing the restlessness that leads to bleeding.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which add heat and fuel the fire. Eat cooling, bitter greens like dandelion leaves, cucumber, and mung beans. Practice stress-reducing activities like meditation, gentle walking, or deep breathing to prevent anger from flaring up. Keep a regular sleep schedule to allow the Liver to rest and regenerate.

### Stomach Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sharp stabbing or cutting pain in the upper abdomen, Pain fixed in one location that does not move, Pain worsens with pressure on the abdomen, Pain worsens after eating, Black tarry stools, Vomiting of dark or coffee-ground material, Dark or purplish lips and dull complexion
- **Worse with**: Heavy or greasy meals, Pressure on the abdomen, Cold foods and drinks, Stress and anger, Night time
- **Better with**: Warmth on the abdomen, Rest, Small, light meals, Gentle walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow in the stomach collaterals becomes sluggish and stagnant, the vessels become fragile and prone to rupture. This leads to slow, dark bleeding into the digestive tract, often manifesting as black tarry stools or coffee-ground vomit. The fixed, stabbing pain is a hallmark of stasis, and the dark color of the blood reflects that it is old and clotted.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple or dusky body, possible purple stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coating. Pulse: choppy (涩 Sè) or wiry (弦 Xián).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold congeals blood and worsens stasis, while warmth and gentle movement promote circulation. Heavy meals and pressure obstruct Qi and blood flow, intensifying the fixed pain. Stress and anger stagnate Liver Qi, which can further block Stomach collaterals. Rest and light meals allow the Stomach to recover without overburdening it, and warmth helps dissolve stasis to ease bleeding and pain.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold and raw foods that congeal blood, and steer clear of greasy or spicy foods that create stagnation. Eat warm, light, easy-to-digest meals in small portions. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi and blood flow, but avoid heavy lifting or intense exercise that may aggravate pain. Manage stress with relaxation techniques, as emotional tension can worsen Qi stagnation.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Scant, slow bleeding (dark stools or occult blood), Dry mouth and throat, thirst for small sips, Gnawing discomfort or dull burning pain in the upper abdomen, Feeling hungry but reluctant to eat, Red tongue with little or no coating, dry center
- **Worse with**: Spicy, fried, or greasy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Stress and overwork, Late nights and insufficient sleep, Dry, rough foods
- **Better with**: Sipping warm fluids, Moistening foods (pear, white fungus soup), Small frequent meals, Rest and calm, Cool environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Stomach Yin is the cooling, moistening juice that protects the stomach lining. When it is depleted by poor diet, stress, or chronic illness, the mucosa becomes dry and fragile like a parched riverbed. This fragility allows small amounts of blood to ooze out slowly, producing dark, tarry stools or occult bleeding rather than large hemorrhages.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating with possible cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin is the cooling, moistening substance that protects the lining. Spicy, fried, and alcohol create heat and dry up Yin, making the lining more fragile and prone to bleeding. Stress and late nights consume Yin further. Moistening foods and sipping fluids rebuild Yin and soothe the stomach, while rest allows the body to regenerate. Small meals reduce the digestive burden, preventing further irritation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat soft, moist, and easily digestible foods like congee, steamed pears, and white fungus soup. Avoid spicy, fried, and dry foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which deplete Yin. Sip warm water throughout the day rather than drinking large amounts at once. Rest is essential; go to bed early to allow Yin to regenerate. Manage stress with gentle activities like walking or meditation.

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and fullness in the upper abdomen, Sticky or bitter taste in the mouth, Dark, tarry stools with a foul smell, Heavy feeling in the body and limbs, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Nausea or poor appetite, Burning sensation in the stomach
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried foods, Spicy foods, Alcohol, Humid weather, Overeating, Stress
- **Better with**: Light, bland diet, Cooling herbal teas, Rest, Gentle exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Damp-Heat brews in the Stomach and Spleen when heavy, greasy foods and humid environments overwhelm the digestive system. The turbid heat irritates and erodes the stomach and intestinal lining, causing tiny blood vessels to break and ooze blood slowly into the digestive tract. The Dampness makes the bleeding sticky and slow, while the Heat adds a burning sensation and darkens the blood.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, thick yellow greasy coating especially at centre and root. Pulse: soggy (Ru), rapid (Shu), and slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, spicy, and heavy foods directly add Dampness and Heat to the body, feeding the very environment that erodes the gut lining. Alcohol and humid weather further worsen Damp-Heat. Cooling, bland foods and herbal teas help clear Heat and drain Dampness. Rest allows the Spleen to recover its transport function, while gentle movement prevents stagnation without overtaxing the digestive system.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and barley. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods that create Damp-Heat. Limit alcohol and coffee. Include cooling, damp-draining foods such as mung beans, cucumber, and wax gourd. Gentle daily movement like walking helps the Spleen transform Dampness.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM stop my bleeding quickly?
Yes, for many patterns the bleeding can be brought under control within a week or two. Herbs like San Qi (notoginseng) and Bai Ji (bletilla) have direct hemostatic effects. However, stopping the bleeding is only the first step - the underlying pattern must be corrected to prevent recurrence, which takes longer.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs if I'm on blood thinners?
Some TCM herbs, such as San Qi and Dang Gui, can affect blood clotting. If you are taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications, you must inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor. They can adjust formulas or monitor you more closely. Never stop or change your medication without medical guidance.

### What foods should I avoid?
Avoid spicy, greasy, deep-fried, and very cold or raw foods, as they can irritate the digestive lining or worsen Heat and Dampness. Alcohol, coffee, and strong tea should be minimized. Instead, eat warm, cooked, easily digestible meals like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Small, frequent meals are gentler on the stomach.

### How long will I need treatment?
Acute bleeding can be controlled in days to a couple of weeks. To fully rebuild the Spleen's ability to contain blood or to clear deep-seated Heat or Stasis, most people need 3 to 6 months of consistent herbal therapy and dietary changes. Acupuncture is often used weekly at first, then spaced out.

### Can acupuncture help with chronic GI bleeding?
Yes. Acupuncture can help regulate the flow of Qi and Blood, strengthen the Spleen, clear Heat, and calm the mind. Points like Zusanli (ST-36) and Sanyinjiao (SP-6) are commonly used to tonify the Spleen and stop bleeding. It is a supportive treatment, usually combined with herbal therapy for best results.

### Will the bleeding come back after TCM treatment?
TCM aims to correct the underlying imbalance, so the risk of recurrence is lower once the pattern is resolved. However, if the lifestyle or dietary factors that caused the imbalance return, the bleeding can recur. Your practitioner will guide you on long-term dietary and self-care habits to maintain digestive health.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage**
   *Journal/Publication: Liu J, Wang L, Yang K, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2012; (4): CD008311.*
   A Cochrane systematic review evaluating the efficacy and safety of Chinese herbal medicines for acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding. The review included 12 randomized trials with over 1,000 participants and found that some herbal preparations, when added to conventional treatment, may increase the rate of hemostasis and reduce the need for surgery. However, the evidence was rated low-quality due to risk of bias and small sample sizes.
