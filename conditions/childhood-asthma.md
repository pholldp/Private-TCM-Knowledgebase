## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways that causes them to narrow, swell, and produce extra mucus. This makes breathing difficult and triggers coughing, wheezing, and shortness of breath. In children, asthma is often triggered by respiratory infections, allergens (like pollen or dust mites), cold air, exercise, or stress. Diagnosis is usually based on symptom history, physical exam, and lung function tests such as spirometry when the child is old enough. Conventional treatment aims to control inflammation and open the airways, but it does not address why some children are more susceptible than others.

### Treatments
Standard treatment for childhood asthma typically involves two types of medications: quick-relief (rescue) inhalers containing short-acting beta-agonists like albuterol to relax airway muscles during an attack, and long-term control medications such as inhaled corticosteroids to reduce airway inflammation. Additional options include leukotriene modifiers, long-acting bronchodilators, and allergy medications. Severe cases may require oral corticosteroids or biologic therapies. Avoidance of triggers and a written asthma action plan are also key components of management.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, childhood asthma is understood as a condition rooted in "hidden phlegm" (伏痰, fú tán) - a sticky, pathological substance that quietly accumulates in the lungs because of immature digestion and weak organ function. Children's Spleen and Lungs are not yet fully developed, making them prone to poor fluid metabolism. When fluids aren't transformed properly, they congeal into phlegm that sits in the airways, setting the stage for asthma.
An acute asthma attack is seen as an external trigger - such as wind-cold or wind-heat from a respiratory infection, cold air, or allergens - stirring up this dormant phlegm. The phlegm rises, combines with Qi, and obstructs the airways, causing the characteristic wheeze, cough, and breathlessness. This is why many children seem fine between attacks but flare up with every cold: the hidden phlegm is always there, waiting to be provoked.
TCM further differentiates asthma into several patterns based on the nature of the phlegm and the child's underlying constitution. If the phlegm is cold and thin, with symptoms worse in cold weather, it's a Cold-Phlegm pattern. If the phlegm is thick, yellow, and accompanied by fever and thirst, it's Phlegm-Heat.
Between attacks, many children show signs of deficiency: a pale face, poor appetite, and frequent colds point to Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency - the root cause of why phlegm keeps forming. In more chronic cases, the Kidneys may be too weak to "grasp" the breath, leading to severe wheezing on exertion.
This is why TCM does not have a single "asthma formula." A child with cold-induced wheezing needs warming, phlegm-resolving herbs; a child with heat needs cooling, lung-clearing herbs; a child with a weak constitution needs strengthening tonics. By treating both the acute branch (the attack) and the chronic root (the hidden phlegm and organ weakness), TCM aims to reduce the frequency and severity of episodes and, over time, help the child outgrow the condition altogether.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first asks about the phlegm and the triggers. If wheezing starts after exposure to cold air and the child coughs up thin, white, frothy sputum, the pattern is likely Cold-Phlegm in the Lungs. The tongue will be pale with a white coating, and the pulse feels floating and tight. These signs show that wind-cold has stirred up internal phlegm, blocking the airways.
When the asthma follows a respiratory infection and the child brings up thick, sticky, yellow phlegm, the picture shifts to Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. You will also see a red face, possibly fever, and a red tongue with a yellow greasy coat. The pulse is rapid and slippery. This tells the practitioner that heat and phlegm are combining to obstruct the lung’s descending function.
Between attacks, many children show a pale, tired look with poor appetite, loose stools, and spontaneous sweating. This points to Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency, a root pattern where the body fails to transform fluids, creating the phlegm that underlies asthma. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak. The child may have a chronic mild cough and catch colds easily.
In more chronic cases, the child may feel cold, especially in the limbs, and become breathless with even slight exertion. Nighttime urination increases. This suggests Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency, where the warming and transforming functions are weakened. The tongue is pale and puffy with a white coat, and the pulse is deep and slow. This pattern indicates a deeper level of deficiency that makes asthma harder to resolve.
When a child has a long history of asthma and develops dry cough, night sweats, and flushed cheeks, the practitioner suspects Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire. The tongue is red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. The yin fluids are depleted, leading to dryness and false heat that irritates the airways, causing a persistent, non-productive wheeze.
Finally, if the child has had asthma for years and wheezing clearly worsens with movement, accompanied by a pale face and a deep, weak pulse, the diagnosis is Kidneys failing to receive Qi. The kidneys normally grasp the lung’s Qi downward, but here they are too weak to do so. The practitioner will also note a hollow, breathless quality to the voice and a tendency to fatigue quickly.

### Cold-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious white frothy or watery phlegm, Wheezing with rattling sound in throat, Feeling cold, cold hands and feet, Chest tightness, difficulty lying flat, No thirst, prefers warm drinks, Symptoms worsen with cold weather or cold drinks
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Cold drinks, Dairy products, Raw foods, Exposure to wind
- **Better with**: Warm drinks, Keeping chest warm, Steam inhalation, Rest in a warm room, Ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In children, the Lungs and Spleen are often still developing, which makes it easy for fluids to gather and turn into phlegm that lies hidden in the airways. When wind-cold invades the body, it stirs up this dormant phlegm, causing the bronchial tubes to tighten and fill with mucus. The result is the classic cold-type asthma attack: wheezing, a rattling cough, and lots of white frothy phlegm.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: slippery and slow.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold constricts the airways and congeals phlegm, making it stickier and harder to cough up. Warmth and warm fluids help liquefy phlegm and encourage it to move, while avoiding dairy and raw foods reduces the internal production of phlegm. Protecting the chest and back from drafts keeps the Lungs’ defensive Qi strong against wind-cold invasion.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the child warm, especially the chest and upper back. Offer warm fluids like ginger tea or light broths, and avoid cold drinks, ice cream, and raw foods. Reduce dairy and greasy or sugary snacks, which encourage phlegm production. Gentle steam inhalation can help loosen mucus.

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Wheezing with gurgling sounds in the throat, Cough with thick yellow sticky phlegm, Flushed face and feeling of heat, Thirst with desire for cold drinks, Chest tightness and rapid breathing, Worse after a respiratory infection or in warm weather
- **Worse with**: Hot and humid weather, Spicy or greasy food, Respiratory infections, Overexertion, Dust and smoke
- **Better with**: Cool, fresh air, Drinking cool water, Light, non-greasy meals, Rest, Sitting upright
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Children's Lungs are still developing and easily overwhelmed by wind-heat from respiratory infections. This heat transforms body fluids into thick, yellow phlegm that clogs the airways, causing the sudden wheeze, chest tightness, and rapid breathing. The heat component also brings thirst, flushed cheeks, and restlessness.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating, often with red prickles at the front. Pulse: slippery and rapid (滑数 huá shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Heat is a combination of sticky phlegm and internal heat. Heat and dampness from hot weather, spicy food, or infections add fuel to the fire, making phlegm thicker and more obstructive. Cooling measures like fresh air and cool water help clear Heat, while a light diet reduces the production of new phlegm. Rest prevents Qi from becoming agitated and rising, which would worsen wheezing.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: During an acute attack, keep the child calm and in a well-ventilated, cool room. Offer small sips of cool water. Avoid all dairy, greasy, fried, and sugary foods, which create more phlegm and heat. Light, easily digested foods like congee with a little pear or radish can help. Keep the child away from smoke, strong perfumes, and known allergens. Once the acute episode passes, a balanced diet and gentle outdoor play can strengthen Lung Qi.

### Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Shortness of breath that worsens with exertion, Poor appetite and loose stools, Spontaneous sweating and frequent colds, Pale face and fatigue, Weak cough with thin watery phlegm
- **Worse with**: Overexertion and fatigue, Cold, raw, or sweet foods, Exposure to cold drafts or dampness, Catching frequent colds
- **Better with**: Rest and quiet play, Warmth, especially on chest and back, Warm, easy-to-digest meals like congee, Gentle outdoor play in clean air
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In children, the Spleen and Lungs are still maturing. When Spleen Qi is weak, it fails to transform fluids properly, allowing phlegm to accumulate. At the same time, Lung Qi deficiency weakens the body's defensive shield, making the child prone to catching colds. These frequent infections stir up the dormant phlegm, which blocks the airways and triggers asthma attacks with wheezing, cough, and shortness of breath. Over time, this cycle of deficiency and phlegm becomes chronic.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy body with teeth marks and a thin white slippery coating. Pulse: Weak and forceless, especially in the right cun (Lung) and guan (Spleen) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen and Lung Qi are already weak, so anything that further taxes them-like cold foods that chill the Spleen or overexertion that depletes Qi-will worsen symptoms. Warm, cooked foods and rest support the Spleen's ability to transform nutrients and the Lung's ability to defend the body, reducing phlegm and asthma attacks.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Offer warm, cooked foods like soups and congees. Avoid cold drinks, raw vegetables, dairy, and sugar, which weaken the Spleen and generate phlegm. Gentle daily movement, like walking or playing outside in fresh air, helps strengthen Lung Qi without overexertion. Keep the child warm and dry, especially the chest and back.

### Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cold hands and feet, especially below the knees, Early-morning diarrhea (around 5 AM) with undigested food, Sore and cold lower back and weak knees, Wheezing and shortness of breath that worsen with even mild activity, Frequent urination with clear, pale urine, Swelling of legs and ankles
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Cold, damp weather or drafts, Overexertion and prolonged standing, Excessive worry or mental strain, Dairy and greasy foods
- **Better with**: Warmth on the lower back and belly, Warm, cooked meals and soups, Rest and avoiding overexertion, Moxibustion, Gentle, non-strenuous movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In this pattern, the body's internal fire-Spleen and Kidney Yang-is too weak to transform and transport fluids. The Spleen fails to process moisture, so dampness accumulates and congeals into phlegm, while the Kidneys cannot warm the body or help the Lungs grasp Qi. This leaves phlegm lodged in the airways and breathing shallow, especially with any exertion. The asthma is chronic, cold in nature, and deeply rooted in a lack of vital warmth.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen and puffy with teeth marks on the edges; white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak and slow, especially feeble in the rear (Kidney) and right middle (Spleen) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and raw foods directly damage the Spleen Yang, while cold environments and overexertion further deplete the already weak Kidney Yang. Warmth, rest, and warm foods conserve and gently restore the body's vital fire, supporting the Spleen's ability to transform fluids and the Kidneys' ability to grasp Qi. Moxibustion adds deep, penetrating warmth to the core, helping to dispel internal cold and reduce phlegm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the child warm, especially the lower back, abdomen, and feet. Serve warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congee, and avoid raw, cold, or dairy-heavy meals that create dampness. Gentle daily movement is fine, but rest is essential-overexertion drains Kidney Qi. Moxibustion on the lower back and belly can be very helpful to restore warmth.

### Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-and-kidney-yin-deficiency-with-empty-fire)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry cough with little or no phlegm, Night sweats, Malar flush (red cheeks), Heat in palms and soles, Wheezing worse at night or with exertion, Dry mouth and hoarse voice
- **Worse with**: Dry, hot weather, Spicy or fried foods, Overexertion and late nights, Emotional stress or frustration
- **Better with**: Cool, moist air, Pear or white fungus soup, Rest and early nights, Gentle breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In childhood asthma, the Lungs and Kidneys work together to control breathing. When their Yin (the body's cooling, moistening energy) is depleted, an empty heat flares upward into the chest. This dry heat irritates the airways, causing a persistent tickling cough with very little phlegm, and makes wheezing worse at night when Yin should be strongest.
Because the Kidneys fail to anchor Qi downward, the child may feel breathless after even mild activity, and the lack of Yin fluids leads to classic signs like night sweats, a flushed face, and warm palms and soles.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine and rapid (细数 xì shuò), weak at the rear positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin is the body's cooling and moistening resource. When it is low, any heat or dryness - from weather, food, or overactivity - fans the empty fire, triggering a dry cough and night sweats. Cooling, moistening foods and a calm, well-rested lifestyle help replenish Yin and gently put out that fire.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Offer cooling, moistening foods like pear, lily bulb, white fungus soup, and a little honey in warm water. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly roasted foods that dry up fluids. Keep the bedroom cool and use a humidifier if the air is dry. Gentle, restful activities are best - protect sleep and avoid late nights, which burn Yin.

### Kidneys failing to receive Qi
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidneys-failing-to-receive-qi)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Wheezing and breathlessness that worsen with any exertion, Inhaling feels short and difficult; exhaling is easier, Pale, dull complexion and lack of energy, Weak, sore lower back and knees (or delayed development in children), Cold hands and feet, and frequent nighttime urination
- **Worse with**: Physical activity or running, Cold weather or drafts, Crying or emotional upset, Cold drinks and raw foods
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warmth on the lower back, Gentle, slow breathing, Warm soups and stews
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In long-standing childhood asthma, the Kidneys become depleted and lose their ability to "grasp" the Qi that the Lungs breathe in. Instead of the breath settling deep into the body, it gets stuck in the chest, making inhaling feel especially short and difficult. This is why the wheezing and breathlessness get much worse with even mild activity, and why the child often looks pale and washed out.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks and a thin white moist coating. Pulse: deep, weak, and particularly feeble at the rear (chi) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Kidneys are the root of all Qi and govern the ability to receive and hold the breath. Overexertion, cold, and emotional strain further drain this deep Qi, making it even harder for the Kidneys to grasp the Lung Qi. Rest, warmth, and nourishing foods conserve and gently replenish Kidney Yang, allowing the breath to settle and the wheezing to ease.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the child warm, especially the lower back and feet, and avoid overexertion or prolonged crying that drains Qi. Gentle, slow breathing exercises can help train the breath to settle. Warm, easily digested foods like bone broths, stews, and small amounts of kidney-nourishing foods (e.g., walnuts, black sesame, and eggs) are supportive. Avoid cold drinks and raw, chilled foods.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can Chinese herbs really help my child's asthma?
Yes. Chinese herbal formulas are designed to do more than just open the airways - they address the root imbalances that cause asthma. During an attack, herbs can expel cold or heat, resolve phlegm, and stop wheezing. Between attacks, tonic formulas strengthen the Lungs, Spleen, and Kidneys so that the body stops producing the "hidden phlegm" that underlies the condition. Many clinical studies show that combining TCM with standard care reduces the frequency and severity of asthma episodes and improves overall lung function.

### Is acupuncture safe for children?
Yes, acupuncture is very safe when performed by a licensed practitioner experienced in pediatrics. For children, needles are extremely thin and inserted only briefly, often just a quick tap and removal. For very young children or those who are needle-shy, practitioners frequently use non-needle alternatives like acupressure, laser stimulation, or small magnets placed on the points. The points used for asthma are mostly on the back and arms, and the sessions are short and gentle.

### Can we use TCM alongside my child's inhalers and medications?
Absolutely. TCM is meant to complement, not replace, conventional asthma care. You should never stop or reduce prescribed controller medications or rescue inhalers without consulting your pediatrician. Herbal treatment may gradually reduce the need for rescue medication over time, but always keep the inhaler on hand for emergencies. Be sure to inform both your TCM practitioner and your child's doctor about all treatments being used so they can coordinate care safely.

### How long until we see results?
Acute wheezing and coughing often improve within 1-3 days of starting the right herbal formula. Most parents notice a reduction in how often their child gets sick and how severe the attacks are within the first 4-8 weeks. For lasting change - where the child's constitution is truly stronger and asthma episodes become rare - it typically takes 3-6 months of consistent treatment, especially if there is an underlying deficiency pattern.

### Will my child need to take herbs forever?
No. The goal of TCM is to resolve the underlying imbalance so that herbs are no longer needed. Most children take formulas daily for several months, then gradually taper off as symptoms improve. Some may use a maintenance formula only during cold and flu season or at the first sign of a cold to prevent an asthma flare. Once the child's Spleen and Lungs are strong and the hidden phlegm is cleared, many can stop treatment entirely.

### What foods should my child avoid to help asthma?
In TCM, certain foods are known to generate dampness and phlegm, which directly worsen asthma. The main culprits are dairy products (milk, cheese, ice cream), cold and raw foods (salads, iced drinks, cold fruit), greasy or fried foods, and excessive sweets. Instead, focus on warm, cooked meals like rice porridge (congee), soups, and steamed vegetables. Pears, radishes, and a little ginger in cooking are especially good for the lungs. Keeping the chest and back warm and avoiding cold drinks can also make a noticeable difference.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Experienced Chinese Herbal Formulas on Pediatric Asthma** ([Link](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02341573))
   *Journal/Publication: China Medical University Hospital. Experienced Chinese Herbal Formulas on Pediatric Asthma. ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02341573.*
   This interventional study from China Medical University Hospital evaluated the efficacy of experienced Chinese herbal formulas (including She Gan Ma Huang Tang and Xiao Qing Long Tang) in children with asthma. It aimed to assess symptom control and reduction in acute exacerbations. The trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov but results have not been formally published, limiting the strength of its conclusions.

2. **Clinical Randomized Controlled Observation of Modified Wuhu Decoction in Treating Phlegm-Heat Obstructing Lung Syndrome in Children with Acute Asthma Attack**
   *Journal/Publication: [Authors not available]. Clinical Randomized Controlled Observation of Modified Wuhu Decoction in Treating Phlegm-Heat Obstructing Lung Syndrome in Children with Acute Asthma Attack. Chinese Journal of Experimental Traditional Medical Formulae. Year unknown.*
   This Chinese randomized controlled trial investigated modified Wuhu Decoction (a variant of Ma Xing Shi Gan Tang) for children with acute asthma presenting with phlegm-heat obstructing the lungs. The study reported significant improvements in symptom scores and lung function compared to conventional treatment alone, supporting the formula’s use as an adjunctive therapy.

3. **Chinese herbal medicine for asthma in children: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials**
   *Journal/Publication: Li XM, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for asthma in children: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Journal of Asthma. 2019;56(7):735-745.*
   This systematic review pooled data from multiple RCTs evaluating Chinese herbal medicine as an add-on or alternative therapy for childhood asthma. The review found that herbal interventions reduced symptom scores and exacerbation frequency, but highlighted the need for larger, more rigorous studies to confirm efficacy and safety.
