## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
A prolapsed bladder occurs when the supportive tissue between a woman's bladder and vaginal wall weakens and stretches, allowing the bladder to bulge into the vagina. It is common after vaginal childbirth, with aging, and after menopause when estrogen levels drop. Symptoms include a noticeable bulge or pressure in the vagina, a feeling of incomplete bladder emptying, urinary leakage, and discomfort during intercourse. Diagnosis is typically made through a pelvic exam.

### Treatments
Mild cases are often managed with pelvic floor muscle exercises (Kegels), vaginal pessaries to support the bladder, and topical estrogen therapy to strengthen tissues. Moderate to severe prolapse may require surgical repair, such as anterior colporrhaphy or mesh placement, to restore normal anatomy.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the bladder is held in its proper position by the upward, lifting force of what we call Spleen Qi - the body's central energy responsible for holding organs in place. When this Spleen Qi becomes weak from chronic overwork, poor diet, excessive standing, or multiple childbirths, it can no longer perform its lifting function. The result is a sinking sensation and the gradual descent of the bladder into the pelvic floor.
The Kidneys also play a crucial role. They anchor the lower body and govern the bladder's tone and closure. When Kidney Qi is depleted - as often happens with aging or excessive strain - the foundation weakens, and the bladder loses its mooring. This is why many women with prolapse also experience lower back soreness, weak knees, and frequent urination.
Other patterns can complicate the picture. Emotional stress can cause Liver Qi to stagnate, creating a sensation of pressure and distension that pushes downward. In some cases, Damp-Heat - a combination of excess moisture and heat in the lower body - adds a heavy, dragging burden that strains the already weakened supports. This is why TCM does not treat all prolapses the same way: a woman with pure fatigue and a pale tongue needs a very different approach than one with burning urination and a thick yellow tongue coating.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first asks about the quality of the sensation: a dragging-down heaviness that worsens with standing or exertion strongly suggests Qi sinking. They also inquire about energy levels, digestion, urination, and emotional state. The tongue and pulse are examined to confirm the underlying organ weakness.
If the main feeling is a constant bearing-down with profound fatigue, pale complexion, and a weak pulse, this points to Qi Collapsing (Qi Sinking). The tongue is pale with a thin white coating. This is the direct prolapse pattern, where the Spleen’s lifting force is too weak to hold the bladder in place.
When digestive symptoms like bloating, loose stools, and poor appetite combine with lower back soreness, frequent urination, and feeling cold, the root is Spleen and Kidney Qi Deficiency. The tongue is often pale and puffy with teeth marks, and the pulse is deep and weak, showing both organs are depleted.
If the focus is on lower back weakness, knee soreness, and frequent clear urination without major digestive upset, Kidney Qi Deficiency alone may be the cause. The bladder feels unsupported, and the tongue is pale with a deep, weak pulse, especially at the kidney positions.
In cases where shortness of breath, a weak voice, and susceptibility to colds accompany the prolapse, Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency is likely. The dragging sensation may worsen with coughing. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is thready and weak.
When emotional stress, irritability, or rib-side distension are prominent and the prolapse feels worse with mood swings, Liver Qi Stagnation is aggravating the sinking. The tongue may look slightly dark, and the pulse is wiry. This pattern often complicates an underlying deficiency.
If there is a sensation of heat, heaviness, and burning urination or thick yellow discharge, Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner is present. The tongue is red with a yellow greasy coating, and the pulse is slippery and rapid. This adds inflammation to the prolapse and requires clearing heat and dampness.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi Collapsing or Qi Sinking
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-collapsing-or-qi-sinking)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bearing-down heaviness or sensation of something falling out, Worse with prolonged standing or exertion, Profound fatigue and weakness, Chronic loose stools, Pale, puffy tongue with teeth marks
- **Worse with**: Prolonged standing, Heavy lifting, Overwork and fatigue, Cold or raw foods, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Resting lying down, Warmth on the lower abdomen or back, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle abdominal support, Gentle movement like walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen provides the upward lifting force that holds organs in place. When chronic fatigue, poor diet, or overwork deplete Spleen Qi, that lift weakens and the bladder sinks downward into the pelvic floor. This creates a heavy, bearing-down sensation - worse with standing and better lying down - often accompanied by profound exhaustion and loose stools.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks and a thin white coat. Pulse: empty and weak, especially at the right guan (Spleen) position, disappearing under firm pressure.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: When you stand or lift, gravity pulls downward on organs already poorly supported by weak Spleen Qi, worsening the sinking. Resting horizontal removes that gravitational pull and allows Qi to recover. Warmth and warm foods support the Spleen's transformative function, while cold and raw foods further deplete its Yang, making the lifting force even weaker. Stress knots the Liver Qi, which can disrupt the upward flow and exacerbate sinking.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congee. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Rest lying down when you feel the heaviness; avoid heavy lifting and prolonged standing. Gentle pelvic floor exercises and daily walking can help tone the muscles without exhausting Qi.

### Spleen and Kidney Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-kidney-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Persistent tiredness and low stamina, Poor appetite with loose stools, Lower back soreness and weak knees, Frequent urination, especially at night, Feeling of downward pressure or heaviness in the pelvis, Pale complexion and cold hands
- **Worse with**: Prolonged standing, Heavy lifting, Cold or raw foods, Chronic coughing or straining, Overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Resting lying down, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle pelvic floor exercises, Warmth on the lower abdomen or back
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for holding organs in place through its upward lifting force, while the Kidneys anchor the lower burner and control the bladder's tone. When both are depleted, the Spleen fails to lift and the Kidneys cannot secure the bladder, so it gradually descends into the vaginal wall. The accompanying fatigue, weak back, and frequent urination reflect the deeper Qi deficit that distinguishes this root pattern from a simple Spleen Qi sinking.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, with teeth marks and a white moist or slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak, and fine, especially weak at the right Guan (Spleen) and both Chi (Kidney) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Rest and lying down reduce the downward gravitational pull on the already weak Spleen lifting force. Warm, cooked foods support the Spleen's digestive fire, while cold and raw foods further damage Spleen Yang, worsening the sinking. Heavy lifting, prolonged standing, and straining increase intra-abdominal pressure and exhaust the Qi that is already unable to hold the bladder in place. Gentle pelvic floor exercises strengthen the local structures without depleting Qi.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, and easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congees to support the Spleen. Include kidney-nourishing foods such as walnuts, black beans, and goji berries. Avoid cold, raw, and heavy foods that drain Spleen Qi. Perform gentle pelvic floor (Kegel) exercises daily, but avoid heavy lifting, prolonged standing, and high-impact activities. Rest when tired and elevate the hips slightly when lying down to relieve pelvic pressure.

### Kidney Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Frequent urination, especially at night, A dragging or bearing-down sensation in the pelvis, Deep fatigue that feels worse after exertion, Dribbling after urination, Dizziness or ringing in the ears
- **Worse with**: Prolonged standing, Overwork and fatigue, Cold or damp weather, Heavy lifting
- **Better with**: Warmth on the lower abdomen or back, Resting lying down, Gentle movement like walking, Warm, cooked meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys are the root of the body's foundational Qi and govern the lower orifices, including the urethra. When Kidney Qi becomes weak through aging, overwork, or multiple childbirths, it can no longer firmly anchor the bladder in its proper position. The bladder then gradually loses its mooring and begins to descend, producing the sensation of a prolapsed bladder.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, slightly puffy with possible tooth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: deep (chen) and weak (ruo), especially at the rear (chi) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Qi is the body's deep reserve, easily depleted by overwork, prolonged standing, and cold. These triggers further drain the already weak Qi that is meant to hold the bladder in place, making the prolapse feel worse. Rest, warmth, and gentle movement conserve and gently stimulate Kidney Qi, helping to restore a subtle upward lift and reducing the dragging sensation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the lower back and abdomen warm; avoid cold foods and icy drinks. Gentle pelvic floor exercises and short rest breaks during the day help conserve Kidney Qi. Include warming, kidney-nourishing foods like walnuts, lamb, and black beans in your diet.

### Spleen and Lung Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-lung-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Weak chronic cough with thin watery phlegm, Shortness of breath that worsens with exertion, Poor appetite, bloating, and loose stools, Fatigue, weak voice, and reluctance to speak, Pale, puffy tongue with teeth marks and thin white coating
- **Worse with**: Heavy lifting, Chronic coughing or straining, Cold or raw foods, Cold or damp weather, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Resting lying down, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle breathing exercises, Warmth on the lower abdomen or back, Avoiding heavy lifting
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Spleen Qi is too weak to lift and hold your organs in place, while your Lung Qi is too depleted to assist in raising the body's Qi upward. A chronic weak cough from Lung deficiency also creates repeated downward pressure on the pelvic floor, further contributing to the bladder's descent.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with teeth marks, thin white slippery coating. Pulse: weak overall, especially the right cun (Lung) and guan (Spleen) positions, which feel empty or soft and become weaker with pressure.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Rest and warmth conserve Qi and support the Spleen's ability to transform and transport. Warm, cooked foods are easy to digest and don't further tax the Spleen. Gentle breathing exercises strengthen the Lung Qi, which helps to lift the sinking Qi. Conversely, overexertion, heavy lifting, and chronic coughing deplete Qi and create downward pressure, while cold raw foods and damp environments introduce dampness and cold that weaken the Spleen and Lung. Emotional stress disrupts the smooth flow of Qi and further impairs the Spleen's function.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible meals like soups and congees to support your Spleen. Include Lung-nourishing foods such as almonds, pears, and astragalus root in broths. Practice gentle deep breathing exercises daily to strengthen Lung Qi and avoid activities that trigger coughing. Do regular pelvic floor exercises and avoid heavy lifting or prolonged standing.

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Rib-side distension or pain, Irritability or mood swings, Frequent sighing, Lower abdominal pressure or dragging sensation that worsens with emotional stress, Premenstrual aggravation of prolapse feeling
- **Worse with**: Stress and frustration, Alcohol, Prolonged sitting or standing, Premenstrual phase
- **Better with**: Calm, supportive environment, Warm peppermint tea, Gentle movement like walking, Expressing emotions freely
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for keeping the body's Qi moving smoothly in all directions, including upward. When emotional stress, frustration, or long-held resentment cause the Liver Qi to stagnate, that smooth flow is disrupted. The stuck Qi creates a sense of pressure and distension that can push downward, aggravating any underlying weakness and contributing to the bladder's descent. This pattern often makes an existing prolapse feel worse, especially during times of emotional upset.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body with red or slightly purple sides, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), taut like a guitar string, especially at the left Guan (Liver) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and emotional upset cause Liver Qi to stagnate, which disrupts the normal upward flow of Qi and contributes to the sensation of pressure and prolapse. Gentle movement and emotional expression help unblock the stagnation, while alcohol and greasy foods create more heat and stagnation, worsening the condition. Premenstrually, the Liver is already under strain, so symptoms often intensify.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Practice stress management with meditation, deep breathing, or gentle movement like Tai Chi or yoga. Avoid alcohol, caffeine, and greasy foods that can worsen stagnation. Sip warm peppermint or chrysanthemum tea to help move Liver Qi. Ensure regular physical activity to keep Qi flowing smoothly.

### Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-lower-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning or stinging urination, Heavy, dragging sensation in the lower abdomen, Cloudy, dark yellow urine, Foul-smelling vaginal discharge or scrotal dampness, Yellow greasy coating on the tongue, especially at the back
- **Worse with**: Greasy, spicy, or sweet foods, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Prolonged sitting or standing, Heavy lifting
- **Better with**: Cooling, light meals (e.g., mung bean soup), Avoiding greasy, spicy, and sweet foods, Drinking plenty of water, Gentle movement like walking, Cool environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner means that excess moisture and heat have accumulated in the pelvic region. This creates a heavy, dragging sensation that adds downward pressure on the bladder, straining the supportive tissues. When there is already an underlying weakness, this extra burden can cause the bladder to descend. The heat also irritates the urinary tract, leading to burning, urgency, and cloudy urine.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating thickest at the root, slightly swollen. Pulse: slippery (Hua) and rapid (Shu), often soggy, and more pronounced at the Chi position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat is a combination of excess moisture and heat that sinks downward, causing a heavy, dragging feeling that strains the pelvic floor. Foods and environments that are hot, greasy, or humid add more dampness and heat, making the prolapse feel worse. Cooling, drying foods and light activity help clear the damp-heat, reducing pressure and irritation so the bladder feels less heavy and the urinary symptoms ease.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on light, cooling foods that help drain dampness and clear heat. Include mung beans, barley, cucumber, bitter gourd, and celery. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods, as well as alcohol and dairy, which generate more dampness. Drink plenty of water to flush the urinary tract. Gentle daily walking can improve circulation and reduce pelvic congestion, but avoid heavy lifting or prolonged standing that strains the pelvic floor.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM really lift a prolapsed bladder without surgery?
TCM can significantly reduce the sensation of heaviness and improve pelvic floor support by strengthening the Spleen and Kidney Qi. For mild to moderate prolapse, many women avoid surgery entirely. For severe cases, TCM can complement surgical repair and reduce the risk of recurrence by addressing the underlying weakness.

### What does acupuncture do for a prolapsed bladder?
Acupuncture uses points like Baihui (top of the head) to lift Qi upward, and points on the lower abdomen and legs to tonify the Spleen and Kidney. This combination helps restore the body's natural lifting mechanism and strengthens the pelvic floor. Treatments are typically weekly.

### Do I need to do Kegels alongside TCM?
Yes, Kegel exercises are an excellent complement. TCM works internally to rebuild Qi, while Kegels strengthen the muscles locally. Together they provide more complete support.

### What foods should I avoid?
Avoid raw, cold foods and icy drinks, which weaken the Spleen Qi. Also limit greasy, spicy, and sweet foods if you have Damp-Heat. Focus on warm, cooked meals like soups, stews, and congee to support your digestive energy.

### Is TCM safe if I'm using a pessary?
Yes, acupuncture and herbs do not interfere with a pessary. Continue using it as directed by your doctor. TCM can help strengthen the tissues so you may eventually need less support.

### Can I do TCM while pregnant?
Acupuncture can be safe during pregnancy when performed by a qualified practitioner who avoids contraindicated points. Herbs should only be used under strict guidance. Always inform your practitioner if you are pregnant.

### Will the prolapse come back after TCM treatment?
If you maintain the dietary and lifestyle habits that support Spleen and Kidney Qi, the results can be long-lasting. However, heavy lifting, chronic coughing, or future pregnancies can strain the pelvic floor again, so ongoing self-care is important.
