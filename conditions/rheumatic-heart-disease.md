## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Rheumatic heart disease is a complication of rheumatic fever, an inflammatory illness that can follow an untreated streptococcal throat infection. The body's immune response mistakenly attacks its own heart tissue, particularly the valves, causing them to scar, stiffen, or leak. This permanent damage forces the heart to work harder, eventually leading to symptoms like shortness of breath, fatigue, palpitations, chest pain, and swelling in the legs and abdomen. Diagnosis is usually confirmed with an echocardiogram that reveals valve abnormalities, along with a history of rheumatic fever.

### Treatments
Conventional management focuses on preventing recurrent strep infections with long-term antibiotics, controlling symptoms with diuretics to reduce fluid buildup, beta-blockers or digoxin to manage heart rate, and anticoagulants to prevent clots from forming on damaged valves. When the valve damage becomes severe, surgical repair or replacement is often necessary. Anti-inflammatory medications may be used during acute flare-ups of rheumatic activity.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, rheumatic heart disease begins as an invasion of external pathogens - Wind, Cold, Dampness, or Heat - that first settle in the joints and channels. If the body fails to expel them, these pathogens can travel inward and lodge in the heart, obstructing the flow of Qi and blood. This initial attack often corresponds to the acute rheumatic fever episode. The heart, now carrying a hidden pathogenic burden, gradually loses its Yang energy and its ability to pump smoothly.
As Heart Yang weakens, it can no longer warm and transform body fluids. The Kidneys, which share a close relationship with the Heart, also become Yang-deficient over time. This dual deficiency causes water to accumulate internally - leading to swelling in the legs, a sensation of fullness in the chest, and palpitations that worsen when lying down.
In TCM terms, this is called "Water Qi intimidating the Heart." The Spleen, responsible for transforming fluids, also becomes overwhelmed, generating phlegm that rises to obstruct the chest and cause a frothy cough.
When the heart's pumping action falters, blood flow becomes sluggish and congeals, creating a pattern of Heart Blood Stagnation. This produces a fixed, stabbing chest pain and a purplish tongue.
The original nature of the rheumatic invasion also leaves a lasting imprint: some patients retain a Cold-Damp pattern with joint pain that worsens in cold weather, while others carry Damp-Heat, with a history of red, hot, swollen joints. TCM sees these not as separate diseases but as different stages and manifestations of the same underlying process - a progressive weakening of Yang and accumulation of pathological fluids and blood stasis.
This is why the same Western diagnosis of rheumatic heart disease can present so differently from one patient to another. A person with a pale, puffy face, cold limbs, and a deep, weak pulse needs warming and fluid-draining herbs. Someone with a sharp chest pain, purple lips, and a choppy pulse needs blood-moving formulas. By differentiating the pattern, TCM treatment addresses both the acute distress and the constitutional vulnerability that allowed the heart to be damaged in the first place.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
To tell these patterns apart, a TCM practitioner begins by asking about your history - especially any past episodes of rheumatic fever, joint pain, or swelling. The nature of the initial joint problem provides the first clue. If the illness started with joints that felt worse in cold, damp weather and improved with warmth, the root is likely Wind-Cold-Damp (风寒湿, fēng hán shī).
If instead your early joint symptoms were hot, red, swollen, and intensely painful, that points to Damp-Heat in the channels (湿热痹, shī rè bì). The tongue here is red with a yellow, greasy coat, and the pulse feels rapid and slippery. This heat-driven picture leaves a very different imprint on the heart than a cold, damp one.
As the condition settles into the chest, a practitioner looks at the dominant heart and lung symptoms. A sensation of oppression, a cough with frothy or thin white sputum, and a swollen tongue with a greasy coat suggest Phlegm-Fluids above the diaphragm (痰饮凌心, tán yǐn líng xīn). The pulse is typically slippery, reflecting the accumulation of dampness and phlegm obstructing the chest.
When blood flow becomes severely stagnant, the pain changes character. Heart Blood Stagnation (心血瘀阻, xīn xuè yū zǔ) produces a fixed, stabbing or boring chest pain that does not shift. The lips and nails may turn purplish, the tongue becomes dark with stasis spots, and the pulse feels choppy or irregular - a clear sign that the vessels are blocked.
In the most advanced stage, the body’s warming fire weakens. Water Qi intimidating the Heart (水气凌心, shuǐ qì líng xīn) brings pronounced edema, breathlessness that worsens when lying down, cold limbs, and a pale, puffy tongue. The pulse is deep, weak, or slow. This pattern reveals that both Heart and Kidney Yang are too depleted to manage fluids, so water floods upward.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Water Qi intimidating the Heart
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/water-qi-intimidating-the-heart)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of water rushing from stomach to chest, Swelling of lower legs or whole body, Cold hands and feet, Palpitations worse when lying down, Reduced urination
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp, or windy weather, Overexertion or fatigue, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Lying flat
- **Better with**: Warmth and staying dry, Rest with head and shoulders elevated, Warm, easily digestible soups and stews, Elevating the legs
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Heart Yang and Kidney Yang are weakened by long-standing illness, they can no longer warm and transform body fluids. Water builds up internally and, instead of being excreted, surges upward to attack the heart and lungs. This internal flood produces the pounding palpitations, chest oppression, breathlessness, and swelling that are the hallmarks of this pattern.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, tender body with teeth marks; white slippery water-slick coating. Pulse: wiry and slippery (xian hua) or deep and fine (chen xi), with the left cun (Heart) and right chi (Kidney) positions notably weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and dampness are the direct enemies of Yang Qi; they thicken and congeal body fluids, making water harder to move. Rest and warmth conserve the little Yang that remains, allowing it to focus on transforming and expelling fluid. Lying flat makes it easier for water to rush up to the heart and lungs, so sleeping propped up uses gravity to keep the fluid down. Warm, cooked foods are easier to digest and do not extinguish the digestive fire, which is already weakened in this pattern.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the body warm, especially the lower back and feet, and avoid cold, damp environments. Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews, and steer clear of raw, cold, or icy items. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow, but rest is essential-prop yourself up with pillows so you are not sleeping completely flat.

### Phlegm-Fluids above the diaphragm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fluids-above-the-diaphragm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with copious thin white or frothy sputum, Worse when lying flat, better sitting up, Chest fullness and distension below the breastbone, Palpitations, Facial puffiness and swollen limbs, Cold limbs and aversion to cold
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp, or windy weather, Lying flat, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Dairy and greasy foods, Overexertion or fatigue
- **Better with**: Warmth and staying dry, Rest with head and shoulders elevated, Warm drinks, Rest and avoiding movement, Light, non-greasy meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In rheumatic heart disease, the heart's pumping ability weakens over time, which in TCM terms reflects a deficiency of Heart Yang. When the Spleen and Kidneys also become Yang-deficient, they can no longer transform and transport fluids properly. These unprocessed fluids accumulate and rise as phlegm-fluid to the chest, physically obstructing the heart and lungs and causing palpitations, chest oppression, and the hallmark cough with frothy sputum.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy with teeth marks, white slippery coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and dampness exacerbate internal Yang deficiency, promoting fluid accumulation. Lying flat allows phlegm-fluids to rise and obstruct the chest, while sitting upright helps them descend. Warmth and warm drinks support Yang Qi to transform fluids. Rest conserves Qi, and avoiding heavy, damp-producing foods reduces the burden on the Spleen and Stomach.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy foods that generate dampness. Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and stews. Keep the chest and body warm, and sleep with the head elevated. Gentle daily movement such as walking helps Qi flow and fluid metabolism.

### Heart Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Stabbing or pricking chest pain in a fixed location, Purple or dark discolouration of lips and nails, Dark purple tongue with stasis spots, Choppy or knotted pulse, Pain radiates to the shoulder, upper back, or inner left arm, Cold hands
- **Worse with**: Cold environments, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Emotional stress and frustration, Greasy, heavy, or cold foods
- **Better with**: Warmth on the chest, Gentle, regular movement, Warming spices like turmeric and ginger
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After the initial rheumatic invasion has damaged the Heart's Qi and Yang, the weakened pump fails to move blood smoothly through the vessels. Over time, blood congeals and stagnates inside the heart and chest, creating a fixed obstruction. This is why the pain is stabbing, precisely located, and accompanied by purple lips and nails - the visible marks of stuck blood.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 Se) or knotted (结 Jie).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis behaves like a frozen stream - cold and stillness make it congeal further, while warmth and gentle movement encourage it to flow. Emotional stress knots the Qi, and since Qi moves blood, any Qi stagnation worsens the blood stasis in the chest. Warming, blood-moving foods and spices help dissolve the stuck blood, relieving the fixed pain and restoring a smoother pulse.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep warm, especially the chest and hands, and avoid cold raw foods that can congeal the blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps keep blood flowing; prolonged sitting makes stasis worse. Include warming, blood-moving spices such as turmeric, ginger, garlic, and a little black pepper in your cooking. Avoid greasy, heavy meals that create phlegm and further gum up the circulation.

### Wind-Cold-Damp
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-damp)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Joint pain that worsens in cold, damp, or windy weather, Heaviness and aching of the limbs, Pain that improves with warmth or gentle movement, Morning stiffness and difficulty bending joints, Aversion to cold and wind
- **Worse with**: Cold, damp, or windy weather, Prolonged sitting or inactivity, Cold or raw foods and drinks, Exposure to drafts or air conditioning
- **Better with**: Applying heat or warm compresses, Gentle, regular movement, Dry, warm weather, Warm, cooked foods with ginger
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Rheumatic heart disease often begins when Wind, Cold, and Dampness invade the body's surface and joints. If not cleared, these pathogenic factors travel deeper along the channels and lodge in the heart, obstructing the flow of Qi and blood. This obstruction causes the valves and heart muscle to become stiff and inflamed, setting the stage for long-term damage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale or slightly swollen body with a white, greasy coating and moist surface. Pulse: moderate (Huan) or slow, often with a tight (Jin) quality when Cold is prominent; may be floating (Fu) if the invasion is still superficial.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind, Cold, and Dampness are the very pathogens that cause this pattern. Cold contracts the channels and Dampness creates heaviness and stagnation, so warmth and gentle movement relax the channels, promote Qi flow, and help the body expel these invaders. Rest and cold allow the pathogens to settle deeper, worsening pain and stiffness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep the body warm and dry, especially the joints. Gentle daily movement like walking or Tai Chi keeps Qi flowing without straining the heart. Avoid cold, raw foods and iced drinks; instead, eat warm, cooked meals with mild spices like ginger and cinnamon to support circulation.

### Painful Obstruction due to Damp Heat in Channels
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/painful-obstruction-due-to-damp-heat-in-channels)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Joints are red, swollen, hot, and painful, Pain is relieved by cold compresses, worsened by warmth, Fever or sensation of body heat, with thirst, Heavy, aching sensation in the limbs, Yellow, greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Hot, humid weather, Spicy, greasy, or fried foods, Alcohol, Prolonged standing or walking, Applying warmth to joints
- **Better with**: Cold compresses on joints, Cool, dry environment, Rest and avoiding movement, Light, non-greasy meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Dampness and Heat combine in the body, they can settle into the joints and channels, causing the classic signs of acute rheumatic fever: joints that are red, swollen, hot, and intensely painful. This is a Full-Heat condition where the pathogenic factors are actively flaring. If the Damp-Heat is not cleared, it can travel internally along the channels and lodge in the Heart, damaging the heart valves and eventually leading to the chronic breathlessness, palpitations, and edema of rheumatic heart disease.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly slightly swollen, with a yellow greasy coating. Pulse: slippery and rapid, possibly soggy or wiry at the Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat is a combination of two pathogenic factors: dampness, which is heavy and sticky, and heat, which inflames and accelerates. Heat and humidity directly feed the hot, damp nature of the condition, so cool, dry surroundings and cold compresses bring relief. Greasy, spicy foods and alcohol generate more internal dampness and heat, fueling the fire. Rest prevents Qi stagnation and further friction in the joints, allowing the body to focus on clearing the pathogens.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, greasy, or deep-fried foods and alcohol, which add heat and dampness. Eat cooling, damp-draining foods like mung beans, barley, cucumber, and celery. Rest the affected joints during acute flares, and keep the environment cool and dry. Gentle movement can resume once the heat and swelling subside.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture and herbs really help with heart valve damage?
While TCM cannot reverse scarred or deformed valves, it can significantly improve the symptoms and quality of life that result from them. Herbs and acupuncture work by strengthening the heart's pumping ability, reducing fluid buildup, calming palpitations, and easing breathlessness. Many patients find they can reduce their reliance on diuretics and feel more energetic, even though the structural valve problem remains. TCM treats the person with the damaged valve, not just the valve itself.

### How long does it take to see results?
Most people notice a reduction in swelling, lighter breathing, and less fatigue within the first 4 to 6 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs. The timeline depends on the pattern: those with a strong Yang deficiency and long-standing fluid accumulation often need 3 to 6 months to feel a substantial shift. Improvements are usually gradual - you might first sleep better, then notice your ankles are less swollen, and later find you can walk farther without getting winded. Consistency is key.

### Do I need to stop my conventional heart medications?
Never stop or adjust your heart medications on your own. TCM is designed to work alongside conventional treatment, not replace it. Your TCM practitioner will select herbs that complement your medications, but you must keep your cardiologist informed. In some cases, as your condition improves, your doctor may choose to lower a dosage - but this must be a supervised medical decision. Always bring a full list of your medications to your TCM consultation.

### Is it safe to combine herbs with blood thinners like warfarin?
Many TCM formulas for rheumatic heart disease include herbs that invigorate the blood, such as Dan Shen (Salvia) and Chuan Xiong (Ligusticum). These can have a mild anticoagulant effect, which may interact with warfarin, aspirin, or clopidogrel. It's essential to tell both your TCM practitioner and your cardiologist about all medications and supplements you're taking. Your practitioner can adjust the formula or monitor your INR more closely. With proper communication, the combination can often be managed safely.

### What will a TCM treatment session involve?
A session typically begins with a detailed intake - questions about your joint history, energy, temperature preferences, and the nature of any swelling or pain. The practitioner will examine your tongue and feel your pulse. Acupuncture points are chosen based on your pattern, often on the arms, legs, and back, and may be combined with moxibustion (warming therapy) to strengthen Yang. You'll also receive a custom herbal formula, usually taken as a tea or powder, to address the deeper imbalances between sessions.

### Will I need to change my diet?
Yes, diet plays an important supporting role. Generally, you'll be advised to avoid cold, raw foods and icy drinks, which can further weaken Spleen Yang and promote dampness. Instead, favor warm, cooked meals like soups and stews with ginger, cinnamon, and turmeric. Reducing salt is crucial to manage fluid retention. If your pattern involves Damp-Heat, you'll also need to limit spicy, greasy, and fried foods. Your practitioner will give you specific guidance based on your pattern.

### Can TCM help me avoid valve surgery?
If the valve damage is severe and causing heart failure, surgery may be unavoidable and life-saving. However, for mild to moderate valve disease, TCM can help stabilize the condition, reduce symptoms, and improve heart function, potentially delaying or even preventing the need for surgery. Many patients use TCM as a long-term management strategy to keep their heart strong enough that surgical intervention isn't immediately necessary. It's always a discussion to have with both your cardiologist and TCM practitioner.
