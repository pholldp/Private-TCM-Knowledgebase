## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO) occurs when bacteria that normally live in the large intestine multiply in the small intestine, where they don't belong. This leads to fermentation of food too early, producing gas and causing bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and sometimes malabsorption. Diagnosis is typically made via breath tests measuring hydrogen and methane, and it's often associated with conditions like IBS, motility disorders, or prior abdominal surgery.

### Treatments
The conventional approach usually involves antibiotics (such as rifaximin) to reduce bacterial numbers, often combined with dietary changes like a low-FODMAP or elemental diet to starve the bacteria. Prokinetic agents may be used to improve gut motility and prevent recurrence. However, relapse is common once the antibiotics are stopped, and repeated courses can disrupt the broader gut microbiome.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM doesn't see SIBO as an infection but as a failure of the digestive system's transformative functions. The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into Qi and blood, and when it's weak, dampness accumulates - creating a stagnant, boggy environment where bacteria can overgrow. This is the root of the most common pattern: Spleen Deficiency with Dampness.
Another key pattern involves Damp-Heat, where dampness combines with heat (often from diet or emotional stress) to create a hot, turbid state in the intestines. This leads to the foul-smelling, sticky stools and burning sensation that many patients describe. The Liver can also play a role - when stress causes Liver Qi to stagnate, it can invade the Spleen, disrupting digestion and leading to alternating diarrhea and constipation, with bloating that flares with emotions.
Other patterns include Stomach Qi Stagnation, where Qi gets stuck and fails to descend, causing upper abdominal distension and belching, and Turbid Dampness obstructing the middle burner, which creates a heavy, nauseating fullness. In every case, TCM treatment aims not just to kill bacteria but to correct the underlying imbalance - whether that means strengthening the Spleen, clearing heat, moving Qi, or drying dampness - so the gut environment no longer supports overgrowth.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner starts by listening to the story of your bloating, bowel habits, and overall energy. They want to know whether the discomfort feels heavy and dull, sharp and crampy, or comes in waves that shift with your mood. The timing of symptoms after meals and what makes them better or worse are the first big clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the main picture is chronic bloating, loose or unformed stools, and a tired, heavy body, the practitioner suspects Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. The tongue is often pale and puffy with a white, greasy coating, and the pulse feels weak and slippery. This pattern reflects a digestive engine that lacks the power to process food and fluids, so dampness accumulates and feeds the bacterial overgrowth.
When there is abdominal pain, a burning sensation, and foul-smelling sticky stools that feel incomplete, the focus shifts to Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner. A red tongue with a thick yellow, greasy coating and a rapid, slippery pulse confirm that dampness has brewed into heat. This pattern often develops from a lingering, untreated damp condition and brings a sense of internal heat and irritability.
If bloating and bowel changes flare up with stress, frustration, or emotional upset, the practitioner looks at Rebellious Liver Qi invading the Spleen. Distension moves around, pain can be crampy, and symptoms alternate between constipation and loose stools. The tongue may be slightly red with a thin white coating, and the pulse feels wiry, especially on the left side-a sign that the Liver is overacting and disrupting the Spleen’s rhythm.
In cases where the dominant complaint is a persistent, uncomfortable fullness in the upper belly with frequent belching and a sensation of trapped air, Stomach Qi Stagnation is the leading candidate. Here the tongue may look normal or have a thin yellow coat, and the pulse is often wiry. This pattern points to a mechanical slowdown in the middle burner rather than a clear dampness or heat overload, and it often improves with gentle movement or pressure.
When bloating is especially heavy and accompanied by nausea, a foggy head, and a tongue with a very thick, greasy coating, the diagnosis leans toward Turbid Dampness obstructing the Middle Burner. The pulse is typically slippery and full. This pattern describes a severe, sticky dampness that congeals in the digestive tract and blocks the normal upward and downward flow of Qi, creating a profound sense of stagnation.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and fullness worse after eating, Loose, unformed stools or diarrhoea, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Poor appetite and fatigue, Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and a white greasy coating, Drowsiness after meals
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Dairy products and sugar, Overeating, Damp or humid weather, Excessive worry or mental strain
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods, Ginger or cardamom tea, Gentle walking after meals, Abdominal warmth (hot water bottle), Adequate rest and sleep
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into usable energy. When Spleen Qi is weak, it fails to do this properly, allowing dampness to accumulate in the intestines. This stagnant, heavy dampness creates an environment where bacteria can overgrow, leading to persistent bloating, loose stools, and fatigue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), soft and weak, especially at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen's job is to transform food and fluids. When it's weak, dampness builds up, causing bloating and loose stools. Cold, raw, and heavy foods further damage Spleen Yang and increase dampness, so avoiding them is key. Warm, cooked foods and spices like ginger gently strengthen the Spleen and help it dry out excess moisture. Overeating and worry both strain the Spleen Qi, while gentle movement and rest restore it. By reducing dampness and supporting Spleen function, these habits directly address the root of SIBO in this pattern.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups, congee, and stewed vegetables. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, as well as dairy and sugar, which exacerbate dampness. Incorporate warming spices like ginger and cardamom. Eat smaller meals and chew thoroughly. Gentle walking after meals can aid digestion.

### Damp-Heat in the Lower Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-lower-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning abdominal pain, Foul-smelling sticky stools, Yellow greasy tongue coating, Anal burning or itching, Feeling of heaviness in the lower body, Thirst with no desire to drink much
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or heavy meals, Alcohol, Damp or humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Avoiding greasy and spicy foods, Eating light, cooling foods, Gentle exercise, Staying well-hydrated
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When dampness and heat combine and settle in the lower burner, they create a hot, turbid environment in the intestines that disrupts the normal breakdown and movement of food. This stagnant, overheated state is exactly what allows bacteria to overgrow, leading to the foul-smelling, sticky stools and burning abdominal discomfort characteristic of this pattern.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow greasy coating that is thickest at the root. Pulse: slippery (Hua) and rapid (Shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-heat thrives on rich, heating foods and stagnation, so greasy, spicy meals and alcohol literally add fuel to the fire. A sedentary lifestyle allows dampness to accumulate. Cooling, light foods and regular movement help the body drain dampness and clear heat, starving the overgrowth and restoring a balanced intestinal environment.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sugary foods, as well as alcohol, which all generate damp-heat. Favor light, cooling foods like mung beans, coix seed (Job’s tears), cucumber, and lightly cooked leafy greens. Drink plenty of room-temperature water and incorporate gentle daily movement like walking to keep Qi and fluids circulating.

### Rebellious Liver Qi invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/rebellious-liver-qi-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Abdominal pain that triggers diarrhea, Symptoms flare with stress or anger, Distension and fullness along the ribs, Irritability and frequent sighing, Alternating constipation and loose stools
- **Worse with**: Anger and frustration, Cold or raw foods, Greasy, fried, or heavy meals, Sedentary lifestyle, Irregular eating
- **Better with**: Stress management and relaxation, Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods, Gentle walking after meals, Abdominal massage
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Emotional stress such as frustration, anger, or worry causes Liver Qi to stagnate and then surge sideways, disrupting the Spleen. When the Spleen's ability to transform and transport food and fluids is impaired, dampness and gas build up in the small intestine. This leads to the bloating, pain, and irregular bowel habits typical of SIBO, which flare up whenever emotions run high.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body, possible teeth marks, thin white coating that may be slightly greasy. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially at the left middle position, with a softer right middle position indicating Spleen weakness.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger cause Liver Qi to stagnate and invade the Spleen, directly triggering the bloating and bowel changes. Calming the mind and soothing the Liver through relaxation and gentle movement helps restore smooth Qi flow. Warm, cooked foods support Spleen Qi, while cold and greasy foods create dampness that the weakened Spleen cannot handle, worsening symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible meals at regular times and avoid cold, raw, or greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Gentle daily movement like walking or yoga helps keep Qi flowing. Practice stress management: avoid eating when upset, and incorporate relaxation techniques to prevent Liver Qi from stagnating.

### Stomach Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Upper abdominal distension and fullness after eating, Frequent belching that brings temporary relief, Sensation of food sitting heavily in the stomach, Distending pain in the epigastrium, Irritability and moodiness
- **Worse with**: Stress and frustration, Greasy, fried, or heavy meals, Irregular eating, Cold or raw foods, Overeating
- **Better with**: Gentle walking after meals, Small, frequent meals, Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods, Stress management and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Stomach is designed to receive food and send it downward. When stress, irregular eating, or emotional strain disrupt this rhythm, Qi stagnates in the middle burner instead of descending. Food and fluids then sit and ferment, creating a stagnant environment in the small intestine that allows bacteria to overgrow. The result is marked upper abdominal distension, belching, and a sensation of fullness without the heavy dampness or heat seen in other patterns.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body colour with a thin white coating; in prolonged cases, slight redness may appear on the sides. Pulse: wiry, especially at the right Guan (middle) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and emotional tension cause Liver Qi to stagnate and invade the Stomach, further blocking its descending function. Heavy, greasy, or cold foods overwhelm the Stomach's ability to move Qi downward, leading to stagnation. Gentle movement after meals encourages Qi to descend, while smaller meals and warm foods reduce the burden on the Stomach, allowing it to process food smoothly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and chew thoroughly. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that congeal Qi. Gentle walking after meals helps Qi move downward. Practice stress-reducing activities like deep breathing or meditation to prevent Liver Qi from invading the Stomach.

### Turbid Dampness obstructing the Middle Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/turbid-dampness-obstructing-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, stuffy bloating in the upper belly, Nausea or queasiness, Heavy sensation in the body and limbs, Thick, greasy white coating on the tongue, No thirst, bland or sticky taste in the mouth
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Greasy, fried, or heavy meals, Damp or humid weather, Overeating, Sedentary lifestyle, Sweet, sugary foods
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked, easily digestible foods, Ginger or cardamom tea, Gentle walking after meals, Dry, warm environment, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's ability to transform fluids is overwhelmed, dampness accumulates and thickens into turbid dampness. This sticky, heavy substance clogs the middle burner-the digestive center-blocking the normal movement of Qi and causing food and fluids to stagnate. The stagnation creates a perfect environment for bacterial overgrowth, leading to severe bloating, nausea, and a heavy sensation in the body.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, swollen body with teeth marks; thick, white, greasy coating concentrated in the centre. Pulse: Soggy (Ru) and moderate (Huan), may be slippery (Hua) at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Turbid dampness is a heavy, sticky pathogen that accumulates from poor digestion of fluids and foods. Cold and raw foods further weaken the Spleen's digestive fire, while greasy and sweet foods directly add to the dampness burden. Warm, cooked foods and aromatic spices like ginger help the Spleen transform and transport, drying out dampness. Movement stimulates Qi flow, preventing stagnation that allows turbidity to settle. Dry warmth counteracts the cold and wet nature of the pathogen.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals and avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that create dampness. Sip warm ginger tea or congee to support digestion. Light, consistent movement like walking helps Qi move and prevents stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM cure SIBO, or will it just keep coming back?
TCM aims to address the root imbalance that allowed the overgrowth, so while no treatment can guarantee a permanent cure, many patients experience long-term relief when the underlying pattern is corrected. Recurrence is less likely if you maintain dietary and lifestyle habits that support your digestive type.

### How does acupuncture help with SIBO?
Acupuncture helps regulate gut motility, reduce inflammation, and calm the nervous system - all of which can improve the movement of food and bacteria through the small intestine. Specific points like ST36 and REN12 are used to strengthen the Spleen and move Qi, while points like SP9 help drain dampness.

### Do I need to change my diet while taking Chinese herbs?
Yes, dietary modifications are usually recommended alongside herbal treatment. In general, warm, cooked, easily digestible foods are favored, while cold, raw, greasy, and overly sweet foods are limited because they can worsen dampness. Your practitioner will give you specific guidance based on your pattern.

### Can I take Chinese herbs with antibiotics for SIBO?
It's possible, but you should always inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing doctor. Some herbs may interact with antibiotics, and the timing of doses may need to be adjusted. Never stop prescribed antibiotics without medical advice.

### How long before I see results?
Many people feel a reduction in bloating and discomfort within the first 2-3 weeks of consistent treatment. Full resolution of the overgrowth and rebuilding of digestive function typically takes 2-6 months, depending on the pattern's depth and your adherence to diet and lifestyle changes.

### What does a TCM diagnosis for SIBO involve?
Your practitioner will ask detailed questions about your bowel habits, bloating patterns, energy, emotions, and diet. They'll examine your tongue for coating and color, and feel your pulse at the wrists. This information helps them identify which pattern is dominant and tailor a treatment plan specifically for you.

### Is SIBO caused by stress?
Stress alone may not cause SIBO, but it can be a major trigger, especially in the Liver Qi invading Spleen pattern. Emotional strain disrupts the smooth flow of Qi, which directly impacts digestion and can create the stagnant environment where bacteria thrive. Managing stress is often a key part of treatment.

### Are there any side effects from Chinese herbs for SIBO?
When prescribed by a qualified practitioner, Chinese herbal formulas are generally safe and well-tolerated. Some people may experience mild digestive changes as the herbs begin to work, such as looser stools or increased gas, which typically resolve quickly. Always inform your practitioner of any new or worsening symptoms.
