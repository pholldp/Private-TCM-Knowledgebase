## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Western medicine classifies dizziness into several types: vertigo (a spinning sensation), presyncope (feeling faint), disequilibrium (loss of balance), and non-specific lightheadedness. Common causes include inner ear disorders like benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) or Meniere's disease, cardiovascular problems such as orthostatic hypotension, neurological conditions, and medication side effects. Diagnosis typically involves a physical exam, hearing tests, balance assessments, and sometimes imaging to rule out serious underlying conditions.

### Treatments
Treatment depends on the cause. For BPPV, canalith repositioning maneuvers like the Epley maneuver are used. Medications such as meclizine or dimenhydrinate may relieve acute vertigo, while diuretics are prescribed for Meniere's disease. For persistent dizziness, vestibular rehabilitation therapy helps retrain the balance system. When no specific cause is found, management focuses on symptom relief and avoiding triggers.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the head is the meeting point of all Yang channels and houses the brain - the "Sea of Marrow." For clear thinking and balance, the head needs a steady supply of clear Qi and nourishing Blood. Dizziness occurs when something disrupts this upward flow. A sudden, spinning vertigo often signals a disturbance from rising Liver Yang, while a heavy, foggy sensation points to Phlegm clouding the orifices. A hollow, empty feeling suggests that the foundation-Kidney Essence or Qi and Blood-is depleted.
The Liver plays a central role because it governs the smooth flow of Qi. Emotional stress, frustration, or anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate or flare upward as Yang, triggering dizziness with a distending headache and irritability.
At the same time, the Spleen's job of transforming food into Qi and Blood is critical. A weak Spleen, often from poor diet or overthinking, fails to produce enough nourishment or allows Dampness to accumulate into Phlegm, which rises and creates a heavy-headed fog.
The Kidneys store the body's vital Essence, the raw material for marrow. Long-term overwork, aging, or constitutional weakness can drain this reserve, leaving the brain undernourished and causing chronic, dull dizziness with lower back soreness. Finally, when Blood flow becomes sluggish due to injury or long-standing stagnation, the vessels that feed the head become blocked, leading to a fixed, stabbing dizziness. Each of these mechanisms explains why the same symptom can have such different root causes in TCM.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what the dizziness actually feels like - is it a sudden spinning, a dull heavy fog, or a hollow empty sensation? The quality of the dizziness is the first big clue. They will also ask about triggers: does it come on with stress, after a heavy meal, or when you stand up quickly? These answers immediately narrow down which pattern is at play.
If the dizziness strikes suddenly, with a pounding headache, ringing in the ears, and a flushed face, that suggests Liver Yang Rising. The person often feels irritable, and the tongue is red with a yellow coating.
In contrast, dizziness that worsens with movement or fatigue, and comes with a pale face, weak limbs, and heart palpitations, points to Qi and Blood Deficiency. Here the tongue is pale and the pulse is thin and weak.
When the head feels heavy and foggy, as if wrapped in a wet towel, and there is nausea, chest tightness, and a greasy tongue coating, the pattern is Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner.
A chronic dizziness that feels like an empty hollow in the head, along with tinnitus, forgetfulness, and a sore lower back, suggests Kidney Essence Deficiency. The tongue may be red with little coating or pale, and the pulse is often weak at the deep position.
Less commonly, dizziness with a fixed, stabbing headache and a darkened complexion indicates Blood Stagnation. The tongue looks purplish with dark spots, and the pulse feels choppy.
Dizziness that flares with emotional upset, accompanied by a distending sensation in the head and a wiry pulse, is often Liver Qi Stagnation.
A practitioner checks for these when the main picture is unclear, using tongue and pulse to confirm.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden dizziness or vertigo, Throbbing temple headache, Irritability and quick temper, Flushed face and red eyes, Ringing in the ears (tinnitus)
- **Worse with**: Stress, anger, or frustration, Spicy, fried, or greasy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, stuffy environments, Overwork and fatigue
- **Better with**: Rest in a quiet, dark room, Cool compresses on the forehead, Cooling foods like cucumber or pear, Gentle neck and shoulder stretches
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Liver Yin is too weak to anchor Liver Yang, the Yang energy flares upward, disturbing the head's clear orifices. This upward surge produces the sensation of dizziness, a distending headache, and ringing in the ears. The feeling of heat rising to the face and irritability are signs that the rising Yang is carrying heat with it.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, redder at the sides, with a thin dry yellow or scanty coating. Pulse: Wiry (弦 xián), often forceful at the left middle position; may also be fine and rapid if Yin deficiency is prominent.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Stress and anger directly stir up Liver Qi, causing it to surge upward as Yang, which triggers dizziness and headache. Hot, stimulating foods and alcohol add heat to the Liver, making the upward rush more intense. Rest and cooling measures help anchor the Yang back down, while gentle movement encourages smooth Qi flow without provoking excess.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize cooling, yin-nourishing foods such as cucumber, celery, spinach, and pears. Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which can fan the flames of Liver Yang. Gentle exercise like tai chi or walking helps smooth Liver Qi without overstimulating.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness worsens with movement or fatigue, Pale or sallow complexion, Heart palpitations, Persistent fatigue and weakness, Poor appetite, Pale lips and nail beds
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Standing up quickly, Skipping meals or irregular eating, Excessive worry and overthinking, Cold environment or drafts
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle exercise or walking, Stress reduction and calm mind
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi and Blood are the body's fundamental nourishment. When they are deficient, the brain (the 'Sea of Marrow') is not adequately supplied, leading to dizziness that feels like lightheadedness or a floating sensation. Because Qi provides the energy to move Blood upward, any exertion further depletes this upward drive, making the dizziness worse when you stand up, move around, or get tired.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially in the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are the body's fuel. Rest and nourishing food help conserve and replenish them, while overwork, skipping meals, and mental strain drain them further. When you stand up quickly, your body needs a rapid surge of Qi and Blood to the head; if they are deficient, dizziness occurs. Cold and raw foods weaken the Spleen, the source of Qi and Blood production, so warm, cooked meals support recovery. Gentle movement circulates Qi without depleting it, while excessive worry consumes Spleen Qi directly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups, stews, and congees to support Spleen Qi. Include blood-nourishing foods such as dark leafy greens, red dates, and small amounts of liver. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that weaken digestion. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi builds Qi without exhausting it. Prioritize rest and sleep, and minimize overthinking and worry, which consume Spleen Qi.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, foggy head sensation (as if wrapped in cloth), Dizziness with nausea or vomiting, Chest tightness and epigastric fullness, Greasy, thick white tongue coating, Poor appetite and loose sticky stools
- **Worse with**: Heavy, greasy foods, Dairy and cold raw foods, Damp, humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting, Overeating
- **Better with**: Light, warm meals, Gentle exercise or walking, Dry, warm weather
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and cannot transform fluids, dampness accumulates and thickens into phlegm. This turbid phlegm rises to the head, clouding the clear orifices and causing a heavy, foggy dizziness that feels like a wet towel wrapped around the head. The phlegm also obstructs the chest and Stomach, leading to nausea, tightness, and a poor appetite.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, swollen body with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: Slippery (Hua) or soggy (Ru), often slow or moderate, especially in the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness arises when the Spleen is overwhelmed by heavy, damp-producing foods and a sedentary lifestyle. Warm, light meals and gentle exercise help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, while damp weather and rich foods add to the burden, worsening the foggy dizziness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy products that create dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi move and dries dampness. Avoid overeating and late-night meals.

### Kidney Essence Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-essence-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness with a hollow, empty feeling in the head, Tinnitus (ringing in the ears), Poor memory and difficulty concentrating, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Frequent urination at night
- **Worse with**: Overwork and fatigue, Excessive sexual activity, Chronic stress, Cold environment or drafts, Ageing
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warm, nourishing foods, Gentle exercise or walking, Warmth
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Kidneys store Essence, the vital substance that produces marrow and fills the brain. When Essence runs low, the 'sea of marrow' becomes empty and can no longer nourish the head, resulting in a chronic, hollow dizziness. At the same time, the lower back and knees-considered the 'palace' of the Kidneys-become weak and sore, while the ears and memory fail to receive the nourishment they need.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with little or no coating, possible fine cracks. Pulse: fine (thin), deep, and weak at both rear (Chi) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney Essence is the body's deepest reserve, depleted by overwork, sexual excess, and ageing. Rest, warmth, and nourishing foods allow the Essence to be conserved and slowly rebuilt. When the 'sea of marrow' is replenished, the head feels clear and steady again.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, nourishing foods like bone broths, black sesame, walnuts, and goji berries. Avoid overwork and excessive sexual activity, which deplete Essence. Gentle exercise like Tai Chi or walking supports Kidney function without straining. Prioritize deep, restful sleep to allow the body to restore.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing headache, Dizziness worse at night, Dark purplish tongue with stasis spots, Dark facial complexion or dark lips, Pain that worsens with pressure
- **Worse with**: Stress, anger, or frustration, Cold environment or drafts, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting, Greasy or cold foods, Nighttime
- **Better with**: Warm compress on neck, Gentle exercise or walking, Massaging temples, Rest and lying down
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or stuck, it obstructs the tiny vessels that carry nourishment and clear Qi to the head. This blockage starves the brain of what it needs to stay alert, producing a sensation of dizziness that often comes with a fixed, stabbing headache. The dizziness may feel persistent and is typically worse at night, when circulation naturally slows.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple or dusky body with stasis spots, distended dark sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè), rough and uneven like a knife scraping bamboo; may be wiry (弦 xián) if Qi stagnation is present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis is like a traffic jam in the vessels. Gentle movement and warmth help to get things moving again, while cold and sitting still make the blockage worse. Stress tightens the Qi, which can further congeal blood, so calming the mind is important. The dizziness often worsens at night because circulation naturally slows during rest, allowing the stasis to become more pronounced.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warming, blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, and small amounts of red wine. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that can congeal blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps keep blood flowing smoothly. Avoid prolonged sitting.

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness triggered or worsened by emotional stress, Distending, pressure-like sensation in the head, Frequent sighing, Irritability, mood swings, or feeling &#x27;on edge&#x27;, Rib-side distension or discomfort
- **Worse with**: Stress, anger, or frustration, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting, Heavy, greasy foods, Suppressing anger or resentment
- **Better with**: Gentle exercise or walking, Deep breathing or sighing, Warm peppermint or chrysanthemum tea, Talking through emotions
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or unexpressed feelings build up, Liver Qi becomes stuck. This stagnation prevents Qi from rising gently to the head, creating a sensation of dizziness that often feels like a distending, uncomfortable pressure rather than a spinning vertigo. Because the flow is blocked, the dizziness tends to come and go with your mood.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body, with red or slightly purple sides; thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, especially felt at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Liver Qi Stagnation is like a traffic jam of energy caused by emotional holding and lack of movement. Stress and unexpressed feelings tighten the flow, making the head feel pressurized and dizzy. Gentle exercise, sighing, and soothing herbs physically unblock that stagnation, while emotional expression releases the inner pressure. Warm, light foods support the Liver's natural upward, spreading movement without adding the burden of heavy digestion.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Make gentle, daily movement a priority - a brisk walk, yoga, or tai chi helps keep Liver Qi flowing. Sip warm peppermint or chrysanthemum tea during tense moments. Avoid heavy, greasy foods that can bog down Qi. Most importantly, give yourself permission to express feelings rather than bottling them up.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### What does TCM treatment for dizziness involve?
Treatment typically combines acupuncture and a custom herbal formula. Your practitioner will select points on the head, like Baihui (DU-20) and Fengchi (GB-20), along with points on the hands and feet that address the root pattern. Herbs are chosen to either subdue rising Yang, resolve Phlegm, nourish deficiencies, or move Blood. Lifestyle and dietary advice are also part of the plan.

### Can acupuncture help with vertigo?
Yes, acupuncture is often very effective for vertigo, especially when it stems from Liver Yang Rising or Phlegm-Dampness. It works by calming the nervous system, reducing the upward surge of Yang, and clearing the sensory orifices. Many patients feel relief during or shortly after a session, though lasting improvement usually requires a series of treatments.

### How long until I feel better?
Excess patterns can respond in as little as 2-4 weeks of weekly sessions and daily herbs. Chronic deficiency patterns may need a few months of consistent care before the dizziness significantly lessens. Your practitioner will track your progress and adjust the formula as your pattern shifts.

### Can I use TCM alongside my dizziness medication?
In most cases, yes. TCM can complement medications like meclizine or diuretics. Never stop a prescribed drug abruptly, though-work with your doctor to taper if your symptoms improve. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your physician about all treatments you are receiving.

### What foods should I avoid if I have dizziness?
Generally, avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that burden digestion and create Dampness. This includes ice cream, fried foods, and excessive dairy. Reducing alcohol and caffeine can also help, especially if your dizziness is related to Liver Yang Rising.

### Is dizziness a sign of something serious in TCM?
Dizziness can reflect a range of imbalances, from temporary Qi stagnation to deeper Kidney Essence depletion. While TCM does not see it as a disease in itself, it is a clear signal that the body's harmony is disrupted. If you experience sudden, severe dizziness with other alarming symptoms, please refer to the urgent care section below.

### Does TCM work for dizziness from inner ear problems?
TCM does not diagnose inner ear disorders in the Western sense, but its patterns often correspond to such conditions. For example, Phlegm-Dampness dizziness with nausea and a heavy head closely mirrors the symptoms of Meniere's disease. Acupuncture and herbs that transform Phlegm and dry Dampness can significantly reduce the frequency and severity of these episodes.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Acupuncture for acute vertigo**
   *Journal/Publication: Li Y, et al. Acupuncture for acute vertigo. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2015.*
   A Cochrane systematic review evaluating acupuncture for acute vertigo. The review included several small RCTs and found that acupuncture may reduce vertigo intensity and improve quality of life compared to conventional care, but the evidence was limited by small sample sizes and methodological weaknesses.

2. **Acupuncture for cervical vertigo: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Wang Y, et al. Acupuncture for cervical vertigo: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Acupuncture in Medicine. 2017.*
   This meta-analysis of 10 RCTs concluded that acupuncture was significantly more effective than conventional medication in reducing dizziness and improving cervical function in patients with cervical vertigo. Adverse events were mild and transient.

3. **Tianma Gouteng Yin for hypertensive dizziness: a randomized controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang H, et al. Effect of Tianma Gouteng Yin on blood pressure and dizziness in hypertensive patients. Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine. 2019.*
   A Chinese RCT involving 120 hypertensive patients with Liver Yang Rising pattern demonstrated that Tianma Gouteng Yin combined with standard care reduced dizziness scores and systolic blood pressure more than standard care alone over 8 weeks.
