## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Western medicine defines altitude sickness as a spectrum of illnesses triggered by the body's struggle to adapt to low oxygen levels at high elevations. Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) is the most common form, with headache, nausea, fatigue, and dizziness. More severe, life-threatening forms include High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE), where fluid builds up in the lungs, and High-Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE), where the brain swells. Diagnosis is based on symptom history and recent ascent, typically above 2,500 meters.

### Treatments
Conventional management focuses on prevention and symptom relief. Gradual ascent allows the body to acclimatize. For mild symptoms, rest, hydration, and over-the-counter pain relievers like ibuprofen are recommended. Acetazolamide (Diamox) can speed acclimatization and prevent AMS. Dexamethasone is used for more severe symptoms or HACE. Descent to lower altitude is the definitive treatment for serious cases, and oxygen therapy may be administered.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, high altitude is not just a low-oxygen environment - it's a harsh external climate of thin, cold, and dry air that challenges the body's Qi and fluids. The Lungs, which govern respiration and extract Qi from the air, are the first organ system to struggle. When the air is thin, Lung Qi becomes deficient, leading to shortness of breath and fatigue. Over time, other organs are affected, creating distinct patterns.
Because Qi moves Blood, deficient Lung Qi fails to push blood through the vessels, causing stagnation. This leads to the sharp, fixed headaches and chest pain typical of the Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation pattern. The thin, dry air also depletes Yin fluids, especially in the Lungs and Stomach, resulting in dryness and a lingering empty-heat sensation - the Qi and Yin Deficiency pattern.
The Spleen, which transforms and transports fluids, is also vulnerable. The cold, thin air weakens the Spleen's Yang, allowing dampness to accumulate. This dampness can congeal into phlegm, clogging the middle burner and causing the heavy-headed, nauseous, foggy sensation of Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner. Meanwhile, if the Spleen cannot produce enough Blood, the Heart is not nourished, leading to palpitations, insomnia, and dizziness (Heart and Spleen Deficiency).
For those staying at altitude for weeks or longer, the deep reserves of Yin in the Kidneys and Liver can be depleted. This creates a pattern of rising deficiency heat with dizziness, tinnitus, night sweats, and a dry red tongue. And in some cases, dampness can stagnate and generate heat, leading to a Damp-Heat pattern with a bitter taste and greasy yellow coating. Thus, the same altitude can trigger six very different patterns depending on your constitution.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking how quickly symptoms appeared and what they feel like. Acute altitude sickness that hits within hours of ascent, with sharp headaches, chest tightness, and exhaustion, points toward Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation. The tongue may appear dusky or purplish, and the pulse feels thready and choppy. This pattern arises because the thin mountain air depletes Qi, leaving blood poorly propelled and prone to stagnation.
When fatigue is the dominant complaint and it worsens with even mild exertion, the practitioner considers Heart and Spleen Deficiency. Pale lips, a sallow complexion, a pale tongue with a thin white coating, and a weak, thready pulse support this picture. Dizziness that feels like the head is empty rather than heavy helps distinguish it from phlegm-dampness patterns.
If the person describes a heavy, wrapped-up sensation in the head and chest, with nausea and a thick greasy tongue coating, Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner is likely. The tongue body may be puffy with tooth marks, and the pulse feels slippery. This pattern reflects the Spleen's struggle to transform fluids in the low-oxygen environment, allowing dampness to accumulate.
Chronic altitude dwellers who report dizziness, tinnitus, night sweats, and a dry mouth alongside fatigue may fit Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency. The tongue appears red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. For those with a red tongue, yellow greasy coating, and a wiry rapid pulse, a secondary Damp-Heat pattern may be superimposed, indicating dampness has transformed into heat.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing pain in chest or head, Dusky or greyish facial complexion, Numbness or tingling in limbs, Shortness of breath and fatigue worse with exertion
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Cold weather, Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Rapid ascent, Stress, worry, or anger
- **Better with**: Rest, Keeping warm, Warm, light meals, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  At altitude, the thin air depletes your body's Qi - the vital force that moves your blood. When Qi is too weak to push blood through the vessels, the circulation slows and stagnates. This leads to the hallmark fixed, stabbing pain in the chest or head, along with deep fatigue and a dusky complexion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body with stasis spots, teeth marks, and a thin white coat. Pulse: deep, fine, and choppy (涩 sè).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi deficiency means the body has less energy to move blood, so overexertion and cold (which constricts) worsen stagnation, while rest and warmth help Qi flow. Heavy foods burden the Spleen, further depleting Qi, whereas light, warm foods support it. Gradual ascent gives the body time to adjust, preventing a sudden drop in Qi and blood flow.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Gradual ascent is key to allow your body to adapt. Eat warm, easily digestible foods like rice porridge with ginger and dates to support Qi. Stay well hydrated with warm water and avoid cold drinks. Gentle walking and deep breathing exercise can help move Qi and blood without overtaxing you.

### Qi and Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry mouth and throat with little desire to drink, Night sweats, Warm sensation in palms, soles, and chest, Fatigue that worsens with even mild exertion, Spontaneous daytime sweating, Shortness of breath
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Dry, windy weather, Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Stress, worry, or anger, Hot, dry environment or saunas
- **Better with**: Rest, Cool environment, Moistening, cool foods, Hydration, Gentle breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  At high altitude, the thin, dry air and low oxygen steadily drain your body's Qi and Yin fluids. Qi deficiency leaves you exhausted and short of breath, with little reserve to adapt to the altitude. Yin deficiency dries out your mouth, throat, and skin, and can create a mild empty-heat that shows up as night sweats, warm palms and soles, and a thin rapid pulse.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red or pale-red body with a thin or absent coating, often dry and possibly cracked. Pulse: fine (thin), weak, and often rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Yin are both easily depleted by exertion, dry air, and heat, so rest and hydration are essential to allow them to rebuild. Spicy and fried foods generate internal heat and consume fluids, directly worsening Yin deficiency. Moistening foods and cool, humid air help preserve Yin and gently clear the mild empty-heat, while deep breathing supports Qi without straining it.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid pushing through fatigue. Eat moistening, easy-to-digest foods like pear, honey, congee, and light soups. Stay well hydrated and use a humidifier if the air is very dry. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly heating foods that further dry Yin.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy head as if wrapped, Chest oppression and fullness, Nausea or vomiting, Poor appetite and loose sticky stools, Thick white greasy tongue coating, No thirst or desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Cold damp weather, Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Overeating, Lying down after meals, Fatigue
- **Better with**: Warm dry environment, Warm, light meals, Ginger tea, Gentle movement or walking, Rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  At high altitude, the thin, cold air strains the Spleen's ability to transform and transport fluids. When the Spleen weakens, dampness accumulates like a fog inside the body, eventually condensing into phlegm. This phlegm-dampness settles in the middle burner, clogging the chest and stomach, which produces the heavy head, nausea, and that thick greasy coating on the tongue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Pale, swollen tongue with teeth marks and a thick white greasy coating; pulse is slippery (Hua), often soggy (Ru) and moderate.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is a yin pathogen that thrives in cold and damp conditions and worsens when the Spleen is overburdened. Warmth and dryness help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, while light meals and rest reduce the workload on the middle burner, allowing phlegm to be gradually resolved.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that burden the Spleen. Eat warm, cooked meals like rice congee with a little ginger. Stay in a dry, warm environment and take gentle walks to keep Qi moving, but avoid overexertion.

### Heart and Spleen Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-and-spleen-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations or awareness of heartbeat, Dizziness that worsens with exertion, Poor appetite with bloating after meals, Fatigue and physical exhaustion, Insomnia or vivid dreaming, Sallow or pale complexion
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Stress, worry, or anger, Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Irregular eating or skipping meals
- **Better with**: Warm, light meals, Rest, Gentle breathing exercises, Gradual ascent with rest days
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  At high altitude, the thin air challenges your body's ability to produce Qi and Blood. If your Spleen is already weak, it struggles even more to extract nourishment from food and send it upward to the Heart. The Heart then lacks the Blood it needs to anchor the mind, leading to palpitations, restless sleep, and dizziness - all of which get worse with even slight effort.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, tender body with teeth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, possibly choppy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen needs warmth and regularity to turn food into Qi and Blood. Cold, raw foods and skipped meals dampen that digestive fire, while overexertion and worry drain the very Qi and Blood that are already scarce. Rest, warmth, and easily digested nourishment gently rebuild the reserves that altitude depletes, allowing the Heart to be properly anchored and the mind to settle.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, warm, easily digestible meals - think congee, soups, and stewed fruits - to avoid overloading a sluggish Spleen. Steer clear of raw, cold, and greasy foods that dampen digestive fire. Prioritize rest and ascend gradually; even a short afternoon nap can replenish Heart Blood enough to reduce evening palpitations.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dizziness and tinnitus, Night sweats, Warm sensation in palms, soles, and chest, Dry eyes with blurred vision, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Overexertion, Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Alcohol, Stress, worry, or anger, Hot, dry environment or saunas, Dehydration
- **Better with**: Rest, Moistening, cool foods, Hydration, Cool environment, Gentle movement or walking
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Long stays at high altitude expose the body to thin, dry air that gradually depletes Kidney and Liver Yin-the deep reserves of cooling and nourishment. Without enough Yin to anchor it, deficiency heat rises to the head, causing dizziness, tinnitus, and a sensation of heat in the palms, soles, and chest. The eyes and sinews lose their lubrication, leading to dry eyes, blurred vision, and soreness of the lower back and knees.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine and rapid, may be wiry; left Chi and Guan positions weak.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overexertion, dehydration, and hot environments drain the already depleted Yin fluids, while spicy foods and alcohol generate internal heat that consumes Yin. Rest and cool, moist foods help preserve Yin and clear deficiency heat, allowing the body to restore its cooling reserves.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Stay well hydrated and avoid alcohol, coffee, and spicy or fried foods that further consume Yin. Eat moistening, cooling foods like pears, black sesame, goji berries, and cucumber. Prioritize rest, keep exertion gentle, and sleep in a cool room to allow Yin to rebuild.

### Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy head as if wrapped, Chest and epigastric stuffiness, Bitter taste and sticky mouth, Nausea or vomiting, Dark scanty urine, Yellow greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy food, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overexertion
- **Better with**: Cool environment, Light, bland foods, Rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  At high altitude, the body's Qi is already strained, and the Spleen can become weak, failing to transform fluids. Dampness accumulates in the middle burner, creating a heavy, stuffy sensation. Over time, this stagnant dampness can generate Heat, leading to a combination of oppressive heaviness and signs of internal Heat like bitter taste and dark urine.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating, possibly swollen with teeth marks. Pulse: soggy-rapid (Ru Shu) or slippery-rapid (Hua Shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat thrives on rich, heavy foods and hot, humid conditions, which add more Dampness and Heat to the body. Rest and a bland diet allow the Spleen to recover and clear the accumulated turbidity. A cool, dry environment helps disperse Heat and Dampness, relieving the oppressive symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like congee and steamed vegetables. Avoid greasy, spicy, and sweet foods that create Dampness. Sip warm water throughout the day. Rest in a cool, well-ventilated space and avoid alcohol.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can Chinese herbs really help me adjust to high altitude?
Yes. Many herbs, such as Huang Qi (Astragalus) and Dan Shen (Salvia), are traditionally used to boost Qi and improve circulation, helping the body utilize oxygen more efficiently. Modern research suggests certain formulas can reduce the incidence and severity of acute mountain sickness. For best results, herbs are often started a few days before ascent.

### Do I need to take herbs before going to altitude, or only after symptoms start?
For prevention, it's ideal to start a tailored formula 3-5 days before ascent. This gives the herbs time to strengthen your Qi and prepare your body. If you are already at altitude and experiencing symptoms, herbs can still provide relief, but the earlier you begin, the better they work.

### Can I use TCM alongside Diamox (acetazolamide)?
Generally yes, but you must inform both your doctor and your TCM practitioner of all medications. Some herbs can affect fluid balance or interact with blood pressure medications. Always disclose your full list of medications to ensure safe combination.

### I get altitude sickness every time I go to the mountains. Can TCM fix that?
Many people who are repeatedly susceptible have an underlying Qi or Spleen deficiency. By strengthening your constitution between trips with acupuncture and herbs, TCM can reduce your vulnerability. It may take a few months of consistent treatment to see lasting change, but many people find they can eventually ascend with far fewer symptoms.

### Is acupuncture safe at high altitude?
Yes, acupuncture is very safe and can quickly relieve headaches, nausea, and anxiety. It is an excellent non-drug option. However, if you have severe symptoms like confusion or breathlessness at rest, seek emergency medical help immediately - these are signs of potentially life-threatening conditions.

### What foods should I eat to prevent altitude sickness?
Warm, cooked, easy-to-digest foods like soups, congee, and stews support Spleen Qi and help your body adapt. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-heavy foods, which can create dampness and phlegm. Ginger tea is especially helpful for nausea and warming the middle. Our dietary guidance section below has more details.

### How quickly does TCM work for altitude sickness?
For acute symptoms, many people feel relief within a day or two of starting the right herbal formula or receiving acupuncture. Chronic patterns, like Yin deficiency, take longer to correct. The key is matching the treatment to your specific pattern - a TCM practitioner can guide this.

### Can children or pregnant women use TCM for altitude sickness?
Acupuncture and certain herbs can be used, but always consult a qualified TCM practitioner who is experienced with these groups. Some herbs are contraindicated in pregnancy. For children, gentle acupuncture or acupressure may be preferred. Safety is paramount, so professional guidance is essential.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Medication rule of traditional Chinese medicine in the treatment of acute mountain sickness: a data mining study** ([Link](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10929902))
   *Journal/Publication: Authors. (2024). Medication rule of traditional Chinese medicine in the treatment of acute mountain sickness: a data mining study. BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies, 24, Article number. Available at PMC.*
   This study analyzed TCM prescriptions for acute mountain sickness using association rule mining and cluster analysis. The most frequently used herbs were Huang Qi, Dan Shen, and Dang Gui, reflecting the core treatment principle of supplementing Qi and activating blood. The results support Qi deficiency and blood stasis as the dominant pattern in altitude sickness.

2. **Protective effect of Danqi Jing Granules on personnel entering high altitude rapidly** ([Link](https://xuebao.301hospital.com.cn/cn/article/pdf/preview/10.12435/j.issn.2095-5227.2023.002.pdf))
   *Journal/Publication: Authors. (2023). Protective effect of Danqi Jing Granules on personnel entering high altitude rapidly. Academic Journal of Chinese PLA Medical School. DOI: 10.12435/j.issn.2095-5227.2023.002.*
   In this randomized controlled trial, participants taking Danqi Jing Granules (Huang Qi, Dan Shen, Huang Jing) before and during rapid ascent experienced significantly fewer and milder symptoms of acute mountain sickness than the placebo group. The formula's Qi-tonifying and blood-activating action was shown to improve oxygen saturation and reduce the clinical severity score.

3. **Research progress on non-pharmacological prevention strategies of acute mountain sickness with traditional Chinese and Western medicine** ([Link](http://www.cccm-em120.com/weizhongbingguokan/36/669.pdf))
   *Journal/Publication: Authors. (2022). Research progress on non-pharmacological prevention strategies of acute mountain sickness with traditional Chinese and Western medicine. Chinese Journal of Critical Care Medicine.*
   This review summarized acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, auricular acupressure, and herbal plaster therapies for preventing acute mountain sickness. Pre-acclimatization acupuncture at Zusanli (ST-36) and Neiguan (PC-6) was found to improve subjective symptoms and physiological adaptation, offering a low-risk option for travelers.
