## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, a persistent feeling of heaviness or fullness in the upper abdomen is often described as functional dyspepsia, gastroparesis, or a symptom of gastroptosis (stomach prolapse). It may be investigated with endoscopy, gastric emptying studies, or imaging to rule out structural causes like ulcers, tumors, or obstructions. When no clear physical cause is found, the discomfort is often attributed to motility disorders or visceral hypersensitivity.

### Treatments
Standard treatments include prokinetic agents to speed stomach emptying, antacids or acid-suppressing drugs for associated reflux, and dietary modifications such as smaller, more frequent meals. In cases of severe gastroptosis, surgical intervention may be considered, although this is rare. Lifestyle advice often focuses on stress reduction and avoiding trigger foods.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the sensation of heaviness in the upper abdomen is most closely tied to the Spleen and Stomach organ systems. The Spleen is responsible for transforming food into Qi and for holding the organs in place with an upward, lifting energy. When the Spleen becomes weak - from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness - its Qi can sink rather than lift.
This creates a characteristic dragging, bearing-down heaviness that worsens after eating or standing, as if the stomach is being pulled downward.
But weakness isn't the only story. The digestive fire, which TCM calls Yang, must be warm enough to cook food and fuel the Spleen's lifting function. When Kidney Yang is low, the whole digestive system runs cold. The stomach feels heavy, cold, and achy, and the discomfort eases with warmth and pressure. This pattern often includes a sore lower back and cold hands and feet, showing that the root is deeper than the stomach alone.
Sometimes the problem is fluid rather than fire. A weak Spleen can fail to manage body fluids, allowing thin, watery phlegm to pool inside the stomach. This trapped fluid creates a heavy, sloshing sensation - you might even hear splashing sounds when you move. The heaviness is often accompanied by nausea or vomiting of clear, watery liquid, and the tongue appears puffy with a slick, wet coating.
Emotional stress plays a role too. The Liver is meant to keep Qi flowing smoothly, but frustration, anger, or worry can cause that flow to jam up. The stuck Liver Qi often attacks the Stomach sideways, disrupting its normal downward movement. The result is a distending, pressure-like heaviness that swells with stress and eases when you're calm, often with frequent sighing and tension along the ribcage.
In rarer cases, long-standing Qi stagnation can lead to Blood Stagnation, causing a fixed, stabbing heaviness, or the Stomach's Yin fluids can dry up, leaving a dull, gnawing discomfort.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first asks what the heaviness feels like and when it worsens. The quality of the sensation - whether it is a dull dragging, a cold ache, a sloshing fullness, a distention that moves with mood, a fixed stabbing, or a subtle discomfort - is the first clue that points toward one pattern rather than another.
If the heaviness is a dragging, sinking sensation that gets much worse after eating or standing for long periods, and is accompanied by fatigue and a pale, tooth-marked tongue, the root is likely Spleen Qi Sinking. The pulse is typically weak and thready, reflecting the Qi’s inability to lift and hold the stomach in place.
When the heavy feeling is cold and painful but feels better with warmth and gentle pressure, and the person also has sore lower back, cold limbs, and loose stools, Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency is the picture. The tongue is pale with teeth marks and the pulse is deep, thin, or slow.
If the abdomen feels heavy and full and there is a splashing sound when moving, along with vomiting of clear watery fluid, the problem is Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach. The tongue is pale, swollen, and has a slippery white coating; the pulse is wiry and slippery. This pattern arises when weak Spleen Qi fails to transform fluids.
Heaviness that is more of a distention and fluctuates with emotional stress - worse when upset, better when calm - points to Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. Frequent sighing and belching are common. The tongue coating is thin and white, and the pulse is wiry, especially on the left side.
A fixed, stabbing pain with an underlying heavy sensation that intensifies after eating or at night suggests Stomach Blood Stagnation. The complexion may look dark or dull, and the tongue shows purple spots or a dusky body with a thin coating. The pulse feels choppy, as if it hesitates.
When the heaviness is subtle and comes with a dry mouth, no appetite, and a sensation of hunger without desire to eat, Stomach Yin Deficiency is likely. The tongue appears red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and possibly rapid. The stomach lacks the moisture it needs to function comfortably.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi Collapsing or Qi Sinking
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-collapsing-or-qi-sinking)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bearing-down heaviness worse after eating or standing, Chronic loose stools or diarrhoea, Profound fatigue and weak limbs, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Rectal heaviness, frequent urge to defecate, or prolapse, Low, weak voice with reluctance to speak
- **Worse with**: Overeating or large meals, Overwork, fatigue, or prolonged standing, Cold and raw foods and drinks, Stress, anxiety, or chronic worry
- **Better with**: Lying down or resting after meals, Warm, easily digested foods like soups, Small, frequent meals, Gentle rest and sleep, Warmth on the abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Spleen Qi is meant to hold your organs in place and lift them upward. When it becomes weak and depleted, it sinks instead of rising. The stomach loses its muscular support, creating a dragging, bearing-down heaviness in the upper abdomen that worsens after eating or standing because the weight of food and gravity pull harder on a system that can no longer hold itself up.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, teeth-marked body with thin white coat. Pulse: empty and weak overall, especially at the right guan (Spleen/Stomach) position; may disappear under firm pressure.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: When Spleen Qi is sinking, the stomach's lifting power is already exhausted. Overeating and standing strain it further, pulling everything downward. Resting lying down removes the pull of gravity and lets the Qi gather again. Warm, small meals are easy to digest and don't overwhelm the weakened Spleen, while cold and raw foods damage its Yang and make the sinking worse. Mental strain consumes Spleen Qi, deepening the fatigue and heaviness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent, warm meals and avoid raw, cold, or heavy foods. Rest lying down for 15-20 minutes after eating. Gentle daily walking helps Qi flow, but avoid prolonged standing or heavy lifting. Keep the abdomen warm with a hot water bottle if it feels cold.

### Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cold, heavy sensation in the upper abdomen, Relief with warmth and pressure, Chronic loose stools or early&#x2011;morning diarrhea, Cold hands and feet, especially below the knees, Sore and cold lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Cold and raw foods and drinks, Overwork, fatigue, or prolonged standing, Stress, anxiety, or chronic worry
- **Better with**: Warmth on the abdomen, Gentle pressure or holding the stomach, Lying down or resting after meals, Warm, easily digested foods like soups, Moxibustion on the lower back
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the warming fire of Kidney Yang is low, the entire digestive system grows cold. The Spleen relies on that warmth to transform food and lift Qi. Without it, Spleen Yang sinks, and the stomach feels heavy, cold, and unsupported - as if a weight is pressing down. The heaviness is worse after eating or standing, when the body’s Qi is already struggling to hold things up.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen and puffy with teeth marks, white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak, slow, especially feeble in the Chi positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Coldness directly depletes Yang, the very energy that lifts and warms the digestive organs. Warmth and rest conserve that Yang, helping the Spleen and Kidneys hold Qi in place. Cold foods and drinks introduce more cold, forcing the already-weak fire to work harder, while overwork drains Qi downward. Stress knots the Liver Qi, which can further block the Spleen’s ability to rise. Gentle warmth, pressure, and moxibustion all add the Yang energy the body is missing, easing the heavy, sinking feeling.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, or iced items that further chill the Spleen. Keep your abdomen warm with a belly band or warm compress. Gentle daily walking supports Qi flow without draining energy. Moxibustion at home on the lower back and navel can be especially helpful.

### Phlegm-Fluids in the Stomach and Small intestine
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fluids-in-the-stomach-and-small-intestine)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Feeling of heaviness and fullness in the upper abdomen, Splashing or gurgling water sounds in the stomach, Vomiting of thin, clear watery fluid, No thirst, or thirst without desire to drink, Dizziness and a heavy sensation in the body, Preference for warmth on the abdomen
- **Worse with**: Cold and raw foods and drinks, Overeating or large meals, Drinking large amounts of water, Damp, cold weather, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting
- **Better with**: Warmth on the abdomen, Small, frequent meals, Warm ginger tea, Gentle movement like walking or yoga
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it cannot properly transform and transport fluids. Instead of being moved out of the body, water collects inside the Stomach and Small Intestine, turning into a thin, sloshing phlegm-fluid. This trapped fluid weighs down the upper abdomen, creating a heavy, full sensation, and can be heard as a splashing sound when you move or press on the belly.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, and a white slippery coating that is excessively moist. Pulse: deep (Chen), wiry (Xian), and slippery (Hua), especially noticeable at the right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>This pattern is driven by a weak Spleen that cannot handle fluids. Cold and raw foods, overeating, and large amounts of water all overwhelm the Spleen, creating more internal Dampness and phlegm-fluid. This directly worsens the heavy, waterlogged feeling and can trigger vomiting. Damp weather adds external Dampness, further burdening the system.</p><p>Warmth, both on the abdomen and from warm foods and drinks, helps the Spleen Yang to vaporize and move the trapped fluids. Small, frequent meals reduce the digestive workload, and gentle walking stimulates Qi flow, which supports fluid transformation and prevents stagnation.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, and easily digested foods like soups and congees. Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, which further weaken the Spleen. Drink warm ginger tea between meals to gently warm the stomach. Eat small, frequent portions instead of large meals, and avoid drinking large amounts of water at once. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow and supports fluid metabolism.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Upper abdominal distending heaviness that radiates to the ribs, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent sighing, Belching or acid reflux, Irritability or moodiness, Pain or discomfort along the rib cage
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Overeating or large meals, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged sitting
- **Better with**: Stress reduction, relaxation, or deep breathing, Gentle movement like walking or yoga, Warmth on the abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger cause the Liver Qi to stagnate, it often attacks the Stomach horizontally, disrupting the Stomach’s natural downward movement. This creates a blockage of Qi in the middle burner, leading to a sensation of bloating, distention, and heavy fullness in the upper abdomen that rises and falls with your emotional state.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body, often with redder sides; thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a taut guitar string, especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly causes Liver Qi to stagnate and then invade the Stomach, which is the very mechanism of this pattern. Calming activities and deep breathing help the Liver Qi flow smoothly again, while gentle movement prevents stagnation from building up. Warmth relaxes the Stomach and encourages Qi to descend. Heavy, greasy foods clog the digestive tract and make it harder for Qi to move, while alcohol heats and agitates the Liver, making the attack on the Stomach more likely.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Make stress management a priority-even a short daily walk or a few minutes of deep breathing can keep Liver Qi moving. Eat smaller, regular meals and avoid skipping breakfast. Steer clear of greasy, spicy, or deep-fried foods that overstimulate the Liver and burden the Stomach. A warm cup of peppermint or chrysanthemum tea after meals can gently soothe the digestive system.

### Stomach Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sharp, stabbing pain that is fixed in one spot, Pain worsens after eating, Pain worse at night, Dark or purplish lips and complexion, Black, tarry stools
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Emotional stress, anger, or frustration, Cold weather, Lying down right after eating
- **Better with**: Warmth on the abdomen, Gentle movement like walking or yoga, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Qi flow in the stomach stalls for a long time, the blood in the tiny vessels around the stomach can become sluggish and form microscopic clots. This is Blood Stagnation. These stuck blood spots create a fixed, heavy sensation that feels like a weight sitting in your upper belly, and the blockage causes sharp, stabbing pain that doesn't move around.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark, dusky, or purplish body, often with purple stasis spots; sublingual veins engorged and tortuous. Pulse: choppy (涩, Sè) like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦, Xián) reflecting pain.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood Stagnation means the flow is physically blocked, so anything that encourages movement-like warmth, gentle exercise, and light meals-helps the blood circulate and eases the stuck, heavy pain. Heavy meals, cold, and stress make the Qi and blood more sluggish, worsening the stabbing sensation and fullness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, warm, and easily digestible meals. Avoid heavy, greasy, or very cold foods that can worsen stagnation. Gentle daily movement like walking helps keep blood moving, but avoid intense exercise right after eating.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, heavy, gnawing discomfort in the upper abdomen, Feeling hungry but not wanting to eat, Sensation of food sitting heavily after eating, Dry mouth and throat, thirst for small sips, Dry stools or constipation
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Late-night eating, Overwork and mental strain, Excessive talking
- **Better with**: Small sips of warm water, Moist, soft foods like porridge, Lying down or resting after meals, Cool, quiet environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Stomach lacks Yin, it loses the cooling, moistening fluids that keep digestion smooth and comfortable. This dryness creates a dull, gnawing heaviness that feels as though food just sits there, because the Stomach can't properly process it without enough liquid nourishment. The sensation is often accompanied by a subtle burning and a peculiar hunger that disappears the moment you try to eat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating, possible cracks. Pulse: Fine (细) and rapid (数), especially weak at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stomach Yin is like the cooling, moistening fluid that keeps digestion gentle and easy. Spicy, fried, and hot foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, directly deplete this fluid, making the Stomach dry and irritated-hence the burning heaviness. Rest, warm sips of water, and soft, moist foods replenish Yin and allow the Stomach to function without strain, while overwork and constant talking consume Qi and Yin, leaving the digestive system even more parched.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals of moistening foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid spicy, dry, and fried foods that consume fluids. Sip warm water throughout the day, and limit alcohol and coffee. Rest after eating and try not to rush or talk excessively during meals, as this scatters Qi and dries Yin.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Why does my stomach feel heavy after eating, even when I eat a small amount?
In TCM, this is a classic sign that your Spleen Qi is too weak to lift and process food properly. The stomach feels heavy and distended because the digestive fire isn't strong enough to transform the meal into energy, and the Qi sinks instead of rising. It's common to feel better after lying down, because gravity isn't pulling on the weakened tissues. Treatment focuses on strengthening Spleen Qi with herbs like Huang Qi (Astragalus) and acupuncture points that lift the body's energy.

### Can acupuncture really help with a heavy, bloated stomach?
Yes, acupuncture can be very effective. Points like Zusanli (ST-36) and Qihai (REN-6) are used to strengthen the Spleen and raise sinking Qi, while Zhongwan (REN-12) directly regulates the Stomach. Many patients notice less bloating and a lighter sensation after just a few sessions, especially when combined with herbal formulas tailored to their specific pattern.

### What foods should I avoid if I have a heavy feeling in my upper abdomen?
Cold, raw, and greasy foods are the biggest culprits - they dampen the digestive fire and make the Spleen work harder. Avoid iced drinks, salads, dairy, and fried foods. Instead, favor warm, cooked, easily digestible meals like soups, congee, and steamed vegetables. Eating smaller, more frequent meals and chewing thoroughly also reduces the burden on your stomach.

### Is my stomach heaviness related to stress?
Very often, yes. TCM sees the Liver as the organ most affected by emotional stress, and when Liver Qi stagnates, it tends to invade the Stomach. This creates a distending, pressure-like heaviness that gets worse when you're upset and better when you're relaxed. If you notice you sigh a lot, feel tense in your ribs, or your symptoms flare with frustration, stress is likely a major factor - and your treatment will include herbs and points that smooth the Liver Qi.

### How long does TCM treatment take for upper abdominal heaviness?
It depends on the pattern. Excess conditions like Liver Qi Stagnation or Phlegm-Fluids can improve within 2-4 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs. Deficiency patterns, where the body's Qi or Yang is deeply depleted, usually need 1-3 months of consistent treatment to rebuild strength and see lasting relief. Your practitioner will give you a better estimate after a full diagnosis.

### Can I combine TCM herbs with my current stomach medications?
In most cases, yes, but you should always inform both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor about everything you're taking. Some herbs can interact with medications - for example, blood-moving herbs like Dang Gui (Angelica sinensis) may increase the effect of blood thinners. Your practitioners can coordinate to ensure your treatment is safe and complementary.
