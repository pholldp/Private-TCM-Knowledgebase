## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Polyneuritis, also called polyneuropathy, is inflammation of multiple peripheral nerves, leading to symmetric weakness, numbness, tingling, and often burning or stabbing pain. Symptoms usually begin in the feet and hands and may spread upward.
It can be triggered by autoimmune diseases, infections, diabetes, nutritional deficiencies, or toxic exposures. Diagnosis typically involves a neurological exam, electromyography (EMG), nerve conduction studies, and blood tests to identify the underlying cause.

### Treatments
Conventional treatment targets the root cause-such as strict blood sugar control for diabetic neuropathy, immunosuppressants or IVIG for autoimmune polyneuritis, and antibiotics for infections. Nerve pain is managed with medications like gabapentin, pregabalin, or tricyclic antidepressants, and physical therapy helps maintain muscle strength and function.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, polyneuritis falls under the category of Wei syndrome (痿证), a pattern of flaccid weakness and muscle wasting. It is not seen as a single disease but as a sign that the muscles and channels are not being properly nourished. This malnourishment can arise from several different organ imbalances, which is why two people with the same Western diagnosis may receive completely different TCM treatments.
The Spleen and Stomach are central because they transform food into Qi and Blood, which then travel to the limbs. When the Spleen is weak-often from poor diet, overwork, or chronic illness-the muscles become heavy, weak, and eventually atrophy. If Damp-Heat invades the Spleen, the limbs feel swollen and heavy as if wrapped in wet cloth.
Other patterns include Lung Heat, where a high fever or respiratory illness consumes the body's fluids, leaving the muscles dry and weak; and Blood Stagnation, where sluggish circulation in the small vessels blocks fresh Qi and Blood from reaching the nerves, causing fixed pain and numbness. Each pattern has a distinct feel and requires a different approach.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the onset and the quality of the weakness. If the limbs became weak suddenly after a high fever or respiratory infection, and the person now feels dry throat, thirst, and skin dryness, the practitioner suspects Lung Heat. A red tongue with a yellow coat and a rapid, thin pulse confirm that heat has consumed body fluids, leaving the muscles malnourished.
When the weakness feels more like a heavy, dragging sensation - as if the legs are wrapped in wet cloth - and there is some puffiness or a greasy yellow tongue coating, the focus shifts to Damp-Heat invading the Spleen. The pulse will feel slippery and rapid. The practitioner asks whether symptoms worsen in humid weather or after rich, greasy meals, because dampness clings and sinks downward.
If the weakness crept up gradually, with persistent fatigue, poor appetite, and loose stools, the picture points to Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency. Here the tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse is weak or thready. The practitioner understands that the digestive system is not generating enough Qi and blood to nourish the muscles, so the limbs slowly lose strength.
When the person reports a fixed, stabbing pain or distinct numbness along with the weakness, and the tongue looks purplish or has dark spots, the pattern is Blood Stagnation. The pulse may feel choppy or wiry. The practitioner will ask about any history of injury or prolonged immobility, because static blood blocks the channels, preventing fresh nourishment from reaching the nerves and muscles.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Spleen and Stomach Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-and-stomach-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Progressive weakness and atrophy of muscles, Poor appetite, bloating after eating, Loose or soft stools, Fatigue and lack of strength, Sallow or pale complexion, Heavy sensation in limbs
- **Worse with**: Overwork and physical strain, Skipping meals or eating irregularly, Raw, cold foods and iced drinks, Excessive worry and pensiveness, Damp, cold environments
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Rest and adequate sleep, Gentle movement or exercise, Warmth on the abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen transforms food into Qi and Blood, which nourish the muscles and limbs. When Spleen Qi is deficient, this transformation weakens, leaving the limbs undernourished. Over time, the muscles become weak, heavy, and eventually atrophy, producing the progressive weakness seen in polyneuritis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, puffy body with tooth marks and a thin white coating. Pulse: Weak, soft, and empty, especially at the right middle (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen thrives on warmth and regularity. Warm, cooked foods are easy to transform into Qi and Blood, while raw and cold foods directly weaken the Spleen's digestive fire. Overwork and excessive thinking consume Spleen Qi, so rest and stress management are essential. Gentle movement stimulates Qi circulation without depleting reserves, and warmth on the abdomen supports the Spleen's function. Avoiding damp environments prevents external Dampness from further burdening the already weak Spleen.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that tax the Spleen. Eat regular meals and chew thoroughly. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps circulate Qi without depleting it. Prioritize rest and avoid overwork.

### Damp-Heat invading the Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-invading-the-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Limb heaviness and weakness, Swelling or puffiness in limbs, Abdominal fullness and poor appetite, Sticky or greasy taste in mouth, Yellow greasy tongue coating
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Dairy products, Cold drinks, Humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged inactivity
- **Better with**: Light, warm meals, Gentle movement or exercise, Dry climate, Bitter foods (e.g., bitter melon)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen governs the muscles and limbs. When Damp-Heat invades the Spleen, it obstructs the flow of Qi and fluids, causing Dampness to seep into the muscles and channels. This produces a characteristic heavy, weak sensation in the limbs, often with swelling, while the Heat adds a low-grade inflammation and a greasy yellow tongue coating.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow, greasy coating thickest at the centre and root; may be swollen with teeth marks. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), or slippery (Hua) and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Damp-Heat thrives on heavy, greasy foods and damp environments, which further burden the Spleen and generate more Dampness. Avoiding these reduces the pathogenic load. Light, warm meals and bitter foods help drain Dampness and clear Heat. Exercise moves Qi and transforms Dampness, while rest allows the Spleen to recover its transporting function.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid damp-producing foods such as dairy, greasy or fried foods, refined sugar, and cold raw foods. Eat light, warm, cooked meals with plenty of vegetables and grains like barley and job's tears. Gentle exercise like walking or tai chi helps move Qi and transform Dampness. Stay in a dry environment and avoid prolonged sitting in damp places.

### Lung Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden limb weakness following a high fever or respiratory infection, Cough with thick yellow or green phlegm, Fever and sensation of body heat, Intense thirst with a desire for cold drinks, Dry mouth, dry skin, and scanty dark urine
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Hot, dry weather or heated rooms, Smoking or smoky environments, Overexertion and heavy sweating, Anger and frustration
- **Better with**: Rest in a cool, quiet room, Cool or room-temperature drinks, Moistening foods like pear or lily bulb, Gentle, slow breaths
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heat in the Lungs acts like a dry wind that scorches the body's fluids. When these precious fluids are burned up, the Lungs lose their ability to send moisture and nourishment out to the muscles and limbs through the channels. This is why limb weakness often appears suddenly after a high fever or a severe respiratory infection - the muscles have been cut off from their source of hydration and energy.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a yellow dry coating; prickly papillae may appear on the front third. Pulse: rapid (数 shu), possibly slippery (滑 huá) or overflowing (洪 hóng), especially forceful at the right cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spicy and fried foods, hot environments, and emotional stress all add Heat to the body, which further scorches the Lung's fluids and deepens the weakness. Rest, cool drinks, and moistening foods help to clear that Heat and slowly rebuild the Yin and fluids that the muscles depend on. Gentle breathing encourages the Lungs to descend Qi and spread nourishment, rather than forcing the body to work harder.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat cooling, moistening foods like pears, white fungus soup, and cooked spinach. Sip warm or room-temperature water throughout the day and avoid icy drinks, which can shock the system. Steer clear of spicy, fried, or barbecued foods that add internal Heat. A humidifier in your room can help if the air is dry, and gentle breathing exercises can support the Lungs' function without exhausting you.

### Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed, stabbing pain, Pain worsens at night, Localized numbness or tingling, Purplish lips, nails, or dusky complexion
- **Worse with**: Sedentary lifestyle or prolonged inactivity, Cold exposure, Stress, Injury
- **Better with**: Gentle movement, Warmth, Massage, Stretching
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When blood flow becomes sluggish or obstructed, it creates stasis in the small vessels (collaterals) that supply the muscles and nerves. This blockage prevents fresh Qi and Blood from reaching the limbs, so the muscles gradually weaken and lose their tone. The result is a combination of flaccid weakness, numbness, and the kind of fixed, stabbing pain that is typical of blood stasis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dusky body, often with stasis spots; sublingual veins are distended and dark. Pulse: choppy (涩 Se) like a knife scraping bamboo, or wiry (弦 Xian) if Qi stagnation is also present.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Movement and warmth encourage blood to flow, which directly counteracts the stasis blocking the collaterals. Cold and sitting still cause blood to congeal, making the obstruction worse. Stress tightens the Liver Qi, which further slows blood movement and deepens the stasis.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, lightly spiced foods like turmeric, ginger, and cinnamon to encourage blood flow. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi is essential - it prevents further stagnation. Avoid cold, raw foods and prolonged sitting, which can congeal the blood and worsen symptoms.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture help with nerve pain from polyneuritis?
Yes, acupuncture can reduce nerve pain by calming inflammation, improving local blood flow, and encouraging the body's own pain-relieving chemicals. Many people experience a noticeable decrease in burning or tingling after a few sessions, though the degree of relief varies. It works best as part of a full treatment plan that includes herbs and dietary adjustments.

### How long does it take to see results with TCM?
Most people notice small changes-like better energy, less heaviness, or improved appetite-within 2-4 weeks. Significant improvement in muscle strength and nerve function usually takes 3-6 months of consistent treatment. Acute or excess patterns tend to respond faster than long-standing deficiency patterns.

### Can I take Chinese herbs with my nerve pain medication?
In most cases, yes, but it is essential that both your TCM practitioner and your doctor know exactly what you are taking. Some herbs that move blood (like Dang Gui or Chuan Xiong) can interact with blood thinners, and herbs with sedative effects may add to the drowsiness caused by gabapentin or pregabalin. Your practitioner will choose a formula that is safe alongside your current medications.

### Is polyneuritis curable with TCM?
TCM aims to address the underlying imbalance that allowed the nerve weakness to develop, so many people achieve long-term remission or significant functional recovery. However, the outcome depends on the cause, severity, and how early treatment begins. Even when full reversal is not possible, TCM can often improve quality of life, reduce pain, and slow progression.

### What foods should I avoid if I have polyneuritis?
Cold, raw, and greasy foods tend to weaken the Spleen and create dampness, which can make limbs feel heavier and more swollen. It is wise to limit ice cream, fried food, heavy dairy, and alcohol. Instead, favour warm, cooked meals like soups, congee, and steamed vegetables. Your practitioner may give more specific advice based on your pattern.

### Does acupuncture hurt?
Acupuncture needles are extremely thin-much finer than injection needles-so most people feel only a brief pinch or a dull ache when the needle reaches the right depth. The sensation is often described as a warm or heavy feeling around the point, which is a sign of Qi activation. The treatment is generally relaxing, and many patients fall asleep during the session.

### Can TCM help if I have muscle wasting?
Yes, TCM directly targets muscle nourishment. Herbs like Huang Qi (Astragalus) and acupuncture points like Zusanli ST-36 are specifically used to strengthen the Spleen and build muscle tissue. While regrowth is a slow process, many people see gradual improvement in muscle tone and strength when treatment is combined with gentle exercise and proper nutrition.
