## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, upper abdominal fullness is often described as a symptom of functional dyspepsia, gastritis, or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). It can also be linked to delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) or simply to dietary indiscretions. Patients typically report a sensation of bloating, pressure, or early satiety in the upper abdomen, sometimes accompanied by belching, nausea, or heartburn.
Diagnosis usually relies on a careful history and physical exam, and may be supported by endoscopy, ultrasound, or gastric emptying studies to rule out structural problems like ulcers or gallbladder disease. When no clear organic cause is found, the condition is often labeled functional, and treatment focuses on symptom management.

### Treatments
Standard conventional treatments include antacids, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) like omeprazole, H2 blockers, and prokinetic agents to speed stomach emptying. Dietary advice typically involves eating smaller meals, avoiding trigger foods (fatty, spicy, or acidic items), and reducing alcohol and caffeine. Stress management and sometimes low-dose antidepressants are used when anxiety or nerve hypersensitivity is thought to play a role.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands upper abdominal fullness as a failure of the Spleen and Stomach to properly manage the movement of Qi. The Stomach is meant to send food downward, while the Spleen transforms it into usable energy and sends clear Qi upward. When this dynamic breaks down - whether from weakness, obstruction, or emotional upset - Qi stagnates in the middle burner, creating that stuck, bloated sensation.
The type of fullness tells a practitioner a great deal: a heavy, soggy fullness suggests Dampness; a distended, shifting fullness points to Qi stagnation; a burning, tight fullness hints at Heat or Yin deficiency.
The Liver often plays a hidden role. In TCM, the Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When you're stressed or frustrated, Liver Qi can surge sideways and invade the Stomach, halting its downward movement. This is why so many people experience upper abdominal bloating during tense periods - the emotional state directly disrupts the digestive rhythm.
A classic sign is fullness that spreads to the ribs and improves with belching or a deep sigh.
Dietary factors create their own distinct patterns. Overeating or consuming greasy, cold, or raw foods can overwhelm the Stomach, leading to Food Stagnation with sour regurgitation and a thick tongue coating. Chronic poor eating habits weaken the Spleen, allowing Dampness and Phlegm to accumulate - a sticky, heavy fullness that lingers.
Even a dry, burning fullness can arise when the Stomach's Yin fluids are depleted from late nights, spicy foods, or chronic illness. Each pattern, though it produces a similar symptom, requires a completely different herbal formula and acupuncture approach.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking what the fullness actually feels like-heavy, distended, burning, or simply blocked-and when it strikes. The timing, triggers, and the sensations that make it better or worse are the first clues that point toward one pattern rather than another.
If the fullness flares with emotional stress, accompanied by belching and a sensation of distension that spreads to the sides of the ribs, the picture strongly suggests Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. The tongue may be normal or slightly red on the edges, and the pulse often feels wiry, like a guitar string.
When the fullness is chronic and mild, eases with gentle pressure, and worsens after eating even small meals, Spleen Qi Deficiency is likely. This pattern brings fatigue, loose stools, and a pale tongue with tooth marks on the sides. The pulse is typically weak and thready.
Post-meal bloating that is pronounced and accompanied by sour regurgitation, belching with the smell of undigested food, and a thick greasy tongue coating points to Food Stagnation in the Stomach. The pulse is often slippery, like beads rolling under the finger.
A heavy, blocked sensation in the epigastrium with a sticky taste in the mouth and no appetite suggests Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner. The tongue coating is thick, white, and greasy, and the pulse is slippery or soggy.
If the fullness is accompanied by nausea, a bitter taste, thirst, and a feeling of heat, Damp-Heat in the Stomach and Spleen may be present. The tongue is red with a yellow greasy coating, and the pulse is rapid and slippery.
A gnawing, burning fullness with a dry mouth, especially in the afternoon or evening, and a red tongue with little or no coating indicates Stomach Yin Deficiency. This pattern often appears in chronic conditions or after prolonged illness, and the pulse is thin and rapid.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain radiating to the ribs, Worse with stress or frustration, Frequent belching or acid reflux, Irritability and frequent sighing, Sensation of a lump in the throat
- **Worse with**: Stress and emotional upset, Overeating, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol, Eating while upset
- **Better with**: Gentle exercise or walking, Deep breathing and relaxation, Warm peppermint or ginger tea, Calm, quiet environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress or frustration builds, the Liver's Qi flow becomes stuck. Instead of moving smoothly, it surges sideways and invades the Stomach, disrupting its downward motion. This causes Qi to stagnate in the middle burner, creating a bloated, distended feeling in the upper abdomen that often spreads toward the ribs.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: slightly red body with redder sides and thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress directly causes Liver Qi to stagnate and attack the Stomach, triggering bloating and belching. Calming activities and deep breathing help relax the Liver and allow Qi to flow smoothly. Overeating and heavy foods burden the Stomach, worsening stagnation, while light meals and gentle movement support digestion and Qi movement.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, regular meals and avoid overeating. Reduce spicy, greasy, and fried foods. Practice stress management such as meditation, gentle yoga, or walking. Avoid eating when angry or upset.

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fullness relieved by warmth and gentle pressure, Bloating worsens after eating, even small meals, Loose or unformed stools, Persistent fatigue and lack of energy
- **Worse with**: Large meals, Cold, raw, or icy foods, Overwork and physical exhaustion, Prolonged standing or sitting, Stress and emotional upset
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down, Warm compress on the abdomen, Gentle abdominal massage, Warm, cooked meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food into Qi and transporting it upward. When Spleen Qi is weak, this process slows down, leaving food and fluids to stagnate in the middle burner. The result is a persistent, dull sensation of fullness that feels as though the stomach never empties properly.
Because the digestive engine is underpowered, even a small meal can overwhelm it, making the bloating worse after eating. Rest and gentle pressure help conserve what little Qi is available, which is why the discomfort eases when you lie down or press a warm hand over the area.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, tender body with tooth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: weak and soft, especially at the right guan (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Spleen Qi is like a gentle digestive fire that needs warmth and rest to function. Cold, raw foods and overexertion dampen that fire, making the Spleen too weak to move food along, so stagnation and bloating build up. Rest, warmth, and small, warm meals conserve and support the Spleen's Qi, allowing it to gradually transform and transport again.
Gentle pressure and massage work because they physically encourage movement in the middle burner without demanding extra energy from the body. Worry and mental strain knot the Liver Qi, which can further invade and weaken the Spleen, so emotional calm is also protective.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, congees, and stewed vegetables. Chew thoroughly and stop before you feel completely full. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods, which demand extra Spleen energy to process.
Rest after meals and keep your abdomen warm. Gentle daily movement, such as walking or tai chi, helps circulate Qi without draining it. Prioritize sleep and avoid overworking, as prolonged mental or physical strain further depletes Spleen Qi.

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fullness and bloating that worsens immediately after eating, Sour, rotten-smelling belching, Aversion to food and its smell, Thick, greasy tongue coating, especially in the centre, Bloating relieved by vomiting
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Cold, raw, or icy foods, Eating too quickly, Lying down after meals
- **Better with**: Vomiting, Gentle exercise or walking, Eating light, easily digestible foods, Fasting briefly or skipping a meal
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you overeat or consume heavy, greasy foods, your Stomach cannot process them properly. The undigested food sits and ferments, blocking the normal downward movement of Stomach Qi. This trapped Qi and stagnant food create the sensation of fullness, pressure, and bloating in the upper abdomen, often accompanied by sour belching as the Stomach tries to expel the putrefying contents.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑, Huá), especially forceful at the right middle (Stomach) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Overeating and heavy foods overwhelm the Stomach's ability to digest, causing Qi and food to stagnate. Cold and raw foods further weaken the digestive fire, while eating quickly doesn't allow proper breakdown. Vomiting expels the stagnant material, providing immediate relief. Light movement encourages Qi flow downward, and fasting gives the Stomach a chance to recover.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and chew thoroughly. Avoid overeating, greasy, fried, and cold raw foods. A short walk after meals helps move Qi. Consider a light, warm congee or soup when symptoms flare.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, blocked sensation in the upper abdomen, Thick white greasy tongue coating, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, No thirst or no desire to drink, Heavy, sluggish body and foggy head
- **Worse with**: Damp or humid weather, Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Overeating, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold, raw, or icy foods
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle exercise or walking, Warm, dry environment, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak and cannot transform fluids, Dampness accumulates. Over time, Dampness congeals into Phlegm, which obstructs the Middle Burner. This sticky, heavy Phlegm-Dampness blocks the normal descent of Stomach Qi, causing a persistent, heavy sensation of fullness and stuffiness in the upper abdomen.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, thick white greasy coating, excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 Huá), may be slow or soggy, especially at the right middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Dampness forms when the Spleen is too weak to transform fluids, often aggravated by dietary and environmental Dampness. Warm, cooked foods and dry surroundings lessen the burden on the Spleen, while gentle movement helps Qi circulate and dispel Dampness. In contrast, cold, raw, greasy foods and damp weather introduce more Dampness, and a sedentary lifestyle allows Phlegm to stagnate further.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and avoid cold, raw, greasy, or dairy-rich meals that generate Dampness. Incorporate barley, adzuki beans, and ginger tea to help dry Dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking supports Spleen Qi and helps clear Phlegm-Dampness.

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Bloating and fullness in the upper abdomen, Nausea or vomiting, Bitter or sticky taste in the mouth, Thirst with little desire to drink, Heavy feeling in the body and limbs, Loose stools that feel incomplete or sticky
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and sweet drinks, Damp or humid weather, Overeating, Stress and emotional upset
- **Better with**: Eating light, bland foods, Drinking barley or mung bean tea, Gentle exercise or walking, Cool, dry environment, Rest and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When rich, spicy, or greasy foods and alcohol create Dampness and Heat in the Stomach and Spleen, the middle burner becomes obstructed. The Spleen can't transform fluids, so Dampness accumulates, while Heat stirs up turbidity. This trapped Damp-Heat leads to a heavy, bloated fullness in the upper abdomen, nausea, and a bitter taste.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, thick yellow greasy coating especially at centre and root. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and rapid (Shu), often also slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, spicy, and sweet foods generate Dampness and Heat, directly adding to the pathological obstruction in the middle burner. Damp weather and overeating further overwhelm the Spleen's ability to transform and transport. Conversely, light, bland foods and bitter, cooling drinks help clear Heat and drain Dampness, reducing the turbid blockage. Gentle movement stimulates Qi flow, helping the Spleen process fluids and relieve the heavy fullness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and barley. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sweet foods, dairy, and alcohol. Drink warm barley tea or mung bean soup to clear Damp-Heat. Gentle exercise like walking helps move Qi and transform Dampness. Keep your living environment dry and well-ventilated.

### Stomach Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning, gnawing sensation in the upper abdomen, Dry mouth and throat, Feeling hungry but reluctant to eat, Red tongue with little or no coating, Dry stools or constipation
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or spicy foods, Alcohol and coffee, Large meals, Stress and emotional upset, Late nights and insufficient sleep
- **Better with**: Rest and relaxation, Cool, bland foods, Moistening foods like pear and congee, Small, frequent meals, Sipping warm water
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Stomach relies on Yin fluids to stay moist and to guide Qi downward. When Yin is depleted, the Stomach becomes dry and its Qi cannot descend properly, creating a sensation of burning, gnawing fullness. This is not a heavy bloating from food or phlegm, but a tight, uncomfortable dryness that is often accompanied by a dry mouth and a red, peeled tongue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body, dry centre, little or no coating, possibly with cracks. Pulse: fine (细 xì) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Spicy and drying foods consume Yin fluids, worsening the dryness and burning sensation. Moistening, cool foods replenish Yin and soothe the Stomach. Rest and sleep are essential for Yin recovery, while stress and overwork deplete it. Small meals prevent overburdening the weakened Stomach, allowing it to process food without exhausting its limited Yin.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, fried, and dry foods, as well as alcohol and coffee, which deplete Yin further. Eat moistening, cooling foods like pears, apples, tofu, congee, and steamed vegetables. Sip warm water throughout the day. Rest and avoid overwork; prioritize sleep to allow Yin to recover. Small, frequent meals are easier on a weak Stomach.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can stress really cause upper abdominal fullness?
Yes, and in TCM it's one of the most common causes. When you're under stress, your Liver Qi stagnates, and because the Liver channel runs through the rib area, that tension can spread to the Stomach, disrupting its normal downward movement. The result is a distended, bloated feeling that often gets worse with emotional upset and improves with belching or a good sigh. Acupuncture and herbs that soothe the Liver are usually very effective for this pattern.

### What foods should I avoid if I have this symptom?
In general, avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods, as they weaken the Spleen's digestive fire and promote Dampness. Dairy, fried foods, and excessive sweets can also create Phlegm-Dampness, making the fullness heavier. If your fullness is stress-related, cut back on coffee and alcohol, which can aggravate Liver Qi stagnation. Eating smaller, warm, cooked meals and chewing thoroughly is a universal first step that supports all patterns.

### Can acupuncture really help with bloating?
Yes, acupuncture is a primary tool for relieving upper abdominal fullness. Points like Zhongwan (REN-12) and Zusanli (ST-36) directly regulate the Stomach and Spleen, encouraging Qi to move downward. Many patients feel a gentle release or gurgling in the abdomen during treatment, and the sensation of bloating often diminishes within a few sessions, especially for excess-type patterns.

### How long does it take for Chinese herbs to work?
For acute bloating from overeating, a single dose of a food-stagnation formula can bring relief within hours. For chronic patterns, you should notice some improvement in the first 1-2 weeks, but lasting change usually takes 4-8 weeks of daily herbal tea or granules, combined with dietary adjustments and acupuncture. Deficiency patterns require longer to rebuild the Spleen's strength.

### Is it safe to take TCM herbs with my prescription antacids or PPIs?
In most cases, yes, but always inform both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor about all medications and supplements you are taking. Herbs that strongly move Qi or clear Heat could theoretically alter stomach pH or interact with absorption, so they are best timed apart from antacids (e.g., take herbs one hour before or after). Your TCM practitioner will select formulas that complement your current treatment rather than conflict with it.

### Can TCM help if my doctor says it's 'just functional dyspepsia'?
Absolutely. TCM excels at functional digestive disorders precisely because it doesn't rely on a structural lesion to diagnose and treat. The patterns of Qi stagnation, Spleen deficiency, or Dampness that underlie functional dyspepsia are exactly what TCM addresses with herbs, acupuncture, and diet. Many patients who have been told 'everything looks normal' find significant relief through a pattern-based TCM approach.
