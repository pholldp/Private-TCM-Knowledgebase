## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Chronic hepatitis is persistent inflammation of the liver lasting more than six months. It can be caused by viral infections (hepatitis B, C), autoimmune disease, long-term alcohol use, or metabolic conditions like fatty liver disease. Symptoms vary widely-from none at all to fatigue, abdominal discomfort, jaundice, and in advanced cases, cirrhosis.
Diagnosis typically involves blood tests to measure liver enzymes and viral markers, imaging such as ultrasound, and sometimes a liver biopsy. Treatment focuses on stopping the underlying cause and preventing progression.

### Treatments
Conventional management depends on the cause: antiviral medications for viral hepatitis, immunosuppressants for autoimmune hepatitis, and lifestyle modifications-weight loss, dietary changes, and alcohol cessation-for fatty liver disease. Regular monitoring of liver function is essential to catch progression early.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM understands chronic hepatitis primarily through the Liver's role in ensuring the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When emotional stress, dietary indiscretions, or external pathogens disrupt this flow, Qi becomes stuck. The most immediate sign is a distending pain or discomfort in the rib area, where the Liver channel runs.
But the Liver doesn't work alone: it relies on the Spleen to transform food into energy and the Kidneys to nourish its deeper reserves, so when one system falters, others are pulled into the imbalance.
The first and most common pattern involves the Spleen. When Liver Qi stagnates, it often 'invades' the Spleen, weakening digestion and causing bloating, fatigue, and loose stools. This pattern, Obstruction of the Spleen by Dampness with Liver Qi Stagnation, is like a traffic jam that spills over into the digestive system-the body's ability to process both food and emotions is compromised.
If dietary factors like alcohol or greasy foods add Heat and Dampness, the condition shifts to a more inflammatory phase: Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat. This pattern produces the classic signs of an acute flare-up-bitter taste, jaundice, dark urine, and a thick yellow tongue coating. The inflammation is more intense, but still rooted in the same stuck Qi.
As the condition persists, the stagnation deepens from the level of Qi to the level of Blood. Liver Blood Stagnation causes a fixed, stabbing pain and a dark, purplish tongue. This is a more advanced stage where the liver tissue itself may be starting to change.
Finally, after years of low-grade inflammation, the body's cooling and nourishing fluids-the Yin of the Liver and Kidneys-become depleted. In Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency, the pain is dull and persistent, accompanied by night sweats, dizziness, and a dry, red tongue. This pattern reflects a deep constitutional drain that requires long-term rebuilding.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
To work out which pattern is causing your chronic hepatitis (肝著, gān zhuó), a TCM practitioner asks about the nature of your pain and checks your tongue and pulse. A dull, distending ache in the rib area, along with bloating, fatigue, and loose stools, points to Obstruction of the Spleen by Dampness with Liver Qi Stagnation. The tongue is often pale with tooth marks and a thin white coat, and the pulse feels thin and wiry, revealing a stuck Liver and a waterlogged Spleen.
If instead the discomfort is accompanied by a bitter taste, yellow eyes or skin, dark urine, and a sense of heat, the pattern is Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat. The tongue coating appears thick, greasy, and yellow, and the pulse is rapid and slippery. This tells the practitioner that heat and dampness have brewed in the liver and gallbladder, often during a flare-up of inflammation.
When the pain becomes sharp, stabbing, and fixed in one spot, and the complexion turns dark, the diagnosis shifts to Liver Blood Stagnation. The tongue may be dark purple with stasis spots, and the pulse is choppy or wiry. This pattern develops after years of Qi stagnation and signals that blood flow in the liver has become obstructed, a sign of progressing fibrosis.
In more advanced cases, dizziness, night sweats, a dry mouth, and a sore lower back suggest Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency. The tongue looks red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. Here the illness has used up the body’s yin fluids, leaving a deeper weakness that requires gentle nourishment rather than draining treatments.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Obstruction Of the Spleen By Dampness with Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/obstruction-of-the-spleen-by-dampness-with-liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Abdominal bloating and fullness after eating, Sticky or incomplete bowel movements, Emotional frustration or low mood, Heaviness of the body and limbs, Poor appetite and fatigue, Distending discomfort along the ribs
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Greasy, fried, or fatty foods, Cold or raw foods, Overeating, Damp, humid weather
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked foods or meals, Gentle movement (walking, tai chi, etc.), Emotional calm and stress reduction, Dry, airy environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress or frustration blocks the Liver's ability to keep Qi moving smoothly, the stuck Qi presses into the rib area, causing the characteristic distending pain. This stagnant Liver Qi then invades the Spleen, weakening its power to transform food and fluids. The result is a buildup of Dampness that creates bloating, heaviness, and sticky bowel movements - the key digestive symptoms of chronic hepatitis.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating that is thicker at the center and root, and an excessively moist surface. Pulse: slippery and wiry overall; the right Guan position (Spleen/Stomach) is soggy or soft, the left Guan position (Liver) is wiry.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: <p>Emotional stress directly stagnates Liver Qi, which then invades the Spleen, so calming the mind eases the entire pattern. Greasy, cold, and raw foods overwhelm the already weakened Spleen and feed Dampness, while warm, cooked meals support Spleen Yang and are easier to transform. Gentle movement circulates Qi and dries Dampness, and a dry environment helps the body shed excess moisture rather than absorbing more from the outside.</p>
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals like soups and stews; avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that create more Dampness. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi helps move Liver Qi and dry out Dampness. Create space to unwind emotionally - journaling or talking with a friend can be as therapeutic as an herb.

### Liver and Gallbladder Damp-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-and-gallbladder-damp-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending or painful sensation under the right ribs, Bitter taste in the mouth, Thick yellow greasy tongue coating, Dark yellow urine, Yellowing of the eyes or skin (jaundice), Nausea and aversion to greasy food, Irritability and restlessness
- **Worse with**: Alcohol, Greasy, fried, or fatty foods, Spicy foods, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Hot, humid weather
- **Better with**: Abstaining from alcohol, Cooling herbal teas, Rest in a calm environment, Light, non-greasy meals, Gentle movement (walking, tai chi, etc.)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Dampness and Heat can lodge in the Liver and Gallbladder when the body struggles to process heavy, greasy foods or when emotional stress disturbs the Liver's smooth flow. The trapped Damp-Heat blocks Qi movement, causing the distending pain under the right ribs. Heat rises and steams upward, creating a bitter taste and irritability, while the Damp-Heat overflows into the blood and fluids, staining the skin and eyes yellow and turning urine dark.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, especially red at the sides, with a thick yellow greasy coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), slippery (滑 huá), and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness and Heat accumulate when the body is overloaded with heavy, greasy, or spicy foods and alcohol, which are difficult to digest and create internal Damp-Heat. Emotional stress stirs Liver Qi, generating Heat. Cooling, light foods and hydration help the body drain Dampness and clear Heat. Rest allows the Liver to recover, while gentle movement keeps Qi flowing without overheating the body.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid alcohol, greasy, fried, and spicy foods, which add Dampness and Heat. Choose light, cooling foods like mung beans, bitter melon, cucumber, and watermelon. Drink plenty of water and herbal teas like dandelion or chrysanthemum. Manage stress with gentle exercise like walking or tai chi, as emotional upset can worsen Liver Qi stagnation.

### Liver Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing pain below the ribs, Pain worse with pressure, Palpable lumps or masses in the abdomen, Dark purple tongue with stasis spots, Dark menstrual blood with clots, Dark or dusky complexion, Dry, rough, or scaly skin
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Alcohol and greasy foods, Cold or raw foods, Prolonged inactivity, Cold environments
- **Better with**: Gentle movement (walking, tai chi, etc.), Warm compress on the ribs, Emotional calm and stress reduction, Warm, cooked foods or meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Liver’s Qi remains stuck for too long, the blood it is supposed to move begins to congeal - like a river that slows and forms ice. In chronic hepatitis, this progression from Qi stagnation to Blood stasis is common, and it produces a sharp, fixed pain in the rib area that feels worse with pressure.
Because the Liver stores the Blood and governs the smooth flow of Qi, stasis here can also show up as a dark complexion, purple lips, and in women, dark menstrual blood with clots. As the stasis deepens, palpable lumps or an enlarged liver may develop, reflecting the physical accumulation of stagnant blood.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with purple stasis spots, especially on the sides; distended, dark sublingual veins. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and choppy (涩 sè), like a knife scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and anger surge Liver Qi, which then stagnates and presses on the blood, making stasis worse. Cold contracts and congeals, so cold foods and environments thicken the blood and intensify the fixed pain. Warmth, gentle movement, and emotional ease do the opposite - they relax the Liver, encourage smooth Qi flow, and help break up stagnant blood, which relieves the stabbing sensation and gradually softens any masses.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold and raw foods that can congeal the blood. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps keep Qi and Blood circulating. Manage stress and frustration, as emotional tension directly tightens the Liver and worsens stasis.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull aching pain along the ribs, Night sweats and heat in the palms, soles, and chest, Dizziness and ringing in the ears, Dry eyes with blurred vision, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Overwork and late nights, Spicy, fried, or drying foods, Alcohol and coffee, Emotional stress, frustration, or anger, Hot, dry weather
- **Better with**: Cool, quiet environment, Moistening foods (pears, congee), Adequate sleep, Gentle movement (walking, tai chi, etc.)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Chronic hepatitis is a lingering inflammatory process that gradually consumes the body's Yin fluids, particularly those of the Liver and Kidneys. The Liver stores Blood and the Kidneys store Essence; when these deep reserves are depleted, the Liver loses its nourishment, leading to persistent low-grade inflammation and a dull, aching pain in the rib area. The lack of Yin also allows deficient heat to rise, causing night sweats, a dry mouth, and a red, peeled tongue that are characteristic of this advanced stage.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin), rapid, and wiry; the left Chi (Kidney) position is weak, and the left Guan (Liver) position is thin and slightly wiry.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency is a state of deep fluid depletion with empty heat. Rest and cooling, moistening foods help replenish Yin and settle the heat, while overwork, spicy foods, and emotional stress further consume Yin and stir up internal heat, worsening the symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat moistening, cooling foods like pears, black sesame seeds, goji berries, and congee. Avoid spicy, fried, and drying foods, as well as alcohol and coffee. Prioritize restful sleep and avoid overwork or late nights, which further deplete Yin.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM cure chronic hepatitis?
TCM does not claim to 'cure' chronic hepatitis in the way antiviral medications can eliminate a virus. Instead, it aims to restore balance so that symptoms resolve, liver function improves, and the body is better able to manage the condition. Many people find that with consistent treatment, their energy returns, digestive problems ease, and liver enzyme levels stabilize. The goal is long-term harmony, not a quick fix.

### Do I need to stop my antiviral medication if I start TCM?
Absolutely not. You should never stop or adjust prescribed antiviral or immunosuppressive medications without consulting your doctor. TCM is used alongside conventional treatment to support your overall health and may, over time, help reduce the need for certain medications-but only under medical supervision. Always inform both your TCM practitioner and your prescribing physician about all treatments you are receiving.

### How long until I feel better?
Many people notice improvements in energy and digestive comfort within the first month of herbal therapy and acupuncture. Changes in tongue coating and pulse often appear even sooner. However, deep-seated patterns like Blood Stagnation or Yin Deficiency can take several months to shift. Consistency is key-daily herbs and regular acupuncture sessions produce the best results.

### What dietary changes will I need to make?
You'll be advised to avoid alcohol completely, as it directly fuels Damp-Heat and damages the Liver. Greasy, fried, and very spicy foods should also be minimized. Instead, focus on warm, cooked meals like congee, steamed vegetables, and lean proteins. Your practitioner may give more specific advice based on your pattern-for example, if you have Spleen deficiency with loose stools, you'll want to avoid raw and cold foods.

### Are there any side effects from the herbs?
When prescribed by a qualified TCM practitioner, herbal formulas are generally safe and well-tolerated. Occasionally, mild digestive upset can occur as your body adjusts. Because some herbs have mild blood-thinning properties, it's important to tell your practitioner about all medications you take, especially anticoagulants. Never self-prescribe herbs for liver conditions-professional guidance is essential.

### Can acupuncture help with liver inflammation?
Acupuncture can help regulate the nervous system, reduce stress, and improve digestive function, which indirectly supports liver health. It is particularly useful for alleviating the rib-side discomfort, bloating, and emotional frustration that often accompany chronic hepatitis. While acupuncture alone may not directly reduce liver inflammation, it is a valuable part of a comprehensive TCM treatment plan that includes herbs and dietary changes.

### Is TCM safe for hepatitis B carriers?
Yes, TCM can be a safe and supportive therapy for hepatitis B carriers. In fact, many carriers use TCM to manage symptoms like fatigue and digestive issues. However, it is crucial to work with a practitioner who understands your condition and can select herbs that do not stress the liver. Regular monitoring of liver function remains important, and you should continue any conventional medical follow-up as advised by your doctor.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Active Ingredients and Action Mechanisms of Yi Guan Jian Decoction in Chronic Hepatitis B Patients with Liver Fibrosis** ([Link](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6745137))
   *Journal/Publication: Li M, et al. Active Ingredients and Action Mechanisms of Yi Guan Jian Decoction in Chronic Hepatitis B Patients with Liver Fibrosis. Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine. 2019; 2019: 6745137.*
   This study investigated the active components of Yi Guan Jian and their mechanisms in reducing liver fibrosis in chronic hepatitis B patients. The formula was found to exert hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects, partly by modulating TGF-β1/Smad signaling. It provides a scientific basis for the traditional use of Yi Guan Jian in chronic hepatitis with Liver and Kidney Yin Deficiency.

2. **Chinese Herbal Medicine for Chronic Hepatitis B: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang L, et al. Chinese Herbal Medicine for Chronic Hepatitis B: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Journal of Viral Hepatitis. 2018; 25(12): 1465-1477.*
   This meta-analysis evaluated randomized controlled trials of Chinese herbal medicine for chronic hepatitis B. Results showed that herbal medicine combined with conventional antivirals significantly improved HBeAg seroconversion rates and liver function compared to antivirals alone. The review highlighted the need for higher-quality trials but confirmed the potential of TCM as an adjunctive therapy.
