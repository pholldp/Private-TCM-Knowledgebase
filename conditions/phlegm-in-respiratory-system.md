## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In conventional medicine, phlegm (sputum) is a mixture of mucus, immune cells, and debris produced by the lining of the respiratory tract. It's a common symptom of conditions like acute bronchitis, pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and allergies. The body produces excess mucus in response to infection, irritation, or inflammation, aiming to trap and expel pathogens.
Diagnosis often involves sputum culture, chest X-ray, or pulmonary function tests. Treatment targets the underlying cause - antibiotics for bacterial infection, bronchodilators and steroids for asthma or COPD, and expectorants to thin mucus.

### Treatments
Standard treatments include expectorants like guaifenesin to loosen phlegm, mucolytics such as acetylcysteine, and cough suppressants for dry cough. If a bacterial infection is confirmed, antibiotics are prescribed. For chronic conditions, inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting bronchodilators are used to reduce airway inflammation and open the lungs. In severe cases, oxygen therapy or pulmonary rehabilitation may be recommended.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, phlegm is not just a waste product - it's a sign that the body's fluid metabolism has gone wrong. The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and drink into usable energy and moisture. When the Spleen is weak - often from poor diet, fatigue, or damp environments - it fails to manage fluids, and they accumulate as dampness. Over time, this dampness thickens into phlegm, which then rises and settles in the Lungs. That's why a classic TCM saying is: 'The Spleen produces phlegm, and the Lungs store it.'
But the Lungs themselves can also generate phlegm directly. When an external pathogen like a cold or flu invades, or when internal heat flares up, the Lung's delicate lining can be scorched or congested, turning its normal fluids into sticky phlegm. So phlegm in the respiratory system can arise from a weak Spleen below, a troubled Lung above, or both at once. The color, consistency, and accompanying sensations - heat, cold, dryness, bloating - reveal which pattern is at play.
This is why the same Western diagnosis (chronic bronchitis, for example) can look completely different in two patients: one might have thick yellow phlegm and feel hot, while the other has watery white phlegm and feels cold. TCM treats these as entirely different conditions, because the root imbalance is different. By identifying the precise pattern, treatment can target not just the phlegm itself, but the underlying dysfunction that keeps producing it.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
When phlegm is thick, yellow, and sticky, and the person feels hot, the picture points to Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. A cough that brings up copious yellow sputum, often after a respiratory infection, along with a rapid, slippery pulse and a red tongue with a greasy yellow coating, confirms this very common pattern. The practitioner will also ask about thirst and a sensation of heat in the chest.
If the phlegm is white, sticky, and relatively easy to cough up, and there is a heavy sensation in the chest, Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs is likely. This chronic pattern often comes with a greasy white tongue coating and a slippery pulse. The practitioner will note whether the person feels foggy-headed or has a poor appetite, because dampness often stems from weak digestion.
Cold-Phlegm produces white, watery or frothy sputum, and the person usually feels cold, with a pale tongue and a thin white coating. This pattern is more common in older adults or those with a constitution that runs cold. The practitioner will ask about cold hands and feet, and whether the cough worsens in cold weather, to distinguish it from damp-phlegm.
When the phlegm is thin, watery, and frothy, and the person is breathless, Phlegm-Fluids in the Lungs is suspected. This pattern reflects a deeper yang deficiency, so the pulse is often deep and weak, and there may be swelling in the ankles. The practitioner will look for signs of fluid retention and ask about fatigue and cold intolerance.
Scanty, sticky phlegm that is hard to bring up, along with a dry cough, suggests Dry-Phlegm in the Lungs. This pattern often appears in chronic conditions where yin has been damaged. The tongue is typically red with a dry coating, and the pulse may be thin and rapid. The practitioner will ask about night sweats and a dry throat.
When phlegm is accompanied by a strong sensation of chest oppression and wheezing, and the sputum is thick but not necessarily yellow, Phlegm clogging the Lungs with Qi Stagnation is the likely pattern. This is common in chronic obstructive lung diseases. The practitioner will note whether emotional stress worsens the breathing difficulty and check for a wiry pulse.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious thick yellow or green sputum, Fever or sensation of body heat, Rapid breathing and chest oppression, Restlessness and irritability, Thirst with desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Spicy or fried foods, Hot, stuffy environments, Emotional stress or anger, Smoking or air pollution
- **Better with**: Cool, well-ventilated room, Warm water or pear juice, Gentle deep breathing, Avoiding dairy and sugar
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When an external infection or internal imbalance generates heat in the lungs, that heat scorches the body fluids into thick, sticky phlegm. This phlegm then blocks the airways, trapping the heat inside. The result is a deep cough with copious yellow or green sputum, chest tightness, and a sensation of heat that can feel like a fever.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, often swollen at the front, with a thick yellow greasy coating and red prickles on the front third. Pulse: Slippery and rapid (滑数 huá shuò), like beads rolling under the fingers.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heat and phlegm are the core issues here. Spicy, fried, and greasy foods add more heat and dampness, making the phlegm thicker and the cough worse. Emotional stress stirs up Liver Fire, which can fan Lung Heat and increase restlessness. Cooling foods and a calm environment help clear heat, while gentle deep breathing encourages Lung Qi to descend and phlegm to move out. Avoiding dairy and sugar cuts off the raw materials that form sticky phlegm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid spicy, fried, and greasy foods, as well as dairy and sugar, which generate phlegm and heat. Eat cooling, lung-moistening foods like pears, radish, and water chestnuts. Drink warm water throughout the day to help thin mucus. Keep rooms well-ventilated and avoid smoke, strong perfumes, and air pollution.

### Damp-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious white or grey-white sticky phlegm that is easy to cough up, Heavy, stuffy sensation in the chest, Feeling of heaviness and fatigue in the body, Poor appetite and abdominal bloating, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating, Slippery pulse, like beads rolling under the fingers
- **Worse with**: Damp or cold weather, Dairy products and sugar, Greasy, fried, or heavy meals, Cold raw foods and drinks, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, dry environment, Gentle exercise like walking, Warm cooked foods and soups, Ginger tea, Rest and avoiding overwork
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming fluids in the body. When it becomes weak-often from poor diet, fatigue, or damp environments-it fails to manage moisture. This excess moisture turns into dampness, which then congeals into phlegm. In TCM, the Lungs are the 'receptacle of phlegm,' so this sticky phlegm rises and settles in the chest, obstructing the Lung's ability to descend and causing the hallmark cough with copious white phlegm and a heavy, tight sensation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks; thick, white, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), possibly soggy (濡 rú).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness and cold congeal body fluids into phlegm, so a warm, dry environment and warm foods help the Spleen transform moisture. Heavy, greasy foods and dairy directly create dampness, while cold foods weaken the Spleen's digestive fire. Gentle movement stimulates Qi flow, which helps the body process dampness, whereas sitting still allows dampness to accumulate. Ginger tea warms the middle burner and supports the Spleen's ability to transport and transform fluids.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Focus on warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Avoid dairy, sugar, cold drinks, and greasy or fried foods, which generate dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps move Qi and dry internal dampness. Sip warm ginger tea to support the Spleen's digestive fire.

### Cold-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/cold-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with copious white, watery or frothy phlegm, Feeling cold and chilly, cold hands and feet, No thirst, preference for warm drinks, Wheezing and difficulty lying flat, Chest tightness and stuffiness
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, Cold food and drinks, Dairy products, Raw vegetables, Damp environment
- **Better with**: Warm drinks, Steam inhalation, Moxibustion on chest, Warm, dry weather, Gentle exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body’s internal fire (Yang) is weak-often from aging or chronic illness-the Spleen cannot transform fluids properly, and cold congeals those fluids into phlegm. This cold-phlegm rises and settles in the Lungs, blocking the airways and trapping Qi. The result is a wet, rattling cough with abundant white phlegm and a deep chill that no amount of blankets seems to fix.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale, swollen body with a thick, white, greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: Slippery (滑 huá) and slow (迟 chí).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Cold and dampness are the root of this pattern, congealing fluids into phlegm and weakening the body’s warming Yang. Warmth-from food, drink, or moxibustion-helps thaw and transform that phlegm, while cold and damp inputs deepen the internal chill, making phlegm thicker and breathing harder.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews. Avoid cold, raw, and dairy products that generate phlegm. Add warming spices like ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper to meals. Keep the chest and back warm, and avoid damp environments. Gentle daily movement like walking helps keep Lung Qi flowing.

### Phlegm-Fluids in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-fluids-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Cough with large amounts of white or grey sticky phlegm, Chest stuffiness that improves after expectoration, Wheezing or gurgling sounds in the throat, Cough worse in the morning, Sensation of heaviness in the body, Loose stools and poor appetite
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods, Dairy products, Damp weather, Overeating, Prolonged sitting
- **Better with**: Warm drinks like ginger tea, Coughing up phlegm, Gentle walking, Warm compress on chest, Rest
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen and Kidneys lack the warmth to transform fluids, cold dampness accumulates and turns into a sticky phlegm that rises to the Lungs. This phlegm lodges in the airways, blocking the normal flow of Lung Qi and trapping fluid. The result is a wet, rattling cough with copious white or grey phlegm that fills the chest and makes breathing feel heavy.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, thick white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), strongest in the right front (Lung) position; the right middle (Spleen) position may be soggy (濡 rú) or slippery; if cold-fluid predominates, the pulse can be wiry (弦 xián).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern arises from weak Spleen and Kidney Yang, which fails to transform fluids. Cold, raw, and damp-producing foods further burden the Spleen, creating more phlegm-fluid that pools in the Lungs. Warm drinks and warmth on the chest help Yang Qi to move and thin the phlegm, while gentle walking encourages the Lungs to disperse fluids. Coughing up phlegm temporarily removes the obstruction, easing chest pressure.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods and avoid dairy, sugar, and cold drinks which generate dampness. Add ginger, cinnamon, and a little black pepper to meals. Keep the chest and upper back warm, and take a gentle walk daily to help the Lungs disperse fluids.

### Dry-Phlegm in the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/dry-phlegm-in-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Scanty sticky sputum very difficult to expectorate, Dry throat and mouth, Throat feels scratchy or raw, Hoarse voice, Cough that comes in fits or bouts
- **Worse with**: Dry air or wind, Spicy or fried foods, Smoking or secondhand smoke, Overuse of voice, Late nights and stress
- **Better with**: Warm fluids (herbal teas, soups), Humidifier in room, Moistening fruits like pear and persimmon, Rest and avoid talking, Gentle deep breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Lung Yin is depleted, the normal moistening fluids dry up, turning phlegm into a thick, gluey paste that clings to the airways. The lack of moisture makes the phlegm scanty and extremely difficult to cough up, leading to a dry, hacking cough and a scratchy throat.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red and slightly thin body with a white dry coating, may have cracks. Pulse: fine (Xi) and slippery (Hua).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dry-Phlegm arises when Lung Yin is insufficient and fluids are depleted, turning phlegm into a thick, sticky paste. Anything that further dries the body - dry air, spicy foods, smoking, or overexertion - worsens the condition by consuming the remaining moisture. Soothing, moistening foods and a humid environment help replenish Lung Yin, making the phlegm thinner and easier to expel. Rest and gentle breathing conserve Yin, allowing the Lungs to recover.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat moistening foods like pear, white fungus soup, lily bulb, and honey. Sip warm water or herbal teas throughout the day. Use a humidifier, especially in dry or heated rooms. Avoid smoking, spicy foods, fried foods, and alcohol, which all dry out the body.

### Phlegm clogging the Lungs with Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-clogging-the-lungs-with-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Copious white or whitish phlegm that is easy to expectorate, Chest stuffiness and a sensation of distension or fullness, Feeling of bloating in the upper abdomen, nausea, Sensation of heaviness in the chest and body, Wheezing or a gurgling sound in the throat, Fatigue and low energy
- **Worse with**: Stress and frustration, Heavy, greasy, or dairy-rich meals, Cold drinks and raw foods, Damp, cold environments, Lying down immediately after eating
- **Better with**: Warm, dry weather, Light, warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement like walking or stretching, Deep breathing exercises, Emotional calm and reduced stress
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak, it fails to transform food and fluids, allowing Dampness to collect and thicken into Phlegm. This Phlegm rises and settles in the Lungs, blocking the airways and causing a productive cough with white phlegm. On top of this, emotional stress or dietary irregularities can cause the body’s Qi to become stuck. The stagnant Qi then binds with the Phlegm in the chest, creating a sensation of tightness, distension, and wheezing that goes beyond simple phlegm congestion.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy body with teeth marks, and a thick white greasy coating that is excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) like beads rolling under a cloth, often with a wiry (弦 xián) quality, especially at the right middle position; the right front (Lung) position feels particularly slippery and full.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm is a Yin, Cold pathogen that worsens with Dampness and cold, so warm, dry conditions and cooked foods help dissolve it. Stress and emotional tension directly worsen Qi stagnation, which is the other half of this pattern - they cause the chest to tighten and the Phlegm to feel more stuck. Gentle movement and deep breathing physically move Qi in the chest, breaking the bond between stagnant Qi and Phlegm, while light, warm meals support the Spleen without adding more Dampness.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid dairy, cold drinks, and heavy, greasy foods that create Dampness. Eat warm, cooked meals with ginger, pepper, and a little onion to help move Qi and dry Phlegm. Gentle daily walking or deep breathing exercises can loosen chest tightness and keep Qi flowing.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help with phlegm in the lungs?
Yes. Acupuncture uses specific points to transform phlegm and open the airways. The point ST-40 (Fenglong) is renowned for clearing phlegm from anywhere in the body, especially the Lungs. Combined with points like LU-5 (Chize) to descend Lung Qi and SP-9 (Yinlingquan) to drain dampness, acupuncture can reduce phlegm production and make coughing more productive. Many patients notice easier breathing within a few sessions.

### How long does it take for Chinese herbs to clear phlegm?
Acute phlegm-heat from a fresh infection often clears within 1-2 weeks of herbal treatment. Chronic damp-phlegm or cold-phlegm patterns, where the Spleen has been weak for a long time, usually take 4-8 weeks to see significant reduction. Dry-phlegm, which requires nourishing Lung Yin, can take 2-3 months. Consistency with diet and acupuncture speeds progress.

### What foods should I avoid if I have a lot of phlegm?
Dairy products, sugar, and greasy fried foods are the biggest culprits - they create dampness and phlegm in the body. Cold and raw foods (like salads, iced drinks) weaken the Spleen's digestive fire, making it harder to transform fluids. Instead, favour warm cooked meals, soups, and ginger tea. If your phlegm is thick and yellow (heat pattern), also avoid alcohol and spicy foods. For dry-phlegm, avoid drying foods like crackers and instead eat moistening fruits like pear.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs alongside my inhaler or other medications?
Generally yes, but always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor. The herbs used for phlegm patterns rarely interact with bronchodilators or corticosteroids. However, some herbs like Huang Qin (Scutellaria) have mild anticoagulant effects, so if you're on blood thinners like warfarin, your practitioner may adjust the formula. Never stop prescribed medications abruptly - work with your doctor to taper if phlegm improves.

### Why do I keep getting phlegm even after antibiotics?
Antibiotics kill bacteria but don't strengthen the Spleen or clear dampness. In TCM, phlegm often persists because the underlying digestive weakness hasn't been addressed. You may have cleared the acute infection, but the body is still producing phlegm due to a sluggish Spleen and lingering dampness. TCM herbs and dietary changes target that root, helping break the cycle of recurring phlegm.

### What does the color of my phlegm mean in TCM?
Yellow or green thick phlegm usually signals heat, as in Phlegm-Heat in the Lungs. White, sticky, and easy-to-cough-up phlegm indicates dampness without heat (Damp-Phlegm). White, watery, or frothy phlegm with a feeling of cold points to Cold-Phlegm or Phlegm-Fluids. Scanty, gluey phlegm that's hard to bring up suggests Dry-Phlegm from Lung Yin deficiency. The color and consistency help your practitioner choose the right herb formula.

### Can children use TCM for phlegm and cough?
Yes, TCM is commonly used for children with respiratory phlegm. Herbal doses are adjusted by weight, and pediatric acupuncture uses very shallow needling or non-needle techniques like acupressure and pediatric tuina massage. Dietary changes - like eliminating dairy and sugar - are especially effective in children. Always see a licensed practitioner experienced in pediatric TCM.
