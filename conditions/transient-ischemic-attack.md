## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
A transient ischemic attack (TIA) is a temporary blockage of blood flow to part of the brain, causing sudden stroke-like symptoms that usually last less than an hour and resolve completely. Common symptoms include one-sided weakness, numbness, slurred speech, vision changes, or dizziness. Because the blockage is brief, no permanent brain damage occurs, but a TIA is a serious warning sign: about one in three people who have a TIA will eventually have a full stroke if risk factors are not addressed.
Diagnosis is based on symptom history, a neurological exam, and imaging such as CT or MRI to rule out a stroke. Doctors then look for underlying causes like high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, or atrial fibrillation, and may order carotid ultrasound or echocardiography.

### Treatments
Standard treatment after a TIA focuses on preventing a future stroke. This usually includes antiplatelet medications like aspirin or clopidogrel, or anticoagulants if atrial fibrillation is present. Blood pressure and cholesterol are aggressively managed with medication, and lifestyle changes-smoking cessation, a heart-healthy diet, regular exercise-are strongly recommended. If the carotid arteries are significantly narrowed, surgery (carotid endarterectomy) or stenting may be performed to improve blood flow.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM views a TIA as a “wind stroke precursor” (中风先兆, zhòng fēng xiān zhào)-a critical alarm that the body's internal Wind, Phlegm, Fire, or Deficiency is already disturbing the brain's channels. The Liver is central to this process because it governs the smooth flow of Qi and Blood. When Liver Yin is weak, Yang energy can rise unchecked like a flame without a wick, generating internal Wind that rushes upward and causes sudden dizziness, numbness, or slurred speech.
But the Liver rarely acts alone. A weak Spleen can fail to transform fluids, allowing Phlegm to accumulate. This sticky, turbid substance can then combine with internal Wind to block the brain's delicate collaterals, producing the heavy, numb sensations typical of a Phlegm pattern. If the Phlegm brews into Heat-often from a rich diet and constipation-it surges upward and clouds consciousness, leading to brief confusion or loss of speech.
Not all TIAs are driven by excess. When the body's Yin and Blood are deeply depleted, an “empty wind” stirs in the interior, like a draft in an unfurnished room. This deficiency pattern often follows years of overwork or chronic illness and manifests as fleeting weakness with dry mouth and night sweats. Similarly, when Qi is too weak to move Blood, stagnation forms tiny blockages in the brain's vessels, causing transient symptoms in someone who is profoundly fatigued and pale.
Because a TIA can spring from such different roots, TCM does not offer a one-size-fits-all prevention strategy. The practitioner uses the tongue, pulse, and the quality of the episode-hot and forceful versus sluggish and weak-to identify which pattern is at play, and then tailors the herbal formula and acupuncture points accordingly.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A practitioner begins by asking when the symptoms appear and how long they last, because a transient ischemic attack is by nature fleeting. The quality of the episode - whether it feels hot and forceful or sluggish and weak - gives the first clue. The tongue and pulse are then checked to confirm which internal imbalance is driving the brief loss of function.
If the person describes irritability, a throbbing headache, dizziness, and a red face during the event, the pattern likely involves Liver Wind stirred by rising Liver Yang. The tongue is red with a yellow coating, and the pulse feels wiry and rapid. This picture points to excess heat and upward-rushing energy that needs to be anchored and cooled.
When sudden numbness on one side, slurred speech, and a heavy sensation dominate, the practitioner suspects Wind-Phlegm obstructing the channels. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, often white, and the pulse is slippery. This tells the practitioner that phlegm turbidity has combined with internal wind to block the collaterals, and the priority is to transform phlegm and extinguish wind.
If the person has constipation, foul breath, and thicker yellow phlegm alongside the neurological signs, Phlegm-Heat is likely the culprit. The tongue is red with a greasy yellow coating, and the pulse is rapid and slippery. The heat in the middle burner is steaming phlegm upward, so clearing heat and unblocking the bowels becomes a key part of the strategy.
In contrast, a pattern of Empty-Wind stirring in the Interior arises from yin deficiency. The person may notice a fine tremor, dry mouth, hot palms and soles, and a thin body build. The tongue is red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. Here the root is a lack of cooling yin that fails to anchor yang, so nourishing yin and subduing wind is essential.
When fatigue, pale complexion, and limb weakness are the backdrop, Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation is considered. The tongue is pale and may show purple spots, and the pulse is weak and thready or hesitant. This pattern reflects sluggish circulation due to insufficient Qi, so the treatment must replenish Qi and gently move blood to prevent further episodes.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Wind agitating Internally due to Liver Yang Rising
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-wind-agitating-internally-due-to-liver-yang-rising)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Severe dizziness with a falling sensation, Tremor of hands or head, Throbbing headache with stiff neck, Irritability and easy anger, Flushed face and red eyes
- **Worse with**: Anger or frustration, Alcohol or coffee, Spicy, greasy foods, Hot weather or saunas, Sudden head movements
- **Better with**: Quiet, dark room, Cool compress on forehead, Chrysanthemum or celery tea, Gentle neck stretches, Deep breathing
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver keeps Qi flowing smoothly and stores Blood. When Liver Yin is deficient, it fails to anchor the Yang, which then rises upward like an untethered flame. This rising Yang generates internal Wind that rushes to the head and disrupts the brain’s channels, causing sudden dizziness, tremors, and fleeting neurological signs like numbness or slurred speech-a warning that a full stroke could follow if the Wind is not calmed.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, stiff or trembling, possibly deviated, with thin yellow or scanty coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), fine (细 xì), and possibly rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Anger and stress directly surge Liver Yang upward, which is the very mechanism of this pattern, so calming the mind is essential. Alcohol, coffee, and spicy foods add heat and stimulate Yang, making the internal wind worse. Cooling and yin-nourishing foods help anchor the Yang and extinguish wind, while quiet rest and gentle neck stretches allow Qi to settle downward. Avoiding sudden movements prevents triggering the wind that disrupts the brain’s channels.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid overwork. Eat cooling, yin-nourishing foods like celery, spinach, pears, and chrysanthemum tea. Strictly avoid alcohol, coffee, and spicy or greasy foods that add heat. Gentle activities like tai chi or walking can help, but avoid sudden head movements or heavy lifting.

### Wind-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sudden numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, Slurred or difficult speech, Severe dizziness or vertigo, sometimes with a heavy headache, Copious white, frothy sputum or a gurgling throat, Chest stuffiness, nausea, or epigastric bloating, Feeling of heaviness in the body or limbs
- **Worse with**: Heavy, greasy, or dairy-rich foods, Cold and raw foods, Exposure to wind and drafts, Overeating or irregular meals, Damp living environment
- **Better with**: Warm, light meals (congee, soups), Rest in a calm environment, Gentle movement to circulate Qi, Keeping the neck and head warm
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weak it fails to transform fluids, and those fluids congeal into phlegm-dampness. This sticky phlegm then combines with internal wind - often stirred up by the Liver - and the mixture rises to obstruct the brain’s channels (collaterals). The result is a brief, frightening interruption of speech, sensation, or balance that defines a TIA.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: slightly pale body, swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating. Pulse: wiry and slippery (弦滑 xián huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is the product of a weak Spleen overwhelmed by heavy, greasy, cold, or damp foods. These foods create more phlegm, while wind exposure agitates internal Wind, pushing phlegm upward into the head. Warm, light, cooked foods spare the Spleen and reduce phlegm production. Rest and staying warm calm the Liver and Spleen, while gentle movement helps the body process fluids instead of letting them stagnate into phlegm.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid dairy, fried foods, sugar, and cold raw meals - they create the dampness that thickens into phlegm. Favour warm, light, cooked foods like rice congee, ginger tea, and lightly steamed vegetables. Gentle daily movement such as walking helps keep Qi and fluids moving, and protecting the neck from wind and cold reduces the chance of wind-phlegm surging upward.

### Phlegm-Heat
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-heat)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Thick, yellow, sticky phlegm that is hard to expel, Foul breath and constipation, Feeling of fullness and oppression in the chest and stomach, Irritability and restlessness, Transient dizziness or slurred speech triggered by heavy meals or constipation
- **Worse with**: Heavy, greasy, or spicy foods, Overeating, Constipation, Alcohol, Emotional stress and frustration
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Regular bowel movements, Cooling foods like pear and radish, Gentle daily exercise
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When rich, greasy foods and irregular eating habits overload the digestive system, Phlegm and Heat accumulate in the Stomach and Intestines. This sticky, turbid mass can suddenly surge upward, clouding the Heart and brain orifices. The result is a brief blockage of consciousness and speech, causing the transient dizziness, slurred words, or limb numbness of a TIA. Below, the bowels become sluggish, producing constipation and foul breath, while thick yellow phlegm signals the Heat and Phlegm brewing inside.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, swollen body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and rapid (数 shuò).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-Heat arises when rich foods and irregular habits overload the Spleen and Stomach, creating a sticky, hot obstruction. Greasy, spicy meals and alcohol directly add more Heat and Phlegm, while constipation traps the turbidity inside, allowing it to surge upward. Light, cooling foods and regular bowel movements clear the obstruction at its source. Gentle movement and stress management keep Qi flowing smoothly, preventing the stagnation that feeds Phlegm and Heat.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, spicy, and rich foods that generate Phlegm and Heat. Choose light, cooling meals with pear, radish, bamboo shoots, and plenty of vegetables. Drink warm water throughout the day to keep phlegm loose, and establish a regular bowel habit. Gentle daily movement like walking helps Qi flow and prevents stagnation.

### Empty-Wind agitating in the Interior
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/empty-wind-agitating-in-the-interior)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Trembling or twitching of hands/feet, Deep fatigue and listlessness, Dry mouth and throat, Dizziness and blurred vision, Night sweats, Feeling of heat in palms and soles
- **Worse with**: Overwork and late nights, Spicy, fried, or drying foods, Emotional stress and anger, Excessive heat or sauna
- **Better with**: Adequate, regular sleep, Cooling, moist foods (pear, congee), Quiet, dark environment, Gentle, grounding movement
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body's Yin and Blood are deeply depleted, the Liver and Kidneys lose their ability to anchor and nourish. This deficiency creates an emptiness that allows internal Wind to stir, like a draft in an unfurnished room. That unanchored Wind can rush upward to disturb the clear orifices of the head, causing brief episodes of dizziness, blurred vision, or limb weakness that come and go-the hallmark of a transient ischemic attack.
Because the root is a deep Yin emptiness, these episodes are often accompanied by signs of heat from deficiency, such as a dry mouth, night sweats, and a feeling of heat in the palms and soles.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: crimson, thin, dry, cracked, with little or no coating (mirror-like), and may tremble. Pulse: fine and rapid, wiry at the left guan position, but weak when pressed deeply.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin and fluids are easily consumed by overwork, stress, and heat, which then allows the unanchored Wind to stir. Rest, sleep, and cooling, moist foods rebuild the Yin foundation, so the body can hold the Yang steady and prevent the fleeting upward surges of Wind that cause the transient symptoms.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize rest and avoid late nights, as sleep is when Yin is restored. Eat moistening, cooling foods like pear, black sesame, goji berries, and millet congee. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly drying foods that consume Yin. Gentle practices like Tai Chi or restorative yoga can help anchor the mind without overexertion.

### Qi Deficiency causing Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-deficiency-causing-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Persistent fatigue and lack of strength, Dull or dusky facial complexion, sometimes pale with a greyish tinge, Limb numbness, heaviness, or transient weakness, Shortness of breath on mild exertion, reluctance to speak, Fixed, stabbing pain (e.g., in the chest) that worsens with pressure, Dizziness or lightheadedness
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or prolonged standing, Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Emotional stress and worry, Inadequate sleep or irregular routine
- **Better with**: Gentle daily movement (walking, tai chi), Warm, nourishing meals (soups, congees), Adequate rest and sleep, Keeping warm, avoiding chills
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the body's vital energy (Qi) is weak, it cannot push blood through the vessels with enough force. Over time, this sluggish circulation allows blood to pool and stagnate, forming tiny blockages in the delicate network of brain collaterals. The result is a temporary loss of nourishment to a region of the brain, causing fleeting weakness, numbness, or speech difficulty that resolves as the stasis shifts.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale-dark or pale-purple body, possible stasis spots, teeth marks, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: deep (Chen), fine or weak (Xi/Ruo), and choppy (Se).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi deficiency means the body has a limited budget of vital energy. Overexertion spends it too fast, while rest and gentle movement help replenish and circulate it. Cold foods and chills further weaken the Spleen's ability to produce Qi, so warmth and cooked foods support digestion and energy production. Stress knots up Qi flow, compounding the stagnation, so calming the mind helps keep blood moving smoothly.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize gentle, consistent movement like daily walking or tai chi to stimulate Qi flow without depleting it. Eat warm, cooked foods such as congees with astragalus root, angelica, and a little ginger; avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods that dampen digestive fire. Protect yourself from cold drafts and get ample sleep to rebuild Qi reserves.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture and herbs really prevent another TIA or a full stroke?
Yes, many patients experience a reduction in TIA frequency and a lower overall stroke risk when TCM is used as part of a comprehensive prevention plan. By treating the specific pattern-such as subduing Liver Yang, transforming Phlegm, or nourishing Yin-acupuncture and herbs help restore the internal balance that made the brain vulnerable in the first place. However, TCM should complement, not replace, conventional medical management. Always continue your prescribed medications and lifestyle modifications.

### How quickly will I see results?
Excess patterns like Liver Yang Rising or Phlegm-Heat often respond within 4 to 8 weeks of weekly acupuncture and daily herbs, with episodes becoming noticeably less frequent. Deficiency patterns-such as Yin deficiency or Qi deficiency with Blood stasis-may take 3 to 6 months to rebuild the body's reserves and stabilize. Consistency is crucial; missing treatments can slow progress. Your practitioner will monitor your tongue and pulse to track improvement even before you feel a change.

### Is it safe to use TCM if I'm taking blood thinners?
Acupuncture is generally safe when performed by a licensed practitioner who knows you are on anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs-they will avoid deep needling in high-risk areas. Herbal formulas require more caution because some TCM herbs have mild blood-moving effects that could interact with your medication. Always inform your TCM practitioner of all medications, and discuss any herbal treatment with your prescribing doctor. Do not stop or adjust your blood thinner without medical guidance.

### What will a TCM practitioner look for in my tongue and pulse?
The tongue and pulse reveal the pattern behind your TIA. A red tongue with a yellow coating and a wiry, rapid pulse suggests Liver Yang Rising, while a swollen tongue with a greasy coating and a slippery pulse points to Phlegm. A pale, trembling tongue with a weak pulse indicates Qi and Blood deficiency. These findings, combined with your description of the episode, guide the choice of acupuncture points and herbal formula.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a key role in TCM prevention because certain foods can generate Phlegm or stir up Liver Wind. Across all patterns, it is wise to avoid greasy, fried, and overly sweet foods, as well as alcohol and spicy dishes. Instead, favor light, easily digestible meals-cooked vegetables, soups, congees, and moderate amounts of lean protein. Your practitioner may give more specific advice based on your pattern, such as adding cooling foods for Heat or warming foods for Deficiency.

### What if my TIA was caused by atrial fibrillation?
TCM can still help by addressing the underlying constitutional imbalances that make the heart rhythm unstable and the blood prone to stasis. Patterns like Yin deficiency with empty heat or Qi deficiency with blood stasis are commonly seen in atrial fibrillation. Herbal formulas and acupuncture can support heart function and improve circulation, but they must be carefully integrated with your anticoagulation therapy. Close coordination between your cardiologist and TCM practitioner is essential.
