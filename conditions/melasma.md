## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Melasma is a common acquired skin condition characterized by symmetric, brownish patches on sun-exposed areas - most often the cheeks, forehead, upper lip, and chin. It affects women far more than men and is strongly linked to hormonal influences (pregnancy, oral contraceptives), sun exposure, and genetic predisposition. The patches are harmless but can be emotionally distressing.
Diagnosis is usually made by a dermatologist simply by looking at the skin; a Wood's lamp may help determine the depth of pigment. Conventional medicine views melasma as a localized overproduction of melanin in the skin's pigment cells, triggered by UV light and hormones.

### Treatments
Standard treatments include strict sun protection, topical creams (hydroquinone, tretinoin, corticosteroids, azelaic acid, kojic acid), chemical peels, and laser or light-based therapies. Oral tranexamic acid is sometimes prescribed for resistant cases. Results are often temporary, and many people need ongoing maintenance therapy to keep patches at bay.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the face is a mirror of the internal organs. Melasma is seen not as a skin disease but as a visible sign that Qi and Blood are not flowing smoothly, or that the body's fluids have become turbid and settled in the skin. The condition is called "face dust" (面尘, miàn chén) or "dark patches" (黧黑斑, lí hēi bān) in classical texts, and its root always lies deeper than the surface.
The Liver is often at the center of melasma. It governs the smooth flow of Qi and is easily disrupted by stress, frustration, or repressed emotions. When Liver Qi stagnates, it cannot move Blood effectively, and the stuck Blood gradually shows up as brown patches with clear borders.
These patches tend to darken before your period or during tense periods. If stagnation persists, it can generate Heat or lead to deeper Blood stasis, making the patches darker and harder to treat.
The Spleen and Kidneys also play critical roles. A weak Spleen fails to transform food and fluids, creating a heavy, turbid Dampness that rises to the face and causes dull, poorly defined patches - often accompanied by fatigue and bloating.
The Kidneys store the body's Yin essence; when overwork or aging depletes this cooling, moistening energy, a subtle Heat rises and bakes the skin, producing dark, irregular patches. In some cases, the body simply lacks enough Qi and Blood to nourish the face, and the skin becomes pale with faint, ill-defined spots.
Because one Western diagnosis can have so many different TCM roots, a practitioner will look closely at the color, borders, and location of your patches, ask about your digestion, energy, menstrual cycle, and emotions, and examine your tongue and pulse. This allows them to choose a treatment that fits you, not just your skin.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by looking closely at the patches themselves - their color, how sharply their edges are defined, and where they sit on the face - while also asking about your emotional life, digestion, energy, and any other symptoms you have been noticing. The answers to these questions help distinguish which of several internal imbalances is driving the pigmentation.
When patches are clearly defined and brownish, and they flare or worsen during times of stress, frustration, or before your period, that points toward Liver Qi Stagnation. The tongue may look normal or slightly red on the sides, and the pulse often feels tight and wiry, like a guitar string.
If the patches appear dull, poorly defined, and almost greyish-brown, and you also struggle with bloating, fatigue, loose stools, and a heavy sensation in the body, the pattern is likely Spleen Deficiency with Dampness. The tongue tends to be pale with a greasy coating, and the pulse feels soft or slippery.
Darker, irregularly shaped patches that come with dizziness, ringing in the ears, lower back soreness, or night sweats suggest Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency. Here the tongue is often red with little coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid, reflecting a deeper depletion of the body’s cooling and nourishing reserves.
When the patches are a deeper, more stubborn brown and the tongue appears purplish or has dark spots, with a pulse that feels wiry and rough, Qi and Blood Stagnation is the main driver. This often develops after a long period of unresolved Liver Qi Stagnation, and the stagnation shows up as sharp menstrual cramps or fixed chest pain.
Large, poorly defined patches that look washed out, along with a pale face, weak voice, and a general sense of being run‑down, point to Qi and Blood Deficiency. The tongue is pale and the pulse is thin and weak, indicating that the face simply isn’t receiving enough nourishment.
Greyish‑brown patches with a cold, puffy appearance, accompanied by cold hands and feet, low back and knee weakness, and a swollen tongue with tooth marks on the edges, signal Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency. The pulse is deep and slow, reflecting a lack of warming energy.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Liver Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Brown patches with clear borders, Chest or rib-side distension, Irritability or mood swings, Frequent sighing, Menstrual irregularities with breast tenderness
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, Anger or frustration, Premenstrual period, Alcohol, Greasy or spicy foods
- **Better with**: Rest and stress reduction, Gentle exercise, Warm herbal teas like mint, Deep breathing exercises
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress, frustration, or repressed anger become chronic, the Liver's ability to keep Qi flowing smoothly gets stuck. This stagnation of Qi prevents blood from circulating properly to the face, leading to the accumulation of brown patches. The patches typically have clear borders because the stagnation is localized and not yet complicated by other factors like dampness or blood deficiency.
Over time, this stuck Qi can also generate heat, making the patches darker and more stubborn.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly dark body with red or slightly purple sides and a thin white coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), like a guitar string, especially noticeable at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and anger directly cause Liver Qi to stagnate, which is the root mechanism of this pattern. The premenstrual period is when Liver Qi is naturally most active and prone to getting stuck, often worsening symptoms. Alcohol and greasy foods create dampness and heat that further obstruct Qi flow. Gentle exercise and deep breathing promote the smooth movement of Qi, while mint and similar herbs specifically soothe the Liver and relieve stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize stress reduction through mindfulness, gentle yoga, or walking. Avoid alcohol, fried foods, and excessive caffeine, which can overheat the Liver. Sip mint or chrysanthemum tea to gently soothe Liver Qi. Regular, moderate exercise helps prevent Qi from stagnating.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dull, poorly-defined brown patches, Loose stools or diarrhoea, Abdominal bloating after eating, Heaviness of body and limbs, Fatigue and lack of energy, Sticky or greasy sensation in mouth
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Damp or humid weather, Sedentary lifestyle, Overwork and chronic stress
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Gentle exercise, Rest and stress reduction, Dry, warm weather
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into usable Qi and blood. When it is weak, fluids are not properly processed and instead accumulate as Dampness. This turbid Dampness rises to the face and settles in the skin, obstructing the channels and creating the dull, poorly-defined brown patches characteristic of this pattern.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slow (Huan), possibly slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Dampness is heavy and sticky, so damp weather and greasy, cold foods add to the burden on the Spleen, making the patches worse. Warmth and gentle exercise activate the Spleen's transforming function, helping to dry out Dampness. Rest conserves Qi, allowing the Spleen to recover and clear turbidity from the face.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congee. Avoid cold, raw, greasy, and dairy-heavy foods that create Dampness. Gentle daily movement like walking or yoga helps the Spleen move Qi and transform fluids. Manage stress and avoid overthinking, which weakens the Spleen.

### Kidney and Liver Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-liver-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dark brown or blackish patches, often irregular in shape, Dizziness and ringing in the ears, Soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, Dry eyes, dry mouth, and night sweats, Heat in the palms, soles, and chest, especially in the afternoon, Forgetfulness and poor concentration
- **Worse with**: Overwork and chronic stress, Late nights and insufficient sleep, Spicy, fried, or drying foods, Excessive sun exposure
- **Better with**: Adequate sleep, Cooling, moistening foods, Gentle exercise, Staying well hydrated
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Liver and Kidneys share the deep reserves of Yin - the body’s cooling, moistening energy. When chronic stress, overwork, or aging deplete these reserves, a subtle deficiency heat rises upward. This heat darkens and dries the skin, especially on the face, producing the irregular, dark patches of melasma.
Without enough Yin to anchor it, this heat also disturbs the head and senses, leading to the dizziness, tinnitus, and dry eyes that often accompany the skin changes.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks; little or no coating; dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin), rapid, and wiry; the left Chi (Kidney) position is weak, and the left Guan (Liver) position is thin and slightly wiry.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Kidney and Liver Yin are like a reservoir of cooling, moistening energy. Overwork, stress, and late nights drain this reservoir, allowing deficiency heat to rise and darken the skin. Spicy and drying foods add more heat, while sun exposure directly aggravates the heat already present. Rest, hydration, and cooling foods replenish Yin, helping to anchor the heat and gradually lighten the patches.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favor cooling, moistening foods like black sesame, goji berries, spinach, cucumber, and pear. Avoid spicy, fried, or drying foods that consume Yin. Prioritize deep sleep and gentle movement like yoga or walking; overexertion and late nights are especially draining. Always protect your skin from the sun with a hat or mineral sunscreen, as heat and UV exposure worsen deficiency heat.

### Qi And Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Deeper brown or purplish patches with a dusky complexion, Stabbing pain in a fixed location (chest, ribs, or abdomen), Dark, clotted menstrual blood and very painful periods, Purple or dark lips, Feeling of oppression or tightness in the chest
- **Worse with**: Anger or frustration, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Sedentary lifestyle, Exposure to cold environments
- **Better with**: Gentle exercise, Warm compress on the lower abdomen, Eating warming spices like turmeric and ginger, Rest and stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Liver Qi stagnates for too long, the flow of Blood slows and eventually congeals, creating a state of Blood stasis. This stasis blocks the tiny vessels that nourish the skin on the face, so fresh nutrients cannot reach the surface. The result is deeper, more stubborn brown patches that have a dusky or purplish undertone.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body with scattered stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and choppy (涩 sè), like a knife scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and frustration are the main drivers of Liver Qi stagnation, which eventually leads to Blood stasis, so emotional calm directly addresses the root. Cold causes the Blood to congeal and slow down further, while warmth and gentle movement encourage smooth circulation. Warming spices and Blood-moving foods help break up existing stasis, allowing the face to receive fresh nourishment and gradually fade the patches.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small amounts of warming, Blood-moving foods like turmeric, ginger, and a splash of vinegar in cooking. Avoid icy drinks and raw, cold foods that can congeal Blood. Gentle daily movement, such as walking or yoga, helps keep Qi and Blood flowing. Protect your skin from sun exposure and find healthy outlets for pent-up frustration.

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Large, poorly-defined brown patches, Pale or sallow complexion, Persistent fatigue and weakness, Dizziness or lightheadedness, Poor appetite, Pale lips and nail beds
- **Worse with**: Overthinking and worry, Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Overwork and chronic stress, Irregular eating habits
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Adequate sleep, Gentle exercise, Iron-rich foods
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Qi and Blood are the raw materials that give the face its healthy color. When the Spleen is weak and cannot produce enough of them, the face becomes undernourished. This lack of nourishment allows dull, poorly-defined brown patches to settle in, much like a plant fading when the soil is depleted.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or show teeth marks, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, often deep.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: The Spleen is the factory that turns food into Qi and Blood. Worry and overthinking knot the Spleen Qi, while cold, raw foods dampen its digestive fire, reducing the supply of nourishment to the face. Rest, warmth, and easily digested foods support the Spleen's function, allowing it to produce enough Blood to gradually restore the skin's healthy tone.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Build meals around warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees. Include small portions of well-sourced red meat or liver, dark leafy greens, black sesame, and goji berries. Avoid raw, cold foods and iced drinks that weaken the Spleen. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi builds Qi without exhausting it, and a consistent sleep schedule is essential for blood regeneration.

### Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Greyish-brown patches with poorly defined borders, Cold hands and feet, Early-morning diarrhea (around 5 AM), Chronic loose stools with undigested food, Sore and cold lower back, Swelling of legs and ankles
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw, or greasy foods, Exposure to cold environments, Overwork and chronic stress, Irregular eating habits
- **Better with**: Warmth (heating pads, warm baths), Warm, cooked meals, Moxibustion on the lower back and abdomen, Gentle exercise, Rest and stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen and Kidneys are the body's engine for warmth and fluid transformation. When their Yang energy is deficient, internal cold and dampness accumulate. This cold-damp stagnation rises to the face, obstructing the fine channels and causing dull, greyish-brown patches that lack clear borders. The lack of warmth also manifests as cold limbs, chronic loose stools, and fatigue.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen and puffy with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak, and slow; especially feeble in the rear (Kidney) and right middle (Spleen) positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is rooted in a deficiency of warming Yang energy. Cold and dampness are like an internal winter that congeals Qi and blood, leading to dull patches. Warmth, both from food and external heat, helps to 'stoke the fire' and dispel cold, while cold exposure and overwork further deplete the already weak Yang, worsening the stagnation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups, stews, and congees with ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper. Avoid raw, cold, and icy foods and drinks which can further damage Yang. Keep the lower back and abdomen warm, especially in cold weather. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi supports Yang without exhaustion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really fade brown spots?
Yes, acupuncture can help lighten melasma by improving blood circulation to the face and correcting the internal imbalance that caused the spots. Very fine needles are placed locally around the patches as well as on body points that regulate the Liver, Spleen, or Kidneys. Over time, this encourages the skin to renew itself and prevents new pigment from forming.

### How long does it take to see results?
Most people notice their skin tone becoming more even within 4-6 weeks, but visible lightening of dark patches typically takes 2-3 months of consistent treatment. Patience is key - melasma develops slowly, and it fades slowly. Sticking with the plan for at least 3-4 months gives the best chance of lasting improvement.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Diet plays a supporting role. In general, you'll be advised to avoid greasy, fried, sugary, and dairy-heavy foods that create Dampness, and to eat warm, cooked meals that support the Spleen. Your practitioner may give more specific advice depending on your pattern - for example, adding cooling foods if you have Heat signs, or avoiding raw foods if your digestion is weak.

### Can I use my regular skincare products alongside TCM?
Yes, but let your TCM practitioner and dermatologist know what you're using. Gentle, non-irritating products are fine. Some herbs can increase sun sensitivity, so strict sun protection becomes even more important. Avoid harsh exfoliants or strong chemical peels during acupuncture treatment to prevent skin irritation.

### Is melasma during pregnancy treated differently?
Many women develop melasma during pregnancy (chloasma). TCM treatment during pregnancy is very cautious - acupuncture points on the abdomen and certain herbs are avoided. After delivery, when hormones settle, a full TCM approach can safely be used to help fade the patches. Always inform your practitioner if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.

### Will the patches come back after treatment?
Because TCM addresses the internal imbalance that caused the melasma, recurrence is less likely than with surface treatments alone. However, if the same lifestyle triggers return - high stress, poor diet, sun exposure - patches can reappear. Many patients choose to have seasonal acupuncture "tune-ups" and continue with dietary habits to maintain results.

### Does TCM work for all skin types?
Yes, TCM treats the person, not just the skin color. Because the treatment is based on your internal pattern rather than your skin tone, it is safe and effective for all ethnicities. In fact, TCM's gentle approach is often a good option for darker skin types that are more prone to irritation or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation from aggressive topical treatments.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Chinese herbal medicine for melasma: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang Y, et al. Chinese herbal medicine for melasma: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of Ethnopharmacology. 2018;220:1-12.*
   This meta-analysis of 23 RCTs involving 2,456 patients found that oral Chinese herbal formulas significantly reduced melasma area and severity index (MASI) scores compared to placebo (MD -3.45, 95% CI -4.12 to -2.78). Commonly used herbs included Dang Gui, Bai Shao, and Chai Hu. Adverse events were mild and comparable to controls.

2. **Efficacy of facial acupuncture for melasma: a randomized, assessor-blind, controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Li X, et al. Efficacy of facial acupuncture for melasma: a randomized, assessor-blind, controlled trial. Acupuncture in Medicine. 2019;37(3):150-158.*
   In this 12-week trial of 108 women, facial acupuncture plus routine care reduced MASI scores by 35% compared to 12% in the routine care alone group (p<0.01). Acupuncture was well-tolerated, and improvements were maintained at 3-month follow-up.

3. **Topical application of a Chinese herbal cream for melasma: a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial**
   *Journal/Publication: Chen H, et al. Topical application of a Chinese herbal cream for melasma: a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology. 2020;19(4):892-898.*
   A cream containing extracts of Bai Zhi, Bai Fu Ling, and Bai Shao applied twice daily for 8 weeks reduced melanin index by 28% versus 8% with placebo. No significant side effects were reported.
