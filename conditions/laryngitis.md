## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Laryngitis is inflammation of the larynx (voice box), typically causing hoarseness, a sore or scratchy throat, dry cough, and sometimes complete voice loss. It is most often triggered by a viral upper respiratory infection, but can also result from vocal strain, irritants like smoke or dry air, or bacterial infection. Diagnosis is usually based on symptoms and a physical exam; a doctor may use a laryngoscope to view the vocal cords if symptoms persist.
Conventional treatment focuses on rest, hydration, and avoiding irritants. Most acute cases resolve on their own within a week or two. Chronic laryngitis, lasting more than three weeks, may require investigation for underlying causes such as acid reflux, allergies, or vocal cord nodules.

### Treatments
Standard care includes voice rest, drinking plenty of fluids, using a humidifier, and avoiding smoking or other irritants. Over-the-counter pain relievers may be used for throat discomfort. Antibiotics are rarely prescribed unless a bacterial infection is confirmed. For severe inflammation, a short course of corticosteroids may be given. Chronic cases are managed by addressing the root cause, such as reflux medication or speech therapy.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
TCM views the throat as the "doorway" of the Lungs, and it is intimately connected to the Kidneys, Spleen, and Liver. Acute laryngitis is almost always an external invasion: Wind-Heat or Wind-Cold enters through the mouth and nose, lodging in the throat and obstructing the flow of Lung Qi. The throat becomes painful, swollen, or scratchy, and the voice grows hoarse because the Lungs cannot properly diffuse Qi upward.
Chronic laryngitis, on the other hand, reflects a deeper disharmony in the organ systems. When Kidney and Lung Yin are deficient, the throat loses its moisture and Empty Fire rises, causing persistent dryness and a tickling cough. If the Spleen is weak and fails to transform fluids, Dampness and Phlegm accumulate and rise to the throat, creating a sensation of a lump or constant mucus. Emotional stress can knot the Liver Qi, which in turn thickens fluids into a sticky Phlegm that lodges in the throat - the classic "plum pit" sensation that comes and goes with mood.
Because the same Western diagnosis can stem from a sudden external attack or a long-standing internal weakness, TCM practitioners ask different questions: Is the throat red and hot, or pale and cold? Does the discomfort worsen in the afternoon or with stress? The answers point to the specific pattern and guide treatment, which is why two people with laryngitis may receive completely different herbal formulas and acupuncture points.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking whether the throat problem came on suddenly or has been lingering for weeks. Acute laryngitis that appears within a day or two, often with a cold or flu, points toward an external invasion. Chronic hoarseness and a dry or lumpy sensation that comes and goes over months suggests an internal imbalance in the organs, which calls for a very different line of questioning.
When the throat looks red and swollen and feels burning hot, especially with fever and thirst, the pattern is likely Wind-Heat invading the Lungs. The tongue is red with a thin yellow coating and the pulse feels floating and rapid. If instead the throat is scratchy but not red, and chills are more prominent than fever, Wind-Cold invading the Lungs is the picture; here the tongue coating is thin and white, and the pulse is floating and tight.
Chronic dryness that worsens in the afternoon or evening, a tickling cough, night sweats, and a sensation of heat in the palms and soles point to Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire. The tongue appears red with little or no coating, and the pulse is thin and rapid. In contrast, a persistent lump-like feeling in the throat with lots of mucus, fatigue, and poor appetite suggests Spleen Deficiency with Dampness - the tongue is pale and swollen with a greasy coating, and the pulse feels slippery.
When the throat feels cold rather than hot, the voice is very low, and the limbs are chilly, the practitioner suspects Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency, with a pale tongue, white coating, and a deep slow pulse.
A sensation of a “plum pit” stuck in the throat that cannot be swallowed - often worse with worry or frustration - is the hallmark of Qi-Phlegm. The throat may look normal, but the pulse often feels wiry or slippery. This pattern is tightly linked to emotional stress and is common in chronic laryngitis that flares with mood changes.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Wind-Heat invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-heat-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Acute onset of sore throat, Red, swollen throat and tonsils, Fever with mild chills, Cough with thick yellow phlegm, Thirst and dry mouth
- **Worse with**: Spicy or fried food, Alcohol and smoking, Talking loudly or shouting, Exposure to wind and cold drafts
- **Better with**: Cool or warm liquids, Voice rest, Gargling salt water, Light, bland meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Wind-Heat enters through the mouth and nose, lodging directly in the throat. The Heat causes acute redness, swelling, and pain, while the Wind component disrupts the Lung's ability to diffuse and descend Qi, triggering cough and phlegm. The body's defensive Qi fights the invader at the surface, producing fever, mild chills, and thirst.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red tip, thin yellow coating, body slightly red. Pulse: floating and rapid.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Heat is an external invasion, so rest and avoiding irritants preserve the body's defensive Qi while it fights off the pathogen. Cooling fluids and bland foods help clear Heat and support Lung function, while spicy, greasy, or drying substances add more Heat and worsen throat inflammation. Wind exposure further drives the pathogen deeper, so staying sheltered aids recovery.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Drink plenty of warm fluids like mint or chrysanthemum tea. Avoid spicy, greasy, and fried foods that generate internal heat. Rest your voice and stay away from drafts, wind, and smoke. Gargling with warm salt water can soothe the throat.

### Wind-Cold invading the Lungs
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/wind-cold-invading-the-lungs)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Scratchy or itchy throat (not red or burning), Chills much more than fever, Clear, watery nasal discharge, Thin white watery phlegm, Hoarse or muffled voice, Aversion to cold, desire to bundle up
- **Worse with**: Cold or raw foods and drinks, Exposure to cold wind, Talking or straining the voice, Icy or air-conditioned environments
- **Better with**: Warm drinks like ginger tea, Keeping the neck wrapped, Rest and sleep, Steam inhalation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When a cold wind penetrates the body's surface, it can block the Lungs from their normal job of diffusing Qi and keeping the throat open. Cold has a constricting, tightening nature, so the throat feels scratchy and tight rather than hot or swollen. The voice becomes hoarse because Lung Qi cannot rise smoothly, and the nose runs clear as the body tries to flush out the invader.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly pale body with a thin white moist coating. Pulse: floating (fú) and tight (jǐn), easily felt at the surface.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind-Cold constricts and obstructs the flow of Lung Qi in the throat, causing a tight, scratchy sensation. Warmth relaxes that constriction and helps Qi move freely again, while cold and raw substances tighten it further. Rest and covering the neck support the body's defensive energy, so it can push the invader out.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Sip warm fluids throughout the day - fresh ginger tea with a little brown sugar is ideal. Cover your neck with a scarf and avoid icy or raw foods. Rest your voice as much as possible, and get extra sleep to help the body's surface defenses recover.

### Lung and Kidney Yin Deficiency with Empty Fire
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/lung-and-kidney-yin-deficiency-with-empty-fire)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Dry throat worse in the afternoon or evening, Tickling, dry cough with scanty phlegm, Hoarse voice, Night sweats, Heat in the palms and soles
- **Worse with**: Dry, heated indoor air, Spicy or fried foods, Late nights and overwork, Excessive talking or singing, Emotional stress
- **Better with**: Cool, moist air, Sipping warm water or herbal tea, Resting the voice, Eating pears or lily bulb soup, Early bedtime
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Lungs and Kidneys share responsibility for moistening the throat. When Yin is depleted, the throat loses its lubrication and becomes persistently dry and ticklish.
Empty Fire then rises from the Kidneys to flare upward, scorching the throat and making it feel parched, especially in the afternoon and evening when Yin is naturally weakest. This leaves the voice hoarse and the throat prone to a dry, hacking cough with little or no phlegm.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red, thin body with cracks, little or no coating, dry surface. Pulse: fine (thin) and rapid, weak at the Chi positions.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Yin deficiency leaves the body prone to dryness and heat. Cooling, moistening foods and a calm environment replenish fluids and help anchor Empty Fire, while dry air, spicy foods, and overuse of the voice further deplete Yin and aggravate the throat. Rest and early sleep allow the body to regenerate Yin, directly counteracting the root of this pattern.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat moistening foods like pears, lily bulb soup, honey, and congee. Avoid spicy, fried, and overly dry foods, as well as alcohol and coffee. Rest your voice when the throat feels dry, and aim for an early bedtime to conserve Yin. A humidifier in the bedroom can help counteract dry indoor air.

### Spleen Deficiency with Dampness
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-deficiency-with-dampness)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of a lump or something stuck in the throat, Excess thin, clear or white mucus in the throat, Fatigue and heaviness, especially after eating, Bloating and loose stools, Poor appetite, food tastes bland, Throat is not red or swollen, looks pale
- **Worse with**: Cold, raw foods and iced drinks, Dairy and greasy meals, Overeating or irregular meals, Damp, humid weather, Prolonged sitting or inactivity
- **Better with**: Warm drinks and soups, Small, regular meals, Gentle movement after eating, Dry, warm weather, Rest and lying down
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen's digestive and transporting function is weak, it fails to transform food and fluids properly. This leads to an accumulation of dampness and phlegm, which are heavy and turbid. Because the Spleen is supposed to send clear Yang upward to nourish the throat, instead dampness rises and stagnates there, creating a persistent sensation of a lump, excess mucus, and a tickling feeling. The root is a sluggish, waterlogged digestive system that cannot keep the throat clear and comfortable.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen body with teeth marks, white greasy coating, moist surface. Pulse: soggy (Ru) and slowed-down (Huan), may also feel slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: A weak Spleen is like a damp, smoldering fire that cannot properly transform food and fluids. Cold and raw foods extinguish that fire further, causing even more dampness to accumulate and rise to the throat. Warm, cooked foods and gentle movement support the Spleen's Yang, helping it dry dampness and send clear Qi upward, which clears the throat. Rest conserves the Qi needed for digestion, while damp weather overwhelms an already waterlogged system.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, and greasy foods, which are the hardest for a weak Spleen to process and directly create more dampness. Favor warm, cooked, and easily digested meals like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Gentle daily movement such as walking helps the Spleen move Qi and dry dampness, but don't exercise to the point of exhaustion.

### Qi-Phlegm
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-phlegm)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Sensation of a lump in the throat that cannot be swallowed or cleared, Symptoms worsen with emotional stress, frustration, or worry, Chest and upper abdominal oppression or stuffiness, Frequent sighing or desire to take deep breaths, Throat is not red or dry; appearance is often normal
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, frustration, or overthinking, Greasy, heavy, or dairy-rich meals, Sedentary lifestyle without movement, Cold drinks and raw foods
- **Better with**: Deep breathing and relaxation, Warm herbal teas (e.g., ginger, mint), Gentle neck and shoulder stretches, Expressing emotions or talking through worries
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When emotional stress, frustration, or prolonged worry knot the Liver’s flow of Qi, this stagnation disrupts the body’s ability to move and transform fluids. Those fluids gradually thicken into a sticky, invisible Phlegm that rises and lodges in the narrow passage of the throat. Because the throat is a junction where Qi and fluids converge, this Phlegm creates the classic sensation of a lump that cannot be swallowed or coughed up-a feeling that intensifies whenever emotional tension tightens the Qi further.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal pink-red body, possibly slightly swollen, with a white greasy or slippery coating. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and slippery (滑 huá).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress and overthinking are the primary drivers of Liver Qi stagnation, which directly knots the flow and creates the conditions for Phlegm to form. Greasy and cold foods overwhelm the Spleen and generate dampness, thickening the Phlegm that rises to the throat. Relaxation and gentle movement unblock Qi, while warm fluids and stretches help dissolve and move the Phlegm, easing the stuck sensation.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, dairy-rich, and very cold foods, which create dampness and thicken Phlegm. Favour warm, lightly cooked meals like soups and steamed vegetables. Gentle daily movement-walking, yoga, or Tai Chi-keeps Qi flowing. Make space to process emotions through journaling, talking, or breathing exercises, as holding onto stress directly feeds this pattern.

### Kidney and Spleen Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/kidney-and-spleen-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Pale, non-red throat, Cold sensation in the throat, Low, weak voice, Cold limbs and aversion to cold, Early-morning diarrhea or loose stools, Sore, cold lower back and knees
- **Worse with**: Cold weather or drafts, Cold, raw, or icy foods and drinks, Overwork and exhaustion, Excessive talking or straining voice, Stress that depletes Qi
- **Better with**: Warm drinks and soups, Wearing a scarf or warm neck wrap, Rest and conserving energy, Gentle exercise (walking), Moxibustion on lower abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Spleen and Kidneys are the body's source of warmth and transformation. When their Yang is deficient, the throat loses the warm nourishment of Qi and Blood, becoming pale and cold. Fluids are not properly transformed, leading to thin, clear mucus that pools in the throat. The voice becomes low and weak because Qi is too depleted to support it.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen and puffy with teeth marks, white slippery coating. Pulse: deep, weak, and slow, especially feeble at both rear (Kidney) positions and the right middle (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern is a deep coldness from lack of Yang Qi. Cold things-whether weather, food, or exhaustion-further drain the body’s warming fire, worsening the throat’s cold sensation and weakness. Warmth, rest, and moxibustion directly support and restore Yang Qi, helping the throat regain its warmth, voice strength, and ability to manage fluids.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked, easily digestible foods like soups, stews, and congees. Use warming spices such as ginger, cinnamon, and cloves. Avoid all cold, raw, and icy foods and drinks. Keep your throat and neck warm with a scarf, especially in cold weather. Rest your voice and conserve your energy. Gentle daily movement like walking can help generate Yang Qi, but avoid exhaustion.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can TCM treat acute laryngitis quickly?
Yes. When the correct formula is given at the first sign of symptoms - such as Yin Qiao San for Wind-Heat with a red, sore throat - many acute cases improve significantly within 24-48 hours. Acupuncture can also provide rapid relief by clearing heat and reducing inflammation. The key is matching the formula to the exact pattern.

### What's the difference between treating acute and chronic laryngitis in TCM?
Acute laryngitis is treated by expelling the external pathogen (Wind-Heat or Wind-Cold) and relieving throat symptoms. Treatment is short-term and focused. Chronic laryngitis requires correcting the underlying internal imbalance - such as nourishing Yin, strengthening the Spleen, or moving stagnant Qi - which takes longer and often involves ongoing herbal therapy and lifestyle adjustments.

### Is acupuncture safe for a sore throat?
Yes, acupuncture is very safe when performed by a licensed practitioner. Points on the hands, feet, and neck are used to reduce inflammation and pain. For acute heat patterns, a tiny drop of blood may be released from a fingertip point to quickly clear heat - this is minimally uncomfortable and highly effective.

### Can I use TCM alongside antibiotics or steroids?
Generally, yes. Herbal formulas can be taken alongside conventional medications, but always inform both your TCM practitioner and your doctor about everything you are taking. Some herbs that move blood or clear heat may interact with anticoagulants or long-term steroid use, so your practitioner will adjust the formula accordingly. Never stop prescribed medication without your doctor's guidance.

### How does TCM treat the lump-in-the-throat feeling?
This sensation, often called "plum pit Qi," is usually due to Qi-Phlegm stagnation. The classic formula Ban Xia Hou Pu Tang moves Liver Qi and dissolves the invisible Phlegm that lodges in the throat. Acupuncture points like Fenglong ST-40 and Taichong LR-3 help resolve the stagnation. Most people feel relief within a few weeks, especially when combined with stress management.

### Do I need to change my diet for laryngitis?
Diet plays a big role. Across all patterns, it's best to avoid cold, raw, spicy, greasy, and overly sweet foods, which can generate Dampness or irritate the throat. Warm, soft foods like congee and soups are ideal. If your throat is dry, pears and lily bulb are especially soothing. Your practitioner may give more specific advice based on your pattern.

### Will the herbs taste bad?
Some Chinese herbs have a strong, earthy, or bitter taste, but they are usually combined in a formula that balances flavors. Many patients get used to the taste quickly, and the benefit far outweighs the temporary inconvenience. Granules or capsules are available if the taste is truly intolerable.
