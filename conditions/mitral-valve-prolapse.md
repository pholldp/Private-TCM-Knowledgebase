## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Mitral valve prolapse (MVP) occurs when the leaflets of the mitral valve bulge backward into the left atrium as the heart contracts. It's one of the most common heart valve conditions, and for many people it causes no problems at all. When symptoms do appear, they can include palpitations, sharp or aching chest pain, fatigue, and sometimes shortness of breath. The diagnosis is usually made with an echocardiogram, which shows the valve's movement and any associated leakage.

### Treatments
Many people with MVP need no treatment beyond reassurance and periodic check-ups. If symptoms are bothersome, beta-blockers may be prescribed to slow the heart rate and reduce palpitations. In rare cases where the valve leaks significantly or causes rhythm problems, antiarrhythmic drugs or even surgery to repair the valve may be considered. Most management focuses on symptom relief rather than addressing the underlying tissue weakness.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the heart is the Emperor of the body - it houses the Shen (spirit) and governs the circulation of blood. The mitral valve's ability to close firmly depends on the strength of Heart Qi, the nourishing quality of Heart Blood, the warmth of Heart Yang, and the cooling moisture of Heart Yin. When any of these substances are deficient, the valve tissue can lose its tautness and sag backward, much like a sail that goes slack when the wind drops.
But deficiency is only half the story. Sometimes the problem isn't a lack of something, but an accumulation of something that shouldn't be there. Stagnant blood can block the vessels that feed the valve, leaving it undernourished and floppy. Or fluids can build up internally - what TCM calls "Water Qi intimidating the Heart" - and physically press on the heart structures, making the valve strain against extra pressure. Each of these root causes produces a different quality of palpitation, a different type of chest sensation, and a different set of accompanying signs that guide the TCM practitioner to the right pattern.
This is why the same Western diagnosis of MVP can look so different from person to person in TCM terms. One patient might feel a fluttering sensation with every skipped beat and be exhausted by the slightest activity (Heart Qi Deficiency). Another might have a stabbing pain that stays in one spot and a purplish tongue (Heart Blood Stagnation). A third might feel their heart pounding at night with night sweats and a feeling of heat in the palms (Heart Yin Deficiency). The valve may look the same on an echocardiogram, but the internal environment that led to its laxity is unique - and so must be the treatment.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner first listens carefully to what the palpitations or chest discomfort actually feel like and when they strike. The quality of the sensation, the time of day it appears, and what makes it better or worse are the earliest clues that steer the diagnosis toward one of several heart patterns.
If the dominant complaint is overwhelming fatigue and breathlessness that worsen with even gentle activity, and the face looks pale and dull, the pattern is likely Heart Qi Deficiency. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels weak and forceless, especially at the left distal position where the heart is reflected.
When poor sleep, dizziness, and a foggy mind accompany the palpitations, and the lips and nail beds are noticeably pale, Heart Blood Deficiency is the prime suspect. The tongue is also pale but may look thinner and drier, and the pulse is thin and choppy, signalling that the blood is insufficient to anchor the mind and nourish the valve tissue.
A stabbing, fixed chest pain that feels worse with pressure or emotional upset points firmly to Heart Blood Stagnation. The tongue takes on a purplish hue, often with dark spots on the sides, and the pulse becomes wiry, choppy, or irregular. This pattern indicates that blood is not moving freely through the vessels that support the mitral valve.
If the person always feels cold, especially in the hands and feet, and the palpitations are severe and accompanied by a heavy, sinking sensation, Heart Yang Deficiency is likely. The tongue is pale and puffy with a wet coat, and the pulse is deep, slow, and faint. The warming, activating force of the heart is too weak to close the valve firmly.
Night sweats, a dry mouth, a fluttering restlessness in the chest, and a tongue that is red with little or no coating suggest Heart Yin Deficiency. The pulse is thin and rapid. Here the cooling, moistening aspect of the heart is depleted, leaving the valve tissue undernourished and more prone to laxity.
When the chest feels heavy and tight as if filled with water, and there is a sensation of fullness or even mild edema, the pattern is Water Qi intimidating the Heart. The tongue coating is thick and greasy, and the pulse is slippery. This fluid overload physically burdens the heart and can worsen the prolapse mechanically.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Heart Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations worse with any exertion, Shortness of breath on mild activity, Spontaneous daytime sweating, Pale, tired-looking face, Weak voice, reluctance to talk, Fatigue and lack of stamina
- **Worse with**: Overexertion or heavy exercise, Emotional stress, worry, or anxiety, Cold weather, drafts, or damp cold, Heavy, greasy, or large meals, Standing for long periods
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Warm drinks and soups, Gentle movement (e.g., walking), Small frequent meals, Warm environment
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Qi (心气, xīn qì) is the vital force that keeps the heart muscle and valve tissue firm and properly closed. When Heart Qi becomes deficient, the mitral valve loses its tautness and can bulge backward into the atrium during contraction. This laxity leads to the sensation of palpitations and an inefficient pump, causing shortness of breath and deep fatigue even with mild effort.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, with a thin white coating; a midline crack toward the tip may appear in chronic cases. Pulse: Empty (Xu) - soft and large superficially but disappears under pressure, weakest at the left Cun (Heart) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Qi deficiency means the heart’s vital energy is low. Overexertion and stress further drain Qi, making the valve even less stable. Rest, warmth, and easy-to-digest foods conserve Qi and allow the heart to recover. Warm drinks and soups provide gentle nourishment without taxing digestion, while gentle movement prevents stagnation without depleting reserves.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews; avoid raw, cold, and greasy items that drain digestive Qi. Small, frequent meals are easier on the heart than large heavy ones. Gentle daily walking helps Qi move without depleting it, but stop before you feel breathless. Prioritize rest, especially after meals, and protect yourself from cold drafts.

### Heart Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations with insomnia and vivid dreaming, Poor memory and forgetfulness, Pale lips, nail beds, and dull complexion, Dizziness and general fatigue
- **Worse with**: Overwork and mental strain, Emotional stress, worry, or anxiety, Skipping meals or poor nutrition
- **Better with**: Adequate rest and sleep, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement (e.g., walking)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Blood is the material foundation that nourishes the heart muscle and the delicate tissues of the mitral valve. When Heart Blood is insufficient, these structures lose their tone and resilience, allowing the valve leaflets to sag or prolapse. The same deficiency fails to anchor the mind, giving rise to the palpitations, insomnia, and dizziness that often accompany this condition.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Pale body, possibly thin, with a thin white coating. Pulse: Fine (细 xì) and weak (弱 ruò), possibly choppy (涩 sè), especially weak at the left Cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Rest and sleep allow the body to conserve and build Blood, while nourishing foods directly supply the raw materials the Spleen needs to produce it. Overwork and mental strain consume Heart Blood, and worry further knots the Spleen Qi, impairing blood production-so both deplete the very substance that keeps the valve tissues toned and the mind settled.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat blood-nourishing foods like dark leafy greens, beets, black sesame, and moderate amounts of red meat. Avoid excessive mental work and screen time before bed to protect heart blood. Gentle exercises like tai chi or walking help circulation without depleting blood.

### Heart Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Stabbing or pricking chest pain in a fixed location, Purple or dark lips and nails, Palpitations with irregular or skipped beats, Cold hands, Anxiety or restlessness during pain episodes
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, drafts, or damp cold, Sedentary lifestyle, Emotional stress, worry, or anxiety, Heavy, greasy, or large meals, Overwork and mental strain
- **Better with**: Gentle movement (e.g., walking), Warmth (warm compress on chest), Hawthorn berry tea, Acupressure on Nei Guan
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Heart Blood stagnates, the vessels that nourish the mitral valve become blocked and the valve tissue loses its proper support. Over time, this lack of healthy blood flow weakens the valve, making it floppy and prone to prolapse. The stabbing chest pain and palpitations are direct signs of blood struggling to move smoothly through the heart.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: purple or dark-red body with stasis spots, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: choppy (涩 sè) or knotted (结 jié), especially at the left Cun position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Blood stasis worsens with cold because cold congeals and slows circulation. A sedentary lifestyle and emotional stress cause Qi stagnation, which in turn leads to blood stasis. Heavy, greasy meals create phlegm-dampness that can obstruct blood flow. Warmth, movement, and blood-moving foods invigorate the blood and help dissolve stasis, relieving chest pain and palpitations.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warming, blood-moving foods like hawthorn, turmeric, and garlic; avoid cold, raw foods and heavy greasy meals. Gentle daily movement like walking or tai chi helps keep blood flowing. Manage stress with breathing exercises and avoid overwork.

### Heart Yang Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yang-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations that worsen with exertion or cold, Cold hands and feet, feeling of cold, dislike of cold, Bright pale or white face, Chest stuffiness or mild chest pain that feels better with warmth, Fatigue, low energy, spontaneous sweating, Mental listlessness
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, drafts, or damp cold, Overexertion or heavy exercise, Raw, cold foods and iced drinks, Emotional stress, worry, or anxiety
- **Better with**: Warmth and rest, Warm drinks and soups, Moxibustion on lower back, Gentle movement (e.g., walking)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Yang is the fire that warms the body and drives blood through the vessels. When that fire is low, the heart muscle loses its propelling strength and the mitral valve may fail to close tightly. Cold congeals Qi and blood, leading to chest stuffiness and palpitations, while the limbs turn cold because Yang cannot reach the hands and feet.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Pale, puffy, tender tongue body with teeth marks and a white slippery coating. Pulse deep, weak, especially at the left Cun position; may be knotted (slow with irregular pauses).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yang is like a small fire that can be easily snuffed out by cold and exhaustion. Rest and warmth feed that fire, allowing the heart to pump with more strength and the valve to close more firmly. Cold, overwork, and stress drain Yang further, making the chest feel tight and the heartbeat irregular. By protecting and gently stoking the body's internal warmth, you address the root of this pattern.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Favor warming foods like ginger, cinnamon, lamb, and walnuts; avoid raw, cold, and icy items. Keep the chest and feet warm, and use moxibustion on the lower back or abdomen regularly. Gentle daily walks can gradually build Yang without overexertion.

### Heart Yin Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/heart-yin-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations worse at rest or at night, Night sweats, Mental restlessness, anxiety, Feeling of heat in palms, soles, and chest, Dry mouth and throat, Malar flush (redness over cheekbones), Insomnia with vivid, disturbing dreams
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress, worry, or anxiety, Late nights and overwork, Spicy, heating foods, Alcohol and coffee, Hot, dry weather
- **Better with**: Cooling foods and drinks, Adequate rest and sleep, Quiet, dark environment, Gentle movement (e.g., walking)
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Heart Yin is the cooling, nourishing substance that keeps the heart settled and its tissues supple. When Yin runs low, empty heat rises, making the heart restless and prone to palpitations. Over time, this lack of moisture and the constant flicker of heat can weaken the structural integrity of the mitral valve, contributing to its prolapse.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: Red body, especially at the tip, with little or no coating, possibly a midline crack, and a dry surface. Pulse: Fine and rapid, often empty on deep pressure, with the left cun position (Heart) weak at the deep level.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Heart Yin is like the cooling oil that keeps the heart's engine running smoothly. When it is depleted, empty heat flares up, causing restlessness and palpitations. Triggers that consume Yin further-like stress, late nights, and heating foods-worsen the condition, while cooling, moistening, and calming habits help replenish Yin and soothe the heart.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Keep your inner environment cool and moist. Avoid spicy, fried, or overly heating foods; instead enjoy pears, lily bulb, black sesame, and goji berries. Prioritize sleep before midnight, wind down with gentle stretching or meditation, and steer clear of overwork and excessive screen time.

### Water Qi intimidating the Heart
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/water-qi-intimidating-the-heart)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Palpitations that worsen when lying down, Sensation of something rushing upward from the stomach to the chest, Swelling of the lower legs or body, Cold hands and feet, Inability to lie flat due to breathlessness, Thin watery sputum or nausea
- **Worse with**: Cold weather, drafts, or damp cold, Lying flat, Raw, cold foods and iced drinks, Excessive fluids, Overexertion
- **Better with**: Warm environment, Sitting up or propped up, Warm, cooked meals, Gentle movement (e.g., walking), Avoiding fluids at night
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Kidney Yang is weak, it fails to transform fluids, and Spleen Qi cannot transport them. Water accumulates internally and rushes upward, overwhelming the Heart. This excess fluid pressure physically strains the heart structures, including the mitral valve, causing it to prolapse and leading to palpitations and chest oppression.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, swollen, tender body with teeth marks; white slippery water-slick coating. Pulse: wiry and slippery (xian hua), or deep and fine (chen xi).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: This pattern involves internal water retention due to Yang deficiency. Cold and dampness worsen the Yang deficiency, while warmth and dry conditions help Yang to transform fluids. Lying flat allows water to pool and press on the Heart, so sitting up relieves pressure. Warm cooked meals support Spleen Yang to transport fluids, whereas cold raw foods and excessive fluids add to the water burden.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid cold, raw, and damp-producing foods like dairy, greasy foods, and excessive fluids. Eat warm, cooked meals that support Spleen Yang, such as congee with ginger and cinnamon. Keep the chest warm and avoid lying flat immediately after eating.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help a structural problem like a prolapsed valve?
Acupuncture doesn't physically reshape the valve leaflets, but it can significantly improve the symptoms and the overall function of the heart by addressing the underlying TCM imbalances. By strengthening Heart Qi, nourishing Blood, or moving stagnation, acupuncture helps the heart work more efficiently and reduces the palpitations, chest discomfort, and anxiety that often accompany MVP. Many patients find that their symptoms become less frequent and less intense, even though the valve's anatomy remains unchanged.

### What does the tongue tell you about mitral valve prolapse?
The tongue is a map of the body's internal state, and in MVP it can reveal which pattern is at play. A pale, puffy tongue suggests Heart Qi or Yang Deficiency. A pale, thin tongue points to Heart Blood Deficiency. A red tongue with little coating indicates Heart Yin Deficiency. A purplish tongue with dark spots signals Blood Stagnation. Your TCM practitioner will combine the tongue picture with your pulse and symptom description to confirm the pattern that needs treatment.

### Is it safe to take Chinese herbs with my heart medications?
In most cases, yes, but you must tell both your cardiologist and your TCM practitioner exactly what you are taking. Some herbs that move blood, like Dan Shen and Chuan Xiong, can interact with anticoagulants such as warfarin. If you are on beta-blockers, herbs are generally safe but should be monitored. Never stop or adjust your conventional medications without your doctor's guidance. A qualified TCM practitioner will choose formulas that are compatible with your current treatment plan.

### How many acupuncture sessions will I need?
Most people start with weekly sessions for 4-8 weeks to see a noticeable change in palpitations and energy levels. After that, sessions may be spaced out to every other week or monthly as your pattern stabilizes. Deficiency patterns often require longer-term support, while excess patterns may resolve more quickly. Your practitioner will adjust the frequency based on how your body responds.

### Can TCM help with the anxiety that comes with MVP?
Absolutely. In TCM, the Heart houses the Shen (spirit), and when the Heart is unsettled by Qi deficiency, Blood deficiency, or heat, anxiety and restlessness naturally follow. Acupuncture points like Shenmen HT-7 and Neiguan PC-6 are specifically chosen to calm the mind, and herbal formulas often include herbs like Suan Zao Ren (sour jujube seed) to anchor the spirit. As the Heart pattern improves, the anxiety usually lifts alongside the physical symptoms.

### What foods should I avoid if I have mitral valve prolapse?
From a TCM perspective, cold, raw, and greasy foods are best minimized because they weaken the Spleen's ability to produce Blood, which the Heart needs. Caffeine and alcohol can stir up empty heat or aggravate palpitations. Instead, favor warm, cooked meals with gentle proteins and lightly cooked vegetables. Small, frequent meals prevent the sluggishness that can follow a heavy meal and strain the Heart. See the Diet & Lifestyle section for more detailed guidance.
