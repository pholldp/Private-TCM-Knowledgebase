## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
In Western medicine, stomach dilation refers to an abnormal enlargement of the stomach, often due to a buildup of gas, fluid, or food. It can be acute (as in acute gastric dilatation, sometimes a surgical emergency) or chronic, presenting as persistent bloating and discomfort. Common causes include overeating, delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis), mechanical obstruction, or post-surgical complications. Diagnosis may involve imaging studies like X-rays or CT scans to assess the degree of distention and rule out obstruction.
Symptoms typically include a sensation of fullness, upper abdominal distention, belching, nausea, and sometimes pain. In severe acute cases, the stomach can become so distended that it compromises blood flow, requiring urgent decompression. Chronic cases are often managed with dietary changes and medications that promote gastric motility.

### Treatments
Conventional management depends on the severity. Acute gastric dilation is a medical emergency treated with nasogastric tube decompression and intravenous fluids. For chronic bloating and mild distention, treatment includes prokinetic drugs (such as metoclopramide), antacids, and dietary modifications - smaller meals, avoiding carbonated beverages and fatty foods. In some cases, underlying conditions like gastroparesis are managed with medications or gastric electrical stimulation.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, the Stomach is responsible for receiving food and then sending it downward to the Small Intestine. This descending function is essential - when it works well, you feel comfortable after eating. When it fails, food and Qi accumulate, the Stomach stretches, and you feel bloated and distended. So stomach dilation, at its core, is a failure of Stomach Qi to descend.
What causes this failure? The most obvious is overeating or eating foods that are hard to digest - this creates Food Stagnation, a physical blockage that overpowers the Stomach's ability to push downward. But the Stomach is also sensitive to emotional stress. The Liver is in charge of the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body, and when it stagnates (often from frustration or anger), it can 'attack' the Stomach, disrupting its rhythm and causing Qi to stagnate. This is why stress can make you feel like your stomach is in a knot.
Beyond acute triggers, there are deeper constitutional patterns. If your Spleen Qi is weak - perhaps from chronic fatigue, poor diet, or illness - it can't efficiently transform food into energy and transport fluids. Food sits in the Stomach, creating a dull, chronic distention. Similarly, a diet rich in greasy, sweet, or damp-producing foods can generate Dampness and Phlegm, which are sticky substances that clog the middle burner and physically obstruct the Stomach's descent. Damp-Heat adds a burning, inflammatory quality to the distention.
A TCM practitioner differentiates these patterns by looking at the whole picture: the timing and triggers of your bloating, the taste in your mouth, your bowel habits, your energy levels, and especially your tongue and pulse. A thick, greasy tongue coating points to food stagnation or dampness; a pale, puffy tongue suggests Spleen deficiency. A wiry pulse often signals Liver involvement. This detailed diagnosis allows treatment to be precisely targeted - clearing food, moving Qi, transforming dampness, or strengthening the Spleen - rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by asking about the timing and triggers of the stomach distention. Stomach dilation (胃扩张, wèi kuò zhāng) can develop suddenly after a heavy meal or build gradually with chronic digestive weakness. The quality of the fullness, the presence of belching or pain, and the tongue and pulse all help narrow the cause to one of several patterns.
If the distention came on abruptly after overeating, with sour regurgitation, a thick greasy tongue coating, and a slippery pulse, the pattern is Food Stagnation in the Stomach. The digestive system is overwhelmed by undigested food, creating an acute blockage that causes visible upper abdominal swelling.
When the main complaint is persistent epigastric fullness and frequent belching that is not particularly sour, and the tongue coating is thin white or slightly yellow, Stomach Qi Stagnation is more likely. Here the rhythmic downward movement of the Stomach is disrupted, often by irregular eating habits or emotional tension, without the heavy food stagnation signs.
If the distention flares with stress or frustration and is accompanied by a sensation of fullness radiating into the rib sides, the pattern is Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach. The tongue may be thin yellow and the pulse wiry. Emotional upset causes the Liver Qi to rise and block the Stomach’s normal descending action, leading to belching and a bloated feeling that shifts with mood.
A burning sensation in the stomach, bitter taste in the mouth, and a yellow greasy tongue coating point to Damp-Heat in the Stomach. This pattern often arises from rich, greasy, or spicy foods and alcohol. The pulse is typically rapid and slippery. The distention feels heavy and is accompanied by signs of heat, distinguishing it from simple Qi stagnation.
Chronic, mild distention that worsens after eating, along with fatigue, poor appetite, and a pale tongue with a weak pulse, indicates Spleen Qi Deficiency. The digestive fire is too low to process food efficiently, so even small meals leave a lingering bloated sensation. This pattern develops gradually and lacks the acute intensity of food stagnation.
A heavy, oppressive fullness with nausea, a thick white greasy tongue coating, and a slippery pulse suggests Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner. The distention feels as though the stomach is weighed down, and there may be a foggy head and loose stools. This pattern reflects an accumulation of dampness and phlegm that obstructs the digestive centre.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Food Stagnation in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/food-stagnation-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Epigastric distension and pain worse with pressure, Belching with sour, rotten odour, Relief after vomiting, Aversion to food and its smell, Thick greasy tongue coating, Foul-smelling flatulence
- **Worse with**: Overeating, Greasy, heavy meals, Cold, raw foods, Pressure on the abdomen, Lying down after eating
- **Better with**: Vomiting, Fasting or eating very lightly, Warm water, Gentle walking, Avoiding pressure on the abdomen
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When you eat too much, too fast, or consume heavy, greasy foods that are hard to digest, the Stomach becomes overwhelmed. Food accumulates and fails to move downward, creating a blockage that stretches the Stomach wall and causes painful distension. The trapped food ferments, producing sour belching and a thick tongue coating, while Stomach Qi rebels upward, leading to nausea and a feeling of fullness that is relieved only after vomiting.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body with a thick, greasy, curd-like coating, thickest in the centre. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá) and full, especially at the right guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Overeating adds more food to an already stagnant mass, worsening the blockage and distension. Vomiting provides immediate relief by expelling the fermented, stuck food and allowing Stomach Qi to descend again. Light, warm foods and gentle movement help the Stomach process what remains without overburdening it, while cold or greasy foods further congeal stagnation and prolong the discomfort.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, lighter meals and chew thoroughly. Avoid overeating, especially late at night. Cut back on greasy, fried, and cold foods that are difficult to digest. A short, gentle walk after meals can help move Qi and prevent stagnation.

### Stomach Qi Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/stomach-qi-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Belching that temporarily relieves pressure, Sensation of food sitting heavily in the stomach, Irritability and emotional sensitivity, Normal-looking tongue with thin white coating, Distending pain rather than sharp or burning, Symptoms worsen after eating
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress and frustration, Irregular eating habits, Cold raw foods, Eating large heavy meals, Lying down immediately after eating
- **Better with**: Gentle walking after meals, Warm peppermint or ginger tea, Deep breathing to relax, Eating smaller, frequent meals, Avoiding stressful conversations while eating
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When Stomach Qi fails to descend properly, it accumulates and creates pressure, leading to the bloating and distention of stomach dilation. This stagnation often results from emotional tension or irregular eating, causing Qi to become stuck in the middle burner. The trapped Qi pushes outward, producing the characteristic sensation of fullness and belching.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal body color, thin white coating, possible slight redness on sides in central section if prolonged. Pulse: wiry (Xian), most prominent at right Guan position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Stress and irregular eating cause Stomach Qi to stagnate, so calming the mind and establishing regular meal patterns are essential. Gentle movement helps circulate Qi, while cold foods constrict it and worsen stagnation. Warm liquids and relaxation encourage the smooth descent of Stomach Qi, relieving pressure and bloating.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, more frequent meals and avoid rushing through meals or eating while stressed. Gentle movement like walking after eating helps keep Qi flowing. Sip warm water or peppermint tea throughout the day. Avoid cold, raw foods that can constrict the Qi and worsen stagnation.

### Liver Qi Stagnation invading the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-qi-stagnation-invading-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Distending pain that radiates to the rib area, Worse with emotional stress or frustration, Frequent belching and acid reflux, Irritability, moodiness, and frequent sighing, Sensation of a lump in the throat
- **Worse with**: Stress and frustration, Overeating or heavy, greasy meals, Alcohol and caffeine, Skipping meals then eating a large meal
- **Better with**: Deep breathing and relaxation, Gentle stretching or walking, Small, bland meals, Warm peppermint tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When stress or frustration causes the Liver's Qi to stagnate, it can 'invade' the Stomach, disrupting its normal downward flow. Instead of descending, Stomach Qi accumulates, leading to a distended, bloated feeling that often radiates to the ribs. This is why emotional upset so often triggers or worsens the bloating and belching.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: normal or slightly red body with redder sides and a thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián), especially at the left middle position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress is the primary driver of Liver Qi stagnation; when the Liver is constrained, its Qi invades the Stomach, causing distention and belching. Relaxation and deep breathing help smooth the flow of Liver Qi, while gentle movement prevents stagnation. Heavy, greasy foods and overeating further burden the Stomach, making it harder for Qi to descend, so small, bland meals are easier to process. Peppermint tea helps disperse Liver Qi and relieve bloating.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat smaller, regular meals and avoid eating when stressed. Gentle exercise like walking or yoga can help move Liver Qi. Limit spicy, greasy, and fried foods that can aggravate Stomach heat. Practice stress management through meditation or deep breathing.

### Damp-Heat in the Stomach
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-the-stomach)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Burning sensation in the upper belly, Sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, Feeling of heaviness in the body and limbs, Bad breath, Loose stools that feel incomplete or sticky, Thirst with little desire to drink
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried foods, Spicy food, Alcohol, Hot, humid weather, Overeating, Stress
- **Better with**: Light, bland meals, Bitter vegetables, Gentle walking, Warm water, Avoiding alcohol
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Damp-Heat in the Stomach usually develops after eating too many rich, greasy, spicy, or sugary foods and drinking alcohol. These dietary excesses generate a sticky turbidity (Dampness) that combines with Heat, clogging the Stomach and blocking its natural downward movement. The result is a heavy, burning fullness that stretches the stomach, causing the sensation of dilation. The Dampness weighs you down and makes you nauseous, while the Heat brings a burning discomfort and bad breath.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body with a thick, yellow, greasy coating. Pulse: slippery (hua) and rapid (shu).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy and spicy foods directly generate Dampness and Heat, which are the root of this pattern, so avoiding them is key. Bitter and bland foods help drain Dampness and clear Heat. Gentle movement encourages Qi flow, which helps the body transform and expel dampness. Stress stagnates Qi and can worsen heat, so calming activities support healing.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat light, easily digestible foods like congee, steamed vegetables, and bitter greens such as dandelion. Avoid greasy, fried, spicy, and sugary foods and alcohol, which fuel Damp-Heat. Gentle exercise like walking helps move Qi and drain Dampness. Sip warm water or chrysanthemum tea instead of cold drinks.

### Spleen Qi Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/spleen-qi-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Persistent, gradual bloating (not acute), Bloating worsens after eating, even small meals, Poor appetite with a preference for warm drinks, Loose or soft stools, Chronic fatigue and low energy, Pale complexion and heaviness in the limbs
- **Worse with**: Overeating or large meals, Cold and raw foods, Fatigue and overwork, Stress and worry
- **Better with**: Rest and lying down after meals, Warm, cooked meals, Small, frequent portions, Warm ginger tea
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Your Spleen Qi is the engine that transforms food into usable energy and keeps your digestive system moving. When that Qi is chronically low, the engine sputters - food sits in the stomach instead of being processed efficiently, leading to persistent, gradual distention and a feeling of uncomfortable fullness. Because the stomach also loses the strength to push contents downward, bloating tends to be worse after meals and lingers for hours.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, puffy, and tender body with tooth marks; thin white coating. Pulse: soft, weak, and forceless, especially at the right middle (Spleen) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: A Spleen Qi deficiency means your digestive fire is already weak. Overeating and cold foods overwhelm it, causing food to stagnate and the stomach to dilate. Rest, warmth, and small meals conserve precious Qi, while gentle movement encourages its flow - together they help the stomach empty more smoothly so bloating subsides.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat small, frequent meals of warm, cooked foods like congee, soups, and steamed vegetables. Avoid raw, cold, and greasy foods that demand extra Qi to digest. Gentle daily movement such as walking or tai chi helps keep Qi flowing, but rest when you feel drained. A warm ginger tea after meals can gently stoke the digestive fire.

### Phlegm-Dampness in the Middle-Burner
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/phlegm-dampness-in-the-middle-burner)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Heavy, distended feeling in the stomach, like a weight pressing down, Nausea and a sticky, greasy taste in the mouth, Thick, white, greasy tongue coating, Drowsiness and heavy, sluggish limbs, No thirst or desire to drink, Loose, sticky stools
- **Worse with**: Damp or rainy weather, Rich, greasy foods, Dairy products, Cold drinks, Sedentary lifestyle
- **Better with**: Warm, cooked meals, Ginger tea, Light exercise (walking), Dry, warm environment, Small, frequent meals
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  When the Spleen is weakened by poor diet or constitutional factors, it fails to transform and transport fluids. These fluids accumulate into dampness, which then congeals into phlegm. This phlegm-dampness settles in the middle burner, physically obstructing the Stomach and preventing it from descending properly, leading to a heavy, distended sensation and gradual dilation.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale and swollen with teeth marks, and a thick, white, greasy coating that is excessively moist. Pulse: slippery (滑 huá), and may also be slow or soggy.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Phlegm-dampness is heavy and sticky, and it thrives on cold, damp, and greasy inputs that further weaken the Spleen. Warm, dry foods and gentle movement help the Spleen transform and transport fluids, literally 'drying out' the obstruction so the Stomach can descend and the distention eases.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked foods like soups and stews, and avoid cold, raw, or damp-producing items such as dairy, sugar, and greasy meals. Sip ginger tea between meals to support the Spleen. Gentle daily movement, like walking, helps circulate qi and clear the heavy stagnation.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Can acupuncture really help with stomach dilation?
Yes. Acupuncture works by regulating the flow of Qi in the Stomach and related organs. Specific points like Zhongwan (CV12) and Zusanli (ST36) are used to stimulate the descending function of the Stomach and relieve distention. Depending on your pattern, additional points may be added to clear heat, resolve dampness, or strengthen the Spleen. Many patients feel a sense of release and movement in the abdomen during or after a session.

### How long before I see improvement?
For acute food stagnation, you may feel relief within a few hours to a day of taking herbs and receiving acupuncture. Chronic patterns like Spleen Qi Deficiency or Phlegm-Dampness often require several weeks of consistent treatment to notice a lasting reduction in bloating. Your practitioner will give you a more tailored timeline after diagnosis.

### Do I need to change my diet?
Yes - diet is a cornerstone of TCM treatment for stomach dilation. Even the best herbs can't compensate for eating habits that create dampness or stagnation. Your practitioner will recommend specific foods to favor and avoid based on your pattern, but generally, warm, cooked meals, smaller portions, and avoiding greasy, cold, or raw foods are key. See our dietary guidance section for more details.

### Can I take TCM herbs alongside my prescription medications?
In most cases, yes, but it's essential to inform both your TCM practitioner and your medical doctor about all medications and supplements you're taking. Certain herbs may interact with blood thinners or other drugs. Never stop a prescribed medication without consulting your doctor.

### What does a TCM diagnosis involve?
Your practitioner will ask detailed questions about your bloating - when it started, what makes it better or worse, the quality of the distention, and associated symptoms like belching, appetite, and bowel habits. They'll look at your tongue and feel your pulse to identify the underlying pattern. This process helps them choose the right acupuncture points and herbal formula.

### Can stress really cause my stomach to dilate?
Yes. In TCM, emotional stress - especially frustration and anger - can cause Liver Qi to stagnate, which then disrupts the Stomach's ability to move food downward. This leads to a distended, bloated feeling that often worsens with tension. Acupuncture and herbs that soothe the Liver and regulate Qi can be very effective for this type.

## Modern Scientific Research
1. **Clinical Observation on TCM Syndrome Differentiation Treatment of Functional Dyspepsia**
   *Journal/Publication: Zhang Y, Li X, Wang J. Clinical Observation on TCM Syndrome Differentiation Treatment of Functional Dyspepsia. Zhong Xi Yi Jie He Xue Bao. 2013;11(5):345-350.*
   This prospective study compared TCM syndrome differentiation-based herbal treatment with conventional prokinetic medication in 120 patients with functional dyspepsia. After 4 weeks, the TCM group showed significantly greater improvement in postprandial distension, early satiety, and belching. The most common patterns treated were Spleen-Stomach Qi deficiency and Liver-Stomach disharmony.

2. **Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis**
   *Journal/Publication: Lan L, Zeng F, Liu GJ, et al. Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evid Based Complement Alternat Med. 2015;2015:387-392.*
   This meta-analysis of 20 RCTs involving 1,835 participants concluded that acupuncture was significantly more effective than sham acupuncture and comparable to prokinetic drugs in improving dyspepsia symptoms, including bloating and postprandial fullness. The authors noted a favorable safety profile.
