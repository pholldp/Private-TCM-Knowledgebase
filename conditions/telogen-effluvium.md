## Conventional Medicine View
### Description
Telogen effluvium is a temporary form of hair loss where a large number of hair follicles are pushed into the resting (telogen) phase, leading to diffuse shedding across the scalp. It is typically triggered by a physical or emotional stressor - surgery, childbirth, rapid weight loss, high fever, or severe emotional shock - with shedding usually becoming noticeable 2-3 months after the event.
Diagnosis is made through a careful history and a hair pull test, and in most cases the condition is self-limiting. Once the underlying trigger is resolved, hair normally regrows within 6-12 months, though the timeline can be frustratingly slow for those affected.

### Treatments
Conventional management focuses on reassurance and addressing the underlying cause - correcting nutritional deficiencies, stabilizing thyroid function, or discontinuing offending medications. Topical minoxidil is sometimes recommended to encourage regrowth, and in persistent cases, low-level laser therapy may be considered. However, for most people, the standard advice is simply to wait for the hair to return on its own.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine View
### TCM Understanding
In TCM, hair is considered the "surplus of Blood." This means that healthy hair growth depends on an abundance of nourished Blood that can reach the scalp. When the body experiences a significant depletion - from childbirth, surgery, heavy bleeding, or a prolonged illness - its Qi and Blood reserves are suddenly drained, and the hair follicles are among the first to suffer. This is why telogen effluvium so often follows a major physical event: the body simply does not have enough resources to spare for hair.
The Liver plays a central role because it stores Blood and ensures its smooth flow to the head. Emotional stress, frustration, and anger can cause Liver Qi to stagnate, and over time this stagnation impedes Blood circulation, creating a state of Blood stasis. The scalp, sitting at the top of the body, is especially sensitive to any blockage in the upward flow. In this scenario, the problem is not a lack of Blood but a roadblock that prevents it from reaching the hair roots.
The Spleen and Stomach are equally important because they are the source of postnatal Qi and Blood. An overconsumption of rich, greasy, or sweet foods overloads these organs, generating internal Dampness and Heat. This Damp-Heat rises like steam to the scalp, clogging the hair follicles and damaging the roots - the hallmark of the oily, inflamed scalp that some people notice alongside their shedding. And when the body's defensive Qi is weakened after a major Blood loss, external Wind can invade the scalp, causing an itchy, shifting sensation and further destabilizing the hair.
Because of these different mechanisms, one Western diagnosis of telogen effluvium can correspond to several distinct TCM patterns - Qi and Blood Deficiency, Liver Blood Stagnation, Damp-Heat, or Blood Deficiency with Wind - each requiring a fundamentally different treatment strategy.

### Practitioner Diagnosis
A TCM practitioner begins by listening to your story - when the shedding started, what was happening in your life, and how your body felt overall. Telogen effluvium often follows a clear trigger, and the nature of that trigger is the first big clue that points toward one pattern over another.
If the hair loss began after childbirth, major surgery, or a long illness, and you feel deeply tired, pale, dizzy, or breathless, the pattern is likely Qi and Blood Deficiency. The tongue is pale with a thin white coat, and the pulse feels weak and thready. This picture tells the practitioner that the body’s raw materials are simply too depleted to nourish the hair.
When stress, frustration, or emotional upset dominates the picture, and the hair loss comes with tension headaches, rib-side distension, or irregular periods, the practitioner suspects Liver Blood Stagnation. The tongue often looks dusky or shows purple spots, and the pulse is wiry. Here the problem is not a lack of blood, but a blockage that prevents blood from reaching the scalp.
If the scalp is oily, the hair feels greasy soon after washing, and you notice skin breakouts, a heavy body, and a sticky taste in the mouth, Damp-Heat in the Stomach and Spleen is the likely pattern. The tongue is red with a thick, greasy yellow coat, and the pulse is slippery and rapid. The practitioner will ask about your diet, because rich, sweet, or fried foods usually lie at the root of this pattern.
A less common picture emerges when the hair is dry and brittle, the scalp itches, and you feel generally depleted after an illness. This is Blood Deficiency with External Wind. The tongue is pale with a thin coat, and the pulse may feel floating as well as thready. The practitioner checks for a history of catching colds easily or exposure to wind, because the weak blood has failed to anchor the scalp’s defenses.

## TCM Patterns and Treatment

### Qi and Blood Deficiency
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/qi-and-blood-deficiency)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Diffuse shedding that began 2-3 months after a major stressor (childbirth, surgery, illness), Hair feels dry, brittle, and limp, Pale complexion, lips, and nail beds, Persistent fatigue, even after rest, Dizziness or lightheadedness when standing up, Poor appetite and loose stools
- **Worse with**: Overwork and insufficient rest, Skipping meals or irregular eating, Cold, raw, or difficult-to-digest foods, Excessive worry and mental strain, Postpartum or post-surgical recovery without proper nourishment
- **Better with**: Adequate sleep and rest, Warm, nourishing foods (soups, congee), Gentle daily movement (walking, tai chi), Stress reduction and relaxation
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  In Traditional Chinese Medicine, hair is considered the 'surplus of Blood.' When the body experiences a major depletion-such as childbirth, surgery, or a severe illness-its Qi and Blood reserves are suddenly drained. Without enough Blood and Qi to reach the scalp, the hair follicles lose their nourishment and enter a resting phase.
A few months later, the undernourished hairs fall out diffusely, often in alarming amounts. The Spleen, which produces Qi and Blood from food, is typically weak, so even with a good diet, the body struggles to rebuild these resources, prolonging the shedding.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale body, may be thin or have teeth marks on the sides, thin white coating. Pulse: fine (thin) and weak, especially in the right Guan position (Spleen/Stomach).
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Qi and Blood are the body's fundamental resources for building and maintaining hair. Rest and sleep allow the body to conserve and regenerate these resources, while gentle movement encourages circulation without depleting them. Warm, cooked foods support the Spleen's ability to transform nutrients into Qi and Blood, whereas cold, raw foods dampen the digestive fire and hinder production. Overwork, stress, and skipping meals directly consume Qi and Blood, leaving none to nourish the hair follicles, which is why these triggers worsen shedding.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat warm, cooked meals that are easy to digest, such as congee with red dates and goji berries, bone broth, and lightly cooked leafy greens. Avoid raw, cold foods and excessive sugar, which weaken the Spleen. Prioritize rest and avoid overexertion; gentle activities like tai chi can help build Qi without depleting it. Ensure adequate sleep to support Blood regeneration.

### Liver Blood Stagnation
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/liver-blood-stagnation)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Fixed stabbing pain in the rib area, Dark menstrual blood with clots, Purple or dark lips and nails, Irritability and moodiness, Dry, rough, or scaly skin
- **Worse with**: Emotional stress and anger, Sedentary lifestyle, Cold raw foods, Alcohol, Suppressing emotions
- **Better with**: Stress reduction, Moderate exercise, Warm cooked meals, Gentle scalp massage, Emotional expression
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  The Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi and Blood. When emotional stress, frustration, or anger build up, Liver Qi stagnates. Over time, this stagnation impedes Blood circulation, creating a state of Blood stasis. The scalp is a high point that depends on a free flow of Blood; when stasis blocks nourishment, hair follicles weaken and shedding becomes diffuse, often accompanied by a sensation of tension in the chest or rib-side distension.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: dark purple body, stasis spots on the sides, distended sublingual veins, thin white coat. Pulse: wiry (弦 xián) and choppy (涩 sè), like a knife scraping bamboo.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Emotional stress is the primary driver of Liver Qi stagnation, which directly leads to Blood stasis. Calming the mind and moving the body helps unblock Qi flow. Cold and raw foods congeal Blood, worsening stasis, while warm cooked meals and gentle massage promote circulation to the scalp.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Prioritize stress management with activities like tai chi, yoga, or walking. Eat warm, cooked foods that gently move Blood, such as turmeric, onion, hawthorn, and dark leafy greens. Avoid excessive cold or raw foods and alcohol, which can congeal Blood. Keep the scalp warm and massage it gently to promote circulation.

### Damp-Heat in Stomach and Spleen
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/damp-heat-in-stomach-and-spleen)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Oily scalp and greasy hair, Sticky or bitter taste in the mouth, Abdominal bloating and loose, sticky stools, Heavy, foggy head and body
- **Worse with**: Greasy, fried, or sweet foods, Dairy and rich desserts, Hot, humid weather, Alcohol, Stress and overwork
- **Better with**: Light, simple meals, Cooling foods like mung bean soup, Regular scalp cleansing, Gentle exercise to move Qi
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  Overconsumption of rich, greasy, or sweet foods overloads the Spleen and Stomach, generating internal Dampness and Heat. This Damp-Heat rises upward like steam, clogging the hair follicles and damaging the roots. The result is excessive hair shedding, often accompanied by an oily, inflamed scalp and digestive discomfort.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: red body, possibly swollen with teeth marks, thick yellow greasy coating especially at centre and root. Pulse: soggy and rapid, often slippery.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Greasy, sweet, and heavy foods directly generate Dampness and Heat in the Spleen and Stomach, which then rises to the scalp. Avoiding them cuts off the source of the problem. Cooling, damp-draining foods and gentle movement help clear the excess Damp-Heat, allowing the scalp to heal.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Avoid greasy, fried, and sugary foods that create dampness. Eat more cooling, damp-draining foods like barley, mung beans, and bitter greens. Keep the scalp clean and dry, and avoid heavy oily hair products.

### Blood Deficiency with External Wind
*Pattern Link: [Detailed Pattern Page](/knowledge-base/patterns/blood-deficiency-with-external-wind)*

- **Clinical Signs & Symptoms**: Diffuse hair shedding with dry, brittle texture, Itchy scalp that comes and goes or shifts location, Aversion to wind and drafts, Pale complexion and dull, brittle nails, Dizziness or light-headedness, Scanty or pale menstrual blood in women
- **Worse with**: Exposure to wind and drafts, Overwork and chronic stress, Postpartum blood loss without replenishment, Heavy menstrual bleeding, Cold and raw foods, Poor sleep
- **Better with**: Warm, wind-free environment, Rest and adequate sleep, Blood-nourishing diet (red meat, black sesame), Gentle scalp massage with warm oil, Covering the head in wind, Stress reduction
- **Why this happens (Mechanism)**:
  After a major blood loss - from childbirth, surgery, heavy menses, or chronic illness - the body's Blood becomes too weak to reach and nourish the hair roots. The hair turns dry, brittle, and falls out easily. At the same time, the defensive Qi that normally guards the scalp becomes unstable, allowing an external Wind pathogen to invade. That Wind stirs up an itching sensation that comes and goes, further disturbing the follicles and aggravating diffuse shedding.
- **Tongue & Pulse**: Tongue: pale, thin body with a thin white and slightly dry coat. Pulse: thin (fine) overall, wiry at the left Guan (Liver) position, and floating at the right Cun (Lung) position.
- **Triggers & Relief Explanation**: Wind invades when defensive Qi is weak due to Blood Deficiency; wind exposure triggers itching and hair fall. Rest, warmth, and blood-building foods restore Blood, strengthening the anchor for hair. Stress and overwork deplete Blood further, while cold and raw foods impair Spleen function, reducing blood production.
- **Diet & Lifestyle**: Eat blood-nourishing foods like red meat, liver, spinach, black sesame, and goji berries. Avoid raw, cold, and windy environments; keep your head covered on windy days. Gentle exercise improves circulation but avoid overexertion that depletes blood.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### How long does it take for TCM to stop my hair from shedding?
Most people notice a reduction in daily hair fall within 4-8 weeks of starting a personalized herbal formula and regular acupuncture. The first sign is often fewer hairs in the shower drain or on your brush. However, complete stabilization and visible regrowth can take 3-6 months, especially if your body's reserves are deeply depleted.

### Can acupuncture really help hair grow back?
Yes, when used as part of a comprehensive pattern-based treatment. Acupuncture improves blood flow to the scalp, calms the nervous system, and helps regulate the underlying organ imbalances that triggered the shedding. Points like Baihui (DU-20) on the crown and Zusanli (ST-36) on the leg are commonly used to direct Qi and Blood upward and strengthen the body's overall energy. For best results, acupuncture is combined with Chinese herbs and dietary changes.

### Is Chinese herbal medicine safe to take for hair loss?
Yes, when prescribed by a qualified TCM practitioner who has taken your full health history. Chinese herbs are typically very safe, but some formulas contain Blood-moving herbs (like Dang Gui or Chuan Xiong) that can interact with anticoagulant medications. Always tell your practitioner about any medications you are taking, and inform your doctor that you are using herbal medicine.

### Can I use minoxidil while doing TCM treatment?
In most cases, yes. Minoxidil works locally on the scalp, while Chinese herbs and acupuncture work internally to correct the underlying imbalance. There are no known direct conflicts, but it's wise to inform both your dermatologist and your TCM practitioner so they can coordinate your care and avoid any overlapping irritants.

### What foods should I eat to help stop telogen effluvium?
Regardless of your pattern, focus on warm, cooked, nutrient-dense foods that are easy to digest. Bone broth, congee, dark leafy greens, eggs, and small amounts of high-quality red meat help build Blood. Black sesame seeds, walnuts, and goji berries are traditionally used to nourish the hair. Avoid cold, raw foods, excessive sugar, and greasy or fried meals, as these weaken the Spleen and generate Dampness.

### Will my hair grow back thinner or the same as before?
With telogen effluvium, the hair follicle itself is not permanently damaged, so regrowth should be the same texture and thickness as before - provided the underlying imbalance is corrected. TCM aims to restore the full nourishment needed for strong, healthy hair. If the shedding has been going on for many months or the body's reserves are severely depleted, regrowth may initially be finer, but it usually thickens over time with continued treatment.

### Does stress really cause hair loss in TCM?
Yes, and it's one of the most direct connections. Emotional stress - especially frustration and unexpressed anger - stagnates the Liver Qi, which in TCM is responsible for the smooth flow of Blood. Over time, this stagnation can turn into Blood stasis, blocking the upward flow to the scalp. This is why a period of intense stress is often followed by diffuse shedding 2-3 months later. Acupuncture and Liver-soothing herbs are particularly effective for this pattern.
